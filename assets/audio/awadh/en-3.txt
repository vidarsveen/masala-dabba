Kebabs, biryani and nihari.

A galouti kebab is handed over sitting on a piece of bread, and there is a reason for that. Pick one up between finger and thumb and it will fall apart before it reaches your mouth. The whole dish is an argument about texture, and the name says so, because galauti means melting.

The meat is goat or lamb, minced and then pounded further until no grain is left in it. Into that go browned onion, ghee, a long list of ground spices and, crucially, a little raw green papaya. The paste rests, and then it is patted into small discs and fried gently in ghee on a flat tawa. Nothing goes on a skewer and nothing sees a coal. Shops will tell you the spice mix runs to more than a hundred ingredients, which is a boast rather than a recipe.

Raw papaya is the mechanism. It contains papain, an enzyme that cuts proteins apart, and it keeps working on the meat for as long as the mixture is left standing. That is what turns minced goat into something with no fibre in it at all. It is also the trap, because the failure mode of a galouti is not toughness. Leave the papaya in too long and the mixture will not hold together, and a cook who has done that has a bowl of paste and nothing to fry.

A famous story says the kebab was invented for a toothless Nawab. Versions name different rulers, so it is best treated as culinary folklore. Tunday Kababi in Lucknow's Chowk traces its business to 1905 and helped make the style widely known.

Kakori is another kebab associated with Lucknow. It is a seekh kebab: seasoned mince is pressed along a skewer and cooked over charcoal. The mixture must remain tender while holding firmly enough not to fall into the fire.

The mixture is different too. Fat from the same animal goes in, along with ground poppy seed, khoya, browned onion and a far lighter hand with the papaya. The kebab takes its name from Kakori, a town a few kilometres outside Lucknow, and its origin story again involves a guest who complained about a coarse kebab. Again, it is a story.

Two more belong to the family. A shami kebab is meat boiled with chana dal and whole spices, ground, shaped into discs and often dipped in egg before frying, so it comes out denser and sourer than a galouti. A boti kebab is not minced at all but cubes of marinated meat, and it is the only one of the four you can eat with your fingers without consequences.

Many Awadhi kormas braise meat with yoghurt, browned onion and nuts, then finish with saffron or kewra. Some omit tomato and use little chilli, producing a pale gravy, while household and restaurant versions vary.

At a formal table, bread, rice, korma and kebabs are often composed to share an aromatic register. Texture and richness can provide as much contrast as chilli heat.

The name nihari is commonly traced to the Arabic nahar, or morning. The link reflects its long association with breakfast. Shank and marrow bones cook for hours with onion, ginger and spices. Gelatine and marrow give the broth body, sometimes reinforced with a little flour near the end.

Nihari is served with ginger, green chilli, coriander, lime and bread such as khameeri roti. In Lucknow and Delhi it retains a strong morning association, though restaurants now serve it at other times too.

Nihari belongs to Delhi and to Lucknow both, and each city will tell you it belongs there. One detail every shop shares is the taar: a ladleful from today's pot is held back and goes into tomorrow's, and a famous shop will say its pot has run unbroken for decades. Nobody can check that, and it gets repeated anyway, but the practice itself is real and it is what gives an old pot its depth.

Shahi tukda turns firm bread into a rich sweet. Slices are fried in ghee, dipped briefly in syrup and covered with slowly reduced milk. Pistachio, almond and optional edible silver finish it, and a short rest lets the layers settle.

Malai makhan is a winter sweet of Lucknow. Milk and cream are aerated into a fragile foam, flavoured with saffron or cardamom and served fresh with sugar and nuts. Contemporary food safety requires the dairy to remain chilled during preparation and sale.

This course also includes Varanasi, or Banaras, with Awadh. Its tamatar chaat cooks tomato with potato, ginger and spices until thick, then serves the mixture hot with lime, coriander and crisp fried shreds.
