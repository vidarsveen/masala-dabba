Kebabs, biryani and nihari.

A galouti kebab is handed over sitting on a piece of bread, and there is a reason for that. Pick one up between finger and thumb and it will fall apart before it reaches your mouth. The whole dish is an argument about texture, and the name says so, because galauti means melting.

Galouti, and the papaya.

The meat is goat or lamb, minced and then pounded further until no grain is left in it. Into that go browned onion, ghee, a long list of ground spices and, crucially, a little raw green papaya. The paste rests, and then it is patted into small discs and fried gently in ghee on a flat tawa. Nothing goes on a skewer and nothing sees a coal. Shops will tell you the spice mix runs to more than a hundred ingredients, which is a boast rather than a recipe.

Raw papaya is the mechanism. It contains papain, an enzyme that cuts proteins apart, and it keeps working on the meat for as long as the mixture is left standing. That is what turns minced goat into something with no fibre in it at all. It is also the trap, because the failure mode of a galouti is not toughness. Leave the papaya in too long and the mixture will not hold together, and a cook who has done that has a bowl of paste and nothing to fry.

The famous story says the kebab was invented for a Nawab who had lost his teeth. Some tellings name Asaf-ud-Daula and others name Wajid Ali Shah, which is usually a sign that a story has been improved along the way, so take it as a story that is told rather than as a fact. What is solid is where most people meet the thing. A shop in the Chowk in Lucknow opened in 1905 under a one-armed cook, tunday means one-armed, and the shop is still there selling the same kebab.

Kakori, which is a different animal.

Kakori is the other kebab Lucknow is known for, and confusing the two is the commonest mistake a visitor makes. A kakori is a seekh kebab. The mince is bound, smoothed by hand along a flat metal skewer and cooked over charcoal, so it has to be soft enough to deserve its reputation and firm enough not to drop into the fire. That balance is the skill, and it is the opposite problem from the galouti's.

The mixture is different too. Fat from the same animal goes in, along with ground poppy seed, khoya, browned onion and a far lighter hand with the papaya. The kebab takes its name from Kakori, a town a few kilometres outside Lucknow, and its origin story again involves a guest who complained about a coarse kebab. Again, it is a story.

Two more belong to the family. A shami kebab is meat boiled with chana dal and whole spices, ground, shaped into discs and often dipped in egg before frying, so it comes out denser and sourer than a galouti. A boti kebab is not minced at all but cubes of marinated meat, and it is the only one of the four you can eat with your fingers without consequences.

Korma, and what sits beside the rice.

The last reading covered the method behind the biryani, so here is what goes round it. Awadhi korma shows the restraint of this kitchen more clearly than anything else on the cloth. Meat is braised in yoghurt with browned onion and a paste of almond or cashew, and there is no tomato in it and no chilli powder. It is finished with cream, with saffron and with a few drops of kewra, and it comes out the colour of old ivory. A korma that arrives red was made somewhere else.

Nothing on an Awadhi table is a relish or a counterweight in the way a Hyderabadi salan is. The dishes are meant to agree with each other. Bread, rice, a korma and two or three kebabs all pull in the same direction, and the variation is in texture rather than in heat.

Nihari, and the reason it is a breakfast.

Nihari takes its name from nahar, the Arabic word for morning, and the name is really an instruction. Shank and marrow bones go into the pot in the evening with browned onion and ginger, the lid is sealed, and the fire is left to die under it. In the morning the seal comes off and the collagen has become gelatine, the marrow has melted out of the bones, and the broth has already thickened on its own. A little wheat flour whisked into water goes in near the end to finish it, a potli perfumes the pot, and hot ghee reddened with chilli is poured over the top.

It comes to the table with the bones in it, under a heap of shredded ginger, green chilli, chopped coriander and a wedge of lime, and it is eaten with khameeri roti. It is genuinely a breakfast. Shops open before dawn and sell until the pot is empty, which is often by nine in the morning.

Nihari belongs to Delhi and to Lucknow both, and each city will tell you it belongs there. One detail every shop shares is the taar: a ladleful from today's pot is held back and goes into tomorrow's, and a famous shop will say its pot has run unbroken for decades. Nobody can check that, and it gets repeated anyway, but the practice itself is real and it is what gives an old pot its depth.

The sweet end, and the street.

Shahi tukda is what a rich kitchen does with stale bread. Slices are fried in ghee until they are dark and crisp, soaked briefly in sugar syrup and covered in rabri, which is milk boiled down slowly until it is thick and faintly grainy. Pistachio, almond and a sheet of edible silver go on top. It is the one dish in this module that is better for having been made a few hours before it is wanted.

Malai makhan belongs to winter and to nowhere else. Milk and cream are whipped to a foam, left out overnight so that the cold and the dew set it, and sold early, dusted with sugar, pistachio and saffron. It collapses in the mouth and it does not survive to the afternoon, so Lucknow sells it off handcarts on winter mornings and not at all between March and November.

Banaras belongs to this region as much as Lucknow does, and Banaras has the chaat. Tamatar chaat is the one to know. Tomatoes are cooked down hard with potato, ginger and spice until the mixture is thick and almost jammy, then spooned hot into a bowl folded out of leaves and finished with lime, coriander and crisp fried shreds. It is eaten standing up, and it is hot rather than cold, which surprises anyone who knows chaat only from Delhi.
