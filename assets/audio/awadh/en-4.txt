The Nawabs, 1856, and where the cooks went.

In 1784 the rains failed across Awadh and people began to starve. The Nawab, Asaf-ud-Daula, answered with a building project, and the project ran for years because it was meant to. The Bara Imambara in Lucknow is what came of it, and it is famine relief in the shape of a building.

Awadh was a Mughal province. In 1722 the emperor sent a Persian nobleman, Saadat Khan, to govern it, and within a generation the post had become hereditary and the province effectively independent. Delhi's authority was collapsing, and the men who held the richest provinces simply stopped sending most of the revenue north. Awadh had revenue to keep, because the Gangetic plain is some of the best farmland in Asia.

What the family brought with it mattered more than the politics did. The Nawabs were Shia and their connections ran to Iran, so Persian poetry, Persian manners and Persian food arrived with them and were paid for out of the wheat of the plain. The court sat at Faizabad first, and Asaf-ud-Daula moved it to Lucknow in 1775.

Asaf-ud-Daula commissioned the Bara Imambara in 1784, with Kifayatullah as architect. Its vast central hall has no supporting beams or pillars. Above it, the Bhul Bhulaiya forms a maze of passages within the upper structure.

A popular story says labourers built by day while impoverished nobles dismantled work at night so both groups could earn without meeting. Uttar Pradesh's official account presents it as tradition. The documented point is that the project provided employment during famine for years.

Culinary accounts distinguish the bawarchi, who cooked in quantity, from the rakabdar, associated with specialised dishes for elite patrons. The boundary was occupational rather than absolute, and later storytelling has embellished their rivalries.

The court's wealth supported cooks, musicians, textile workers and other specialists. Long cooking and costly aromatics became signs of refinement, but Awadhi food also developed in homes, markets and religious settings beyond the palace.

The East India Company had been squeezing Awadh for half a century. A subsidiary alliance signed in 1801 took half the territory and obliged the Nawab to pay for troops he did not command, which left a ruler with revenue, no army and very little power. On 7 February 1856 the Company annexed what was left, on the stated ground that Awadh was misgoverned.

The Company justified annexation by alleging misgovernment, a judgment shaped by its own political and territorial interests. Wajid Ali Shah did not wage war. He travelled to Calcutta to petition and later established a court in exile at Metiabruz, where he lived until 1887.

His mother went further than he did. Janab-i-Aliya sailed for London to put her son's case to Queen Victoria, reached an audience, got nowhere, and died in Paris in 1858 on the way home.

The annexation cost the Company an army. Awadh had supplied a large share of the sepoys in the Bengal Army, and those men's families had just been dispossessed. When the revolt broke out in 1857 Lucknow rose with it, and the British garrison and the civilians with it shut themselves into the Residency compound in the middle of the city.

The siege began in late June. Havelock and Outram reached the Residency in September but were besieged in turn. Colin Campbell evacuated the position in November, and British forces retook Lucknow in March 1858. Begum Hazrat Mahal meanwhile governed in the name of her young son, Birjis Qadr, until she was driven into exile in Nepal.

Some cooks and members of the court followed Wajid Ali Shah to Metiabruz, carrying Awadhi techniques into Calcutta. Kolkata biryani developed from that migration and came to include potato, although explanations for when and why it was added remain disputed. Other cooks remained in Lucknow, where court dishes entered shops, wedding menus and household repertoires.

Awadhi cooking now lives in street food, restaurants, weddings and homes. These are continuing traditions shaped by a former court, not museum copies of one vanished kitchen.

Court patronage helps explain the prestige of saffron, kewra and slow sealed cooking. Cooks then carried those techniques into commercial and domestic settings, where they changed with new customers, budgets and ingredients.
