The Nawabs, 1856, and where the cooks went.

In 1784 the rains failed across Awadh and people began to starve. The Nawab, Asaf-ud-Daula, answered with a building project, and the project ran for years because it was meant to. The Bara Imambara in Lucknow is what came of it, and it is famine relief in the shape of a building.

How a governor became a court.

Awadh was a Mughal province. In 1722 the emperor sent a Persian nobleman, Saadat Khan, to govern it, and within a generation the post had become hereditary and the province effectively independent. Delhi's authority was collapsing, and the men who held the richest provinces simply stopped sending most of the revenue north. Awadh had revenue to keep, because the Gangetic plain is some of the best farmland in Asia.

What the family brought with it mattered more than the politics did. The Nawabs were Shia and their connections ran to Iran, so Persian poetry, Persian manners and Persian food arrived with them and were paid for out of the wheat of the plain. The court sat at Faizabad first, and Asaf-ud-Daula moved it to Lucknow in 1775.

The building and the famine.

The Bara Imambara was begun in 1784 and its architect was Kifayatullah. The central hall runs about fifty metres and stands about fifteen high, and it is roofed without a single beam or pillar, which is why photographs of it look wrong until you work out what is missing. Above that hall, inside the thickness of the roof, runs the Bhul Bhulaiya, several hundred identical doorways and passages that visitors still get lost in.

The story everybody tells is that the labourers built by day while the impoverished nobility, who could not be seen doing manual work, pulled parts down by night, so that the work and the wages went on longer. It is told with great confidence and it cannot be checked. A deliberately slow project during a famine is the plausible part of it. What can be checked is the proverb that stuck to the man: whom God does not provide for, Asaf-ud-Daula provides for.

Bawarchis and rakabdars.

The court fed itself through two kinds of cook, and the distinction is still useful. A bawarchi cooked in quantity, for a household or a garrison, and was judged on a pot that came out right every single day. A rakabdar cooked small, a few plates at a time for one patron, and was judged on invention instead. Rakabdars competed with each other, and the competition ran towards dishes that disguised what they were.

Stories about that kitchen are told very freely, and none of them traces back to a contemporary record. Take the structure rather than the anecdotes. A rich Shia court with cheap labour, expensive aromatics and time on its hands produced exactly the cuisine you would expect: long, slow, pale, perfumed and almost entirely without shortcuts. The same court paid for the attar-makers of Kannauj, for chikankari embroidery and for a great deal of music.

1856.

The East India Company had been squeezing Awadh for half a century. A subsidiary alliance signed in 1801 took half the territory and obliged the Nawab to pay for troops he did not command, which left a ruler with revenue, no army and very little power. On 7 February 1856 the Company annexed what was left, on the stated ground that Awadh was misgoverned.

The charge was not simply invented, because the administration was genuinely bad. It was also self-serving. The Company had spent decades making the state ungovernable and then presented the bill for the result, and the annexation removed a treaty ally who had never fought them. Wajid Ali Shah refused to sign the instrument and refused to fight. He travelled to Calcutta to petition, was detained there, and settled at Metiabruz on the southern edge of the city for the last thirty years of his life.

His mother went further than he did. Janab-i-Aliya sailed for London to put her son's case to Queen Victoria, reached an audience, got nowhere, and died in Paris in 1858 on the way home.

1857, and the Residency.

The annexation cost the Company an army. Awadh had supplied a large share of the sepoys in the Bengal Army, and those men's families had just been dispossessed. When the revolt broke out in 1857 Lucknow rose with it, and the British garrison and the civilians with it shut themselves into the Residency compound in the middle of the city.

The siege ran from the end of June. A relief force under Havelock and Outram fought its way in during September and was promptly besieged in turn, Colin Campbell evacuated the whole position in November, and the city was not retaken until March 1858. Awadh meanwhile had a government of its own. Birjis Qadr, Wajid Ali Shah's young son, was proclaimed ruler, and his mother Begum Hazrat Mahal held the state and the fighting together until she was driven out. She went into exile in Nepal and died there.

Where the cooks went.

A court that stops being a court stops paying cooks. Some of Awadh's cooks followed Wajid Ali Shah to Metiabruz, and the module on Bengal and the east picks that thread up, because Kolkata biryani has a potato in it and it arrived there from this kitchen. The rest stayed in Lucknow, and over the following decades the food came down out of the palaces and onto the street, which is where it still is. The kebab shop in the Chowk that opened in 1905 is the most visible piece of that. It is not a restaurant serving court food; it is a shop that sells one thing extremely well.

That is the honest shape of it. Awadhi cooking survives as street food, as wedding food and as a handful of family recipes, and not as a living court cuisine, because the court has been gone since 1856.

Why this is in a cookery course.

Because the perfume explains itself once you know who was paying for it. Attar, saffron and kewra are court habits, and dum is what a kitchen does when fuel and time cost less than ingredients do. Neither habit would have survived on its own. They survived because roughly a hundred and thirty years of a Persian-speaking, Shia and extremely rich court trained enough cooks that when the money stopped the method did not.
