Dum: rice, bread and the sealed pot.

The pot arrives at the table still closed. A ring of hard browned dough runs round the join between the lid and the rim, and somebody cracks it with the back of a knife. What comes out first is not food. It is the smell, all of it at once, and that is the entire reason the pot was carried out unopened.

Dum means breath, and a dish cooked under dum is cooked in its own. A rope of plain wheat dough is pressed round the rim, the lid goes on, and within a few minutes the dough has baked into a gasket. The pot then sits over a very low fire, usually with hot coals laid on the lid as well, so that heat arrives from above and below at once.

The seal slows moisture and volatile aromas from escaping. Because the pot remains closed, the cook cannot stir or correct the liquid halfway through, so the layering and heat must be right at the start. Gentle heat lets rice and pieces of meat keep their shape.

That also explains the manners. Breaking the seal at the table is not theatre for its own sake. The aroma is a real part of what was cooked, it is released exactly once, and the cook would rather the guests had it than the kitchen did.

Hyderabad is known for kacchi biryani, where raw marinated meat and part-boiled rice finish together. A common Awadhi method is pakki: meat is cooked first, rice is partly cooked in seasoned stock, and the two are layered before the sealed finish.

This often produces a paler, gently spiced biryani, although recipes in both cities vary. Kewra, saffron and whole spices may be added as the layers are built. The boundary between biryani and pulao is debated rather than fixed.

Dum pukht describes an approach rather than one recipe. Awadhi cooks apply sealed, gentle cooking to rice, meat, poultry, vegetables and pulses. Modern recipes differ in how tightly they seal the pot and whether coals sit on the lid.

The method suits tough gelatinous cuts, which is the practical reason a court kitchen with fuel and time to spare became so good at it. Shank, neck and shoulder are full of collagen. Held just under boiling for hours, without stirring and without losing water, that collagen turns to gelatine, so the meat falls apart and the broth thickens on its own. Little or no flour is needed, and no reduction or browning. It is slow cooking taken to the point where the cook's only real jobs are building the seal and keeping the fire small.

A sheermal is baked in a tandoor, but it differs from a lean roti. Shir is Persian for milk. Flour, milk, ghee and a little sugar make an enriched dough that is rested, shaped, baked and brushed with saffron milk while hot.

Sheermal is often paired with kebabs and rich gravies. Its soft crumb folds around a delicate galouti, while its slight sweetness balances savoury meat and spice.

Three other breads belong to the same table. Khameeri roti is raised with a wheat starter called khameer and often accompanies nihari. Taftan is another enriched bread, sometimes scented with saffron and cardamom. Rumali roti is stretched almost translucent, cooked briefly over an upturned iron dome and folded like a handkerchief, or rumal.

A formal Awadhi meal may be arranged on a dastarkhwan, a cloth spread for the meal. Several dishes can be shared together, allowing diners to combine bread, rice, gravies and kebabs.

Bread does the job of cutlery, and that shapes the food above it. Gravies are thick enough to sit on a torn piece of sheermal without running off it, kebabs are boneless and small, and almost nothing on the cloth needs a knife. It is also part of why the kitchen cares so much about aroma. Nothing is plated and nothing is composed, so what announces a dish is the smell coming off it.
