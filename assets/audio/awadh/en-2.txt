Dum: rice, bread and the sealed pot.

The pot arrives at the table still closed. A ring of hard browned dough runs round the join between the lid and the rim, and somebody cracks it with the back of a knife. What comes out first is not food. It is the smell, all of it at once, and that is the entire reason the pot was carried out unopened.

What the seal actually does.

Dum means breath, and a dish cooked under dum is cooked in its own. A rope of plain wheat dough is pressed round the rim, the lid goes on, and within a few minutes the dough has baked into a gasket. The pot then sits over a very low fire, usually with hot coals laid on the lid as well, so that heat arrives from above and below at once.

Three things follow, and they are worth separating. Nothing evaporates, so a dish that would otherwise need water added cooks in the moisture already inside it and the flavour never dilutes. Nothing is stirred, because nothing can be, so grains of rice and pieces of meat keep their shape instead of breaking down. And nothing volatile escapes, which is the point that matters most here. Everything the last reading described — the kewra, the rose water, the saffron, the mace — would be on the kitchen ceiling within a minute of an open pot. Under a seal it has nowhere to go except back into the food.

That also explains the manners. Breaking the seal at the table is not theatre for its own sake. The aroma is a real part of what was cooked, it is released exactly once, and the cook would rather the guests had it than the kitchen did.

The other biryani.

Hyderabad's module in this course described the kacchi method, where raw marinated meat and part-boiled rice go into the pot together and have to finish at the same moment. Awadh generally works the other way round. The meat is cooked first in an aromatic stock, the rice is part-boiled in that same stock, the two are layered, and the sealed pot then finishes a dish that is already most of the way there.

The consequences are on the plate. An Awadhi biryani is paler, milder and more evenly flavoured than a Hyderabadi one, because the rice took up the meat's own broth rather than steaming above raw meat. Less chilli goes in and more whole spice, and the kewra goes in as the layers are built. Lucknow argues about whether its own dish should be called biryani or pulao at all, and that argument has been running long enough that nobody is going to settle it.

Neither method is the original one. Both are old, both are court food, and a restaurant menu that claims otherwise is selling something.

Dum is not only for rice.

Here Awadh parts company with the places that use a sealed pot for biryani and for nothing else. A whole kid goes under the seal. So does a chicken, in dum ka murgh, and so do vegetables, lentils and the overnight stew in the next reading. The court style carries the name: dum pukht is Persian, roughly "cooked by steam", and it describes an approach rather than a single dish.

The method suits tough gelatinous cuts, which is the practical reason a court kitchen with fuel and time to spare became so good at it. Shank, neck and shoulder are full of collagen. Held just under boiling for hours, without stirring and without losing water, that collagen turns to gelatine, so the meat falls apart and the broth thickens on its own. Little or no flour is needed, and no reduction or browning. It is slow cooking taken to the point where the cook's only real jobs are building the seal and keeping the fire small.

The breads.

Punjab's module explains the tandoor, and a sheermal is baked in one, but what comes out has nothing in common with a lean roti. Shir is Persian for milk and the dough is made with it: flour, milk, ghee and a little sugar, kneaded, rested, rolled into a disc and baked, then brushed with saffron milk the moment it comes out. The bread is soft, faintly sweet and stained orange, and it is closer kin to a brioche than to anything else in this course.

Sheermal exists largely because of the kebabs. A galouti is too soft to pick up on its own, and a sheermal is supple enough to fold round one without tearing and sweet enough to argue with the spice. The two are sold side by side on the same street.

Three other breads belong to the same table. Khameeri roti is raised with a wheat starter called khameer, and it is what nihari is eaten with. Taftan is another enriched bread, close to sheermal, and it often carries saffron and cardamom. Rumali roti is the outlier: a huge round of dough is stretched until it is nearly translucent, thrown, cooked for seconds on the back of an upturned iron wok and then folded like a handkerchief, which is what rumal means.

How it all reaches the table.

A formal Awadhi meal is served on a dastarkhwan, a cloth spread on the floor, and the word first meant the cloth rather than the feast. Everything comes at once. Korma, kebabs, biryani, a dal, a vegetable and a stack of bread go down together, and the eater builds each mouthful instead of working through courses.

Bread does the job of cutlery, and that shapes the food above it. Gravies are thick enough to sit on a torn piece of sheermal without running off it, kebabs are boneless and small, and almost nothing on the cloth needs a knife. It is also part of why the kitchen cares so much about aroma. Nothing is plated and nothing is composed, so what announces a dish is the smell coming off it.
