Attar and aroma: the perfumed pantry.

In a Lucknow kitchen the most expensive thing on the shelf is a small bottle with a glass stopper, and it holds about thirty millilitres. It is attar, a distilled essence, and a cook uses it by the drop. Get the dose right and a pot of rice smells of roses from the doorway. Get it wrong and the same rice tastes of soap.

Distilled, not ground.

The perfume trade and this kitchen share a town. Kannauj sits on the Ganges about a hundred and twenty kilometres from Lucknow, it has distilled attar for centuries, and it still does the work by hand. Flowers go into a copper still called a deg, the join is sealed with clay and cotton, and a low fire burns under it. The vapour travels down a bamboo pipe into a receiver, the bhapka, which stands in water and already holds sandalwood oil. The oil catches the scent as the vapour condenses into it, and the same oil is charged with batch after batch until it is saturated.

That process explains why the Awadhi pantry behaves as it does. An attar is not an extract in alcohol and it is not a syrup. It is fragrance carried in oil, it is concentrated to the point of being awkward, and it will not survive cooking, because heat drives it straight off. So it goes in at the very end, or into a sealed pot where it cannot escape, and the sealed pot is the subject of the next reading.

Kannauj also distils a scent that has nothing to do with flowers. Mitti attar is made from baked clay, and it smells of the first rain falling on dry earth. Nobody cooks with it. It is worth knowing about because it shows what the town believes its job to be: catching a smell that would otherwise be gone within the hour.

Kewra, rose, and the question of dose.

Kewra is the signature of this kitchen and the ingredient outsiders meet last. It comes from the male flower of the screwpine, Pandanus odorifer, a spiny coastal shrub whose flower spike is cut at dawn and distilled the same day. Most of India's kewra water is made in Ganjam district in Odisha, several hundred kilometres away, and it reaches Lucknow in bottles. The smell is sweet and honeyed with something green underneath it, somewhere between rose and hyacinth, and it is not a subtle thing.

Rose water is the other half of the pair. Awadh uses both, often in the same meal, because they pull in different directions. Rose is round and soft and sits close to the food; kewra is sharper and carries across a room. A biryani usually gets kewra, a milk sweet usually gets rose, and a korma may get a little of each.

Dose is the whole skill, and it is where a European kitchen goes wrong first. Rose water sold as rose water is dilute, and a tablespoon of it in a pot of rice is about right. Rose essence comes in a much smaller bottle, it is many times stronger, and that same tablespoon would ruin the dish for everyone at the table. The two are different products and the label rarely makes it obvious which one you are holding.

The potli, and the spice you take out again.

Here is the form that belongs to Awadh and to almost nowhere else. A potli is a small muslin bag. Whole spices go into it — mace, green cardamom, black cardamom, cinnamon, bay, coriander seed, peppercorns, and in the older versions dried rose petals, vetiver root and chips of sandalwood. The bag is tied shut, dropped into the simmering pot, and squeezed and lifted out before the dish is served.

Think about what that buys. A ground masala gives up everything it has and then stays where it is, so it darkens the gravy, thickens it and leaves grit on the tongue. A potli gives up only what dissolves out of it and leaves nothing behind, so the gravy stays pale and clean. That matters enormously in a kitchen whose kormas are the colour of cream. The cook also keeps hold of the timing: when the pot smells right the bag comes out, and nothing gets any stronger after that.

It is why nihari tastes the way it does. The overnight stew in reading three is perfumed with a potli rather than with a spoonful of powder, and the difference is a broth you can see through instead of one that has gone muddy.

In the pantry: potli masala.

 Aroma: Mace and green cardamom over cinnamon and bay, with rose and vetiver behind them.

 Flavour: Very little on its own. It perfumes a liquid rather than seasoning it.

 What it does: Scents a stew or a stock without darkening it, thickening it or leaving grit.

 When to add: Tied in muslin, dropped into the simmering liquid, then squeezed and lifted out as soon as the pot smells right.

 Instead of it: Whole spices loose in the pot and strained out later. Close, but much harder to stop at the right moment.

Warmth instead of heat.

Awadh does use chilli, and it uses less of it than almost anywhere else in this course. Andhra grinds Guntur chilli by the kilo, and Lucknow puts in a little Kashmiri chilli for colour and then gets its warmth from somewhere else entirely. White pepper does some of the work, because it is sharp at the front of the mouth and gone quickly. Long slow cooking does the rest.

Green cardamom is the spice you will taste most often, and it is worth handling properly. A whole pod in a pot gives a clean, cool, faintly camphorous perfume and very little else; the same pod ground gives a much stronger and slightly bitter flavour that a korma does not want. So Awadh uses it whole almost every time, in the rice, in the potli and in the milk for a sweet, and a diner who bites one simply puts it on the side of the plate.

Saffron does nothing at all for heat. It colours from the inside and it perfumes, and it is crushed and steeped in warm milk for a quarter of an hour before it goes anywhere near the pot, because threads dropped in dry give up very little. Kashmir grows India's saffron and its own module explains how; what Awadh does with it is different. Here it goes into biryani, into sheermal, into rabri and into shahi tukda, and it is a scent and a colour rather than a flavour.

The habit runs through the whole kitchen. Where another cook would reach for a chilli, an Awadhi cook reaches for a scent. That is one sentence, and it is most of what you need in order to taste the difference.
