Attar and aroma: the perfumed pantry.

A few drops of floral distillate can change a whole pot of rice. In Lucknow's courtly cooking, rose, kewra and food-grade aromatic essences are measured carefully: enough to perfume the food, never enough to make it taste like soap.

The perfume trade and this kitchen meet at Kannauj, on the Ganges west of Lucknow. In the traditional deg-bhapka process, plant material and water heat in a copper still sealed with cloth and clay. Vapour travels through a pipe into a cooled receiver containing a carrier oil, traditionally sandalwood.

Attar is fragrance carried in oil rather than alcohol, but most perfumery attar is not a food ingredient. A cook must use a product explicitly sold as food-grade. Volatile aromas fade with prolonged heat, so culinary essences are added late or trapped inside a sealed pot.

Kannauj also distils mitti attar from baked clay to evoke the smell of rain on dry earth. It belongs to perfumery, not the cooking pot, but it shows the range of materials used by the town's distillers.

Kewra is one of the kitchen's characteristic aromas. It comes from the male flower of the screwpine, Pandanus odorifer. Government sources report that Ganjam district in Odisha produces about 90 per cent of India's kewda flowers, which are distilled into water, essence and oil.

Rose water is the other half of the pair. Awadh uses both, often in the same meal, because they pull in different directions. Rose is round and soft and sits close to the food; kewra is sharper and carries across a room. A biryani usually gets kewra, a milk sweet usually gets rose, and a korma may get a little of each.

Dose is the whole skill, and it is where a European kitchen goes wrong first. Rose water sold as rose water is dilute, and a tablespoon of it in a pot of rice is about right. Rose essence comes in a much smaller bottle, it is many times stronger, and that same tablespoon would ruin the dish for everyone at the table. The two are different products and the label rarely makes it obvious which one you are holding.

A potli is a small muslin bag filled with whole spices such as mace, cardamom, cinnamon, bay, coriander seed and peppercorns. It simmers in stock or stew, then is squeezed gently and removed before serving.

Think about what that buys. A ground masala gives up everything it has and then stays where it is, so it darkens the gravy, thickens it and leaves grit on the tongue. A potli gives up only what dissolves out of it and leaves nothing behind, so the gravy stays pale and clean. That matters enormously in a kitchen whose kormas are the colour of cream. The cook also keeps hold of the timing: when the pot smells right the bag comes out, and nothing gets any stronger after that.

Nihari can be perfumed with a potli rather than a spoonful of powder. The bag contributes aroma while keeping fragments of spice out of the broth.

Many courtly Awadhi dishes use chilli with restraint and build warmth with pepper and aromatic spices. This is a tendency within a diverse regional cuisine, not a rule for every household or street dish.

Green cardamom changes character with preparation. Whole pods release a gentler aroma into rice, stock or milk, while ground seed tastes stronger and can turn bitter in excess. Awadhi cooks use both forms according to the dish.

Saffron contributes colour and aroma, not chilli heat. Crushing the threads and steeping them in warm milk helps disperse both. In Awadhi cooking, saffron may scent biryani, sheermal, rabri and shahi tukda.

The habit runs through the whole kitchen. Where another cook would reach for a chilli, an Awadhi cook reaches for a scent. That is one sentence, and it is most of what you need in order to taste the difference.
