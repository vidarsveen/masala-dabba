Mughals in the mountains: why the valley eats as it does.

Around 1620 the emperor Jahangir developed a pleasure garden beside Dal Lake. Water runs down its middle from terrace to terrace, chinar trees line the walks, and pavilions punctuate the pools. Shalimar Bagh joins the Persian chahar bagh idea to Kashmiri slopes and abundant water. The garden shows how forms from elsewhere changed when they reached the valley.

The valley was a centre of Buddhist and then Hindu learning for a very long time before any of that. Sanskrit scholarship ran deep here, and Kalhana finished the Rajatarangini, a chronicle of the kings of Kashmir, around 1148, which makes it one of the earliest works of its kind written in India. Saffron belongs to that layer too. The crop and the trade in it are attested centuries before the Mughals, so whatever else they brought, they did not bring the valley its most famous spice.

A sultanate was established in 1339. Zain-ul-Abidin, who ruled for much of the period from about 1420 to 1470, is credited with supporting crafts including papier-mâché, carpet knotting, shawl weaving and fine woodwork. Later accounts also connect Central Asian cooks with the development of wazwan, but the route and date cannot be established with the same confidence.

Some versions trace those cooks to migrations around Timur's campaigns at the end of the fourteenth century. That remains a tradition rather than a securely documented origin story. The safer conclusion is that Kashmir's court cuisine developed through sustained contact with Persianate and Central Asian worlds.

Akbar annexed Kashmir in 1586, and Mughal rulers later developed gardens beside the lake and in the side valleys. Jahangir's Shalimar took shape around 1620. Nishat Bagh followed under Shah Jahan in the 1630s, alongside gardens at Achabal and Verinag. Their channels and terraces adapted the chahar bagh tradition to steep ground.

The oriental plane, Platanus orientalis, had been planted in Kashmir under sultans as well as Mughals. Their gardens made the chinar especially visible along walks and watercourses. The immense tree turns red in autumn and has since become an emblem of the valley.

Kashmir's courtly food also shows Persianate and Central Asian influence in its vocabulary and techniques. Names such as rogan josh, yakhni, korma and pulav travelled across languages and courts. Cooks in the valley adapted them to local rice, yoghurt, fennel, chilli and saffron. The result is a Kashmiri cuisine rather than a foreign menu preserved unchanged.

Dal Lake is shallow, and cultivation has long been part of life on it. Growers build beds from aquatic plants and mud, anchor them with willow poles and plant vegetables such as cucumber and tomato. These floating gardens, the rad, can be cut and moved. Produce reaches the boat market early in the morning.

The lotus grows in the same water, and its rhizome is nadru, pulled from the mud in the cold months. Dal Lake is also under severe pressure. Research records decades of deteriorating water quality, sewage and nutrient loading, expanding construction in the catchment, and a decline in floating-garden area since 1980.

A Kashmiri samovar is a copper vessel with a charcoal chamber through its middle, while tea sits in the jacket around it and stays warm. The Russian-derived name and the vessel's wider use across Eurasia point to the valley's northern and western connections.

Mountain passes carried goods and skills in several directions. Shawls and saffron went out; tea, dried fruit and techniques came in. Kashmir's food records those exchanges while remaining rooted in the valley's crops and communities.

Mughal power faded, and the valley changed hands repeatedly. Afghan rule under the Durranis began in 1752 and is remembered badly. The Sikh empire took Kashmir in 1819. In 1846, after the first Anglo-Sikh war, the Treaty of Amritsar handed the territory to Gulab Singh of Jammu for a payment, and his Dogra dynasty ruled until 1947.

Under several regimes, shawl workers and cultivators faced heavy taxation, while nineteenth-century accounts repeatedly describe scarcity and famine. Those conditions shaped household budgets and access to meat, though they do not explain any single recipe. The contrast between everyday haak and rice and the abundance of a wedding wazwan is economic as well as ceremonial.

Kashmir's modern political history remains contested and painful. Amid the insurgency and violence around 1990, most Pandit families left or were forced to leave the valley. Many carried their cooking to Jammu, Delhi and farther afield. Muslim and Pandit traditions both belong here because both are Kashmiri.

The history is visible in ordinary culinary choices. Muslim and Pandit cooks may use the same dish name while following different rules for onion and garlic. Court vocabulary met local rice, fennel, chilli and saffron; a Eurasian tea vessel became Kashmiri household equipment. The valley did not merely receive these things. Its cooks made them belong.
