Mughals in the mountains: why the valley eats as it does.

In 1619 the emperor Jahangir had a garden built on the eastern shore of Dal Lake. Water is carried down its middle from terrace to terrace, chinar trees line the walks, and a stone pavilion sits in the highest pool. Shalimar Bagh was not a Kashmiri idea. It is a Persian garden plan, and the emperor who commissioned it had come up over the mountains from the plains with a court of several thousand people, mainly to get out of the heat.

What was here first.

The valley was a centre of Buddhist and then Hindu learning for a very long time before any of that. Sanskrit scholarship ran deep here, and Kalhana finished the Rajatarangini, a chronicle of the kings of Kashmir, around 1148, which makes it one of the earliest works of its kind written in India. Saffron belongs to that layer too. The crop and the trade in it are attested centuries before the Mughals, so whatever else they brought, they did not bring the valley its most famous spice.

A sultanate was established in 1339, and the reign of Zain-ul-Abidin, from about 1420 to 1470, is the one everybody in Kashmir still names. He is credited with bringing craftsmen from Central Asia and settling them in the valley, and the trades they taught are still working: papier-mâché, carpet knotting, shawl weaving, fine woodwork. Whether cooks came with the carpet weavers is the open question in the story of the wazwan.

That story is usually traced to cooks who arrived with or after the campaigns of Timur, who sacked Delhi in 1398. Traced to is the right phrase, and it should not be firmed up into a fact. What can be said without argument is that the food carries Central Asian and Persian technique, and that the vocabulary it uses is not Indian.

The Mughals come north.

Akbar annexed Kashmir in 1586, and for the next century the court came up for the summer whenever it could. The journey over the Pir Panjal was serious, the destination was cool and green, and the Mughals responded by building gardens along the lake and in the side valleys: Shalimar in 1619, Nishat Bagh in 1633, and others at Achabal and Verinag.

The chinar is part of the same inheritance. It is the oriental plane, Platanus orientalis, and the valley's great chinars were planted rather than wild, many of them under the sultans and the Mughals, who used them to line the gardens the way a Persian garden is lined. A mature chinar is enormous, turns red in October, and is now the emblem of the place.

What arrived in the kitchen alongside the gardens was a way of cooking: meat simmered in yoghurt rather than in tomato, dried fruit and nuts used in savoury food, rice perfumed rather than plain, and saffron used in the same dish as meat. Read a Kashmiri menu and the Persian is obvious once you look. Rogan, josh, yakhni, korma and pulav are all Persian words, and kahwa comes from the Arabic for coffee by way of the same trade.

The lake that is also a farm.

Dal Lake is shallow, and the people who live on it have farmed it for centuries. Weed and mud are piled onto rafts of lake vegetation, anchored with willow poles, and planted with melon, cucumber and tomato. These floating gardens, the rad, can be cut loose and towed. The vegetable market that meets on the water before dawn, boat to boat, sells what has been lifted off them that morning.

The lotus grows in the same water, and its root is nadru, pulled out of the mud in the cold months. So the landmark of this region and one of its best dishes are the same body of water, which is not true of many places. The lake is also under pressure: it has shrunk a great deal over the last century, from silt, sewage and building, and the gardens and the lotus beds have shrunk with it.

The samovar in the corner.

Look at the object rather than the menu and the same conclusion arrives faster. A Kashmiri samovar is a copper vessel with a chamber running up the middle for burning charcoal, and the tea sits in the jacket around it and stays hot all day. The word is Russian and the design belongs to a belt that runs from Iran through Central Asia, and Kashmir sits at the end of it rather than outside it.

The passes did the work. Kashmir traded north and west over the mountains for as long as it traded south into India, and shawls, saffron and paper went out while tea, dried fruit and technique came in. A kitchen is a slow record of who a place did business with, and this one reads as a Central Asian kitchen that happens to be in India.

Afghans, Sikhs and Dogras.

Mughal power faded, and the valley changed hands repeatedly. Afghan rule under the Durranis began in 1752 and is remembered badly. The Sikh empire took Kashmir in 1819. In 1846, after the first Anglo-Sikh war, the Treaty of Amritsar handed the territory to Gulab Singh of Jammu for a payment, and his Dogra dynasty ruled until 1947.

What each of those regimes did to the food is mostly a matter of taxes and poverty rather than recipes. Kashmir's shawl weavers and saffron growers were taxed hard under several of them, and famine is a recurring word in the nineteenth-century record of the valley. A cuisine of rice, greens, curd and one pot of meat is partly the product of that. The wazwan is what a wedding looks like in a place where ordinary dinner is haak and rice.

The modern political history of Kashmir is contested and painful, and this course is not going to adjudicate any of it. Punjab's reading covers 1947 and what the border did to that plain. Here it is enough to say that the valley's kitchens, Muslim and Pandit, grew up next to each other over centuries, that a great many Pandit families left the valley in and after 1990, and that their cooking now lives partly in Jammu and Delhi. Both kitchens are in this module because both are Kashmiri.

Why any of this is on a plate.

Because it explains what is otherwise a contradiction. A Brahmin kitchen here eats meat and refuses onion, and a Muslim kitchen a street away eats the same dishes built on shallot and garlic. The names on both menus are Persian, the tea comes out of a Central Asian vessel, the rice is short because of the altitude, and the one genuinely local luxury, saffron, was growing on the karewa before any of the conquerors arrived. Everything on the trami came from somewhere, and the valley has been keeping the receipts for a thousand years.
