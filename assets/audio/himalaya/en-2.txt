Rice, the wazwan and the seventh course.

Four men sit down on the floor around a copper platter the size of a cartwheel. Rice is heaped on it and divided into four by the meat laid across the top. A boy comes round with a jug and a basin, the tash-t-naer, so everyone can wash their right hand where they sit. Nobody is given a plate, because the platter is the plate. It is called a trami, and it feeds exactly four people.

Rice, and why it is short.

The floor of the Kashmir valley lies at about 1,600 metres, ringed by mountains, with a short growing season and cold nights. Rice grown in those conditions is short-grained and slightly sticky rather than long and separate, and that is the rice the valley eats. Basmati is a plains grain and belongs to Punjab, just over the mountains to the south, where the staple is bread in any case.

Rice goes on the table at both main meals, boiled plain, and everything else is poured over it. A Kashmiri asks what the gravy is, never what the rice is. One variety is worth a name: mushk budji, a small aromatic rice from the higher villages of Anantnag, which very nearly disappeared and has been brought back by a few hundred growers over the last fifteen years or so. It is cooked for weddings and for guests, and almost never for a Tuesday.

Who cooks a wazwan.

A wedding banquet in the valley is not cooked by the household. It is cooked by a waza, a professional cook from a hereditary trade, working with a team under a vasta waza, the head of it. They arrive the day before with their own copper vessels, dig fire pits in the yard, and butcher, pound and cook through the night.

The physical centre of the whole cuisine happens at that stage. Meat is laid on a flat stone and beaten with a wooden mallet until the fibre breaks down and the sinew can be picked out, and what is left is a paste that will hold together in a ball with no egg and no flour in it. Machines now do some of this, and the wazas will tell you at length that the mallet gives a texture a machine cannot. The sound of several men pounding meat in the dark is what a Kashmiri associates with the night before a wedding.

The number of courses is quoted as thirty-six so often that it has become the standard answer, and it should be treated as a figure of speech rather than a count. What is actually served depends on the household and on the money, and a modern wazwan is often shorter. The shape, though, does not change.

The order, and the course that ends it.

The trami arrives already loaded. Under the meat there is rice, and on it sit seekh kebabs, two pieces of tabak maaz, a piece of chicken cooked white and another cooked yellow with saffron, and a portion of methi maaz. Guests begin with those while the rest of the meal is still in the pots.

Then the courses come one at a time, and each is laid onto the same platter by a server working down the row. Rogan josh, aab gosht cooked in milk, daniwal korma green with coriander, marchwangan korma hot with chilli, and rista, the red meatball. Most guests lose count somewhere around the seventh, which is why the thirty-six is best left alone. The order is what carries the meaning, not the total.

Rista and gushtaba are both made from that pounded paste. Rista is the smaller ball, simmered in a red gravy coloured with Kashmiri chilli. Gushtaba is larger, paler and softer, and it is finished in a gravy of whisked yoghurt. Gushtaba is served last, and that is not a matter of taste but of grammar: it is the full stop of the meal. A guest who has been paying attention leaves room for it, because eating it is how you say the evening was good.

All of this belongs to the Muslim kitchen of the valley. Kashmiri Pandit households hold their own feasts, cooked to the rules the next reading sets out, and those are emphatically not a wazwan.

Down the other side: the dham.

Over the Pir Panjal in Himachal Pradesh, the ceremonial meal is called a dham, and it is vegetarian. It is cooked the night before in enormous brass vessels by botis, cooks from a hereditary caste who do this and nothing else, and it is eaten sitting on the floor in rows off plates stitched from leaves. Guests are served in fixed order, and the meal ends with a sweet rice.

The dishes themselves are soured rather than spiced. Madra is chickpeas or kidney beans simmered in a gravy of whisked yoghurt, and it carries cardamom and clove rather than chilli, so it lands surprisingly close to a Kashmiri yakhni without any contact between the two kitchens. Where two mountain cuisines both cook in yoghurt, the reason is usually the same: yoghurt is what a household with animals has, and it will thicken a gravy that has no onion or tomato in it.

Higher, and the rice runs out.

Ladakh is a different country in every way that matters to a kitchen. Leh stands at about 3,500 metres, the air is dry, the growing season is a few weeks long, and rice simply will not do it. Barley will, and barley is therefore the staple. Roasted barley flour is ngamphe, which Tibetans call tsampa, and it is not baked into anything. It is tipped into a bowl and worked into a stiff dough with the fingers, using tea as the liquid.

The tea is gur gur cha, and the name is the noise the churn makes. Strong tea is churned in a tall wooden cylinder with salt and yak butter until the fat emulsifies, and the result is closer to a thin soup than to a drink. At that altitude the salt and the fat are the point, not the flavour, and a visitor who expects something like tea has a bad first cup. Alongside it go thukpa, a noodle soup, and skyu, thumb-pressed pieces of dough boiled with root vegetables. Chilli barely appears. The food has turned Tibetan rather than Indian, and it did so because of the altitude and nothing else.

Between those extremes sit Garhwal and Kumaon, where the fields grow coarse grains such as mandua and jhangora, and the pulses are the hill ones: bhatt, a black soybean, and gahat, horse gram. Both are cooked into thick, dark, slow dishes that a plains kitchen has never heard of.

One rule holds it together.

Altitude writes the menu in this region more plainly than anywhere else in the course. Rice on the valley floor at 1,600 metres, wheat and coarse grain in the middle hills, barley at 3,500, and the fat in the diet rises as the grain gets poorer. What does not change is the shape of the meal: people sit on the floor, eat from a surface they share, and are served in an order everybody present already knows.
