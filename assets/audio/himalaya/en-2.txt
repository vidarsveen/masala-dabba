Rice, the wazwan and the seventh course.

Four men sit down on the floor around a copper platter the size of a cartwheel. Rice is heaped on it and divided into four by the meat laid across the top. A boy comes round with a jug and a basin, the tash-t-naer, so everyone can wash their right hand where they sit. Nobody is given a plate, because the platter is the plate. It is called a trami, and it feeds exactly four people.

The floor of the Kashmir valley lies at about 1,600 metres, ringed by mountains, with cold winters and a shorter growing season than the plains. Farmers have long selected rice varieties suited to those conditions. Many are short-grained and slightly sticky, unlike the long, separate basmati associated with the plains.

Plain rice forms the base of the valley's main meals, and gravies are served over it. One variety deserves its name here. Mushk budji is a small aromatic landrace grown in several higher parts of Kashmir. It became scarce as higher-yielding varieties spread, but farmers and agricultural researchers have worked to conserve and revive it. Its aroma and price reserve it especially for weddings and guests.

A wedding banquet in the valley is not cooked by the household. It is cooked by a waza, a professional cook from a hereditary trade, working with a team under a vasta waza, the head of it. They arrive the day before with their own copper vessels, dig fire pits in the yard, and butcher, pound and cook through the night.

The physical centre of the feast happens at that stage. Meat is laid on a flat stone and beaten with a wooden mallet until the fibres break down and the sinew can be removed. The resulting paste binds into a ball without egg or flour. Machines now do some of this, though wazas prize the texture produced by hand pounding. The sound of mallets through the night announces a wedding kitchen at work.

The number of courses is quoted as thirty-six so often that it has become the standard answer, and it should be treated as a figure of speech rather than a count. What is actually served depends on the household and on the money, and a modern wazwan is often shorter. The shape, though, does not change.

The trami arrives already loaded. Under the meat there is rice, and on it sit seekh kebabs, two pieces of tabak maaz, a piece of chicken cooked white and another cooked yellow with saffron, and a portion of methi maaz. Guests begin with those while the rest of the meal is still in the pots.

Then the courses come one at a time, and each is laid onto the same platter by a server working down the row. Rogan josh, aab gosht cooked in milk, daniwal korma green with coriander, marchwangan korma hot with chilli, and rista, the red meatball. Most guests lose count somewhere around the seventh, which is why the thirty-six is best left alone. The order is what carries the meaning, not the total.

The same pounded meat becomes both rista and gushtaba. Rista is the smaller ball, simmered in a red gravy coloured with Kashmiri chilli. Gushtaba is larger, paler and softer, and it is finished in a gravy of whisked yoghurt. It arrives last and signals that the formal sequence is ending.

All of this belongs to the Muslim kitchen of the valley. Kashmiri Pandit households hold their own feasts, with a different rule for onion and garlic, and those are not a wazwan.

Over the Pir Panjal in Himachal Pradesh, the ceremonial meal is called a dham, and its traditional form is vegetarian. Specialist cooks called botis prepare it in large metal vessels. Diners sit in rows, often eating from plates stitched from leaves, while servers bring the dishes in an established order that ends with something sweet.

Many of the dishes are tangy rather than chilli-hot. Madra is made with chickpeas or kidney beans in a yoghurt gravy scented with cardamom and clove. Its resemblance to Kashmiri yakhni reflects ingredients shared across neighbouring mountain foodways: dairy supplies both acidity and body when onion and tomato are absent.

Leh stands at about 3,500 metres in a cold, dry landscape with a short growing season. Barley tolerates those conditions better than rice and became the staple grain. Its roasted flour is called ngamphe, related to Tibetan tsampa. A diner can work it with tea into a firm dough in the bowl rather than bake it.

Gur gur cha takes its name from the churn used to blend strong tea with salt and butter into an emulsion. Alongside it appear thukpa, a noodle soup, and skyu, thumb-pressed pieces of dough cooked with vegetables. The ingredients reflect both the high-altitude environment and Ladakh's long cultural and trading ties with Tibet.

Between those extremes sit Garhwal and Kumaon, where farmers grow grains such as mandua and jhangora. The hills also have their own pulses: bhatt, a black soybean, and gahat, or horse gram. Both become substantial slow-cooked dishes well suited to the local crops and climate.

Elevation and climate narrow what farmers can grow, from rice in the Kashmir valley to millets in the middle hills and barley in Ladakh. Trade, religion and ceremony then give those crops different meanings. A trami, a leaf plate and a bowl of ngamphe are distinct meals, each organised by rules its diners already know.
