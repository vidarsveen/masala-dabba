Rogan josh, yakhni and the two valley kitchens.

Put two pots of rogan josh side by side in Srinagar and they will not smell alike. One was started by frying sliced shallot and garlic until it went dark. The other was never near an onion, and the cook reached instead for a pinch of asafoetida, a spoon of ground fennel and a spoon of dried ginger. Both are rogan josh, both are correct, and the households that cook them have lived a few streets apart for six hundred years.

The Kashmiri Pandit kitchen is the Hindu Brahmin one, and it breaks the rule an outsider expects. It eats meat without embarrassment and refuses onion and garlic entirely. Its flavour comes from ground fennel, dried ginger, asafoetida, whisked curd and Kashmiri chilli, and its gravies are therefore clean and a little sweet, with a sharp backbone from the curd.

The Kashmiri Muslim kitchen, the one the wazas cook in, uses onion, garlic and praan freely, browns them, and grinds them into the base of a gravy. Its dishes are often darker and rounder, and garlic-based ver can season them.

The consequence is the thing to hold on to. A dish name in this valley does not identify a recipe. Yakhni, rogan josh and dum aloo are all cooked both ways, and asking which is authentic is asking which family you are having dinner with.

The name is often explained through Persian: roghan means oil or clarified butter, while josh is linked to boiling or stewing. Other explanations connect Kashmiri words for red with meat or juice, and the exact etymology remains disputed. None of the main readings of the name simply means chilli-hot.

The dish can be red from Kashmiri chilli and colouring ingredients such as ratan jot or mawal, with fat rising to the surface as it cooks. Its heat varies by recipe, but the traditional colour does not require the searing chilli level found in some restaurant versions outside Kashmir.

The meat is goat or mutton on the bone, and the distinction matters when you shop. Mutton here means the meat of a grown sheep or, more often, a grown goat, and neither behaves like lamb. Both want an hour or more, and both give a gravy body that lamb cannot.

If rogan josh is the dish everybody has heard of, yakhni shows another side of the kitchen. Meat cooks gently with spices such as cardamom, cloves and fennel before whisked yoghurt finishes the gravy. Many versions omit chilli and turmeric and remain pale; family recipes differ.

One technique decides whether it works. Yoghurt splits when it is heated, so it is whisked smooth, poured in off a hard boil, and then stirred in one direction without stopping until the pot comes back to a simmer. Once it has boiled with the meat it is stable and can be left. Stop stirring too early and the gravy curdles into grains, and no amount of later cooking will bring it back together.

The Pandit version leans on fennel and dry ginger; the Muslim version adds praan and a little fried shallot. Nadru yakhni is the same gravy with lotus root instead of meat, and it is one of the best vegetarian dishes in the north of India.

Kashmiri dum aloo has almost nothing in common with the dish of the same name served in Delhi, which arrives in a sweet gravy of tomato, cream and cashew. Here, small potatoes are pricked all over with a fork and deep-fried in their skins until the outside blisters and the inside collapses slightly. They then go into a thin gravy of curd, chilli, fennel and dry ginger, and the pot is sealed and left on a very low flame. That sealing is the dum, and the holes are what let the gravy get inside the potato.

Haak shows the opposite kind of cooking and is a common emblem of home food. A loose-leaf collard is boiled in water with mustard oil, green chilli and, in Pandit versions, asafoetida. Nothing needs frying or grinding, and the cooking liquid, haakh ras, is served over rice.

The other valley vegetable is nadru, lotus root, cut from the beds of Dal Lake and Wular Lake in the cold months by men who work the mud with their feet. It is crisp, holed, and faintly sweet, and it holds its bite through a long simmer, which no other root vegetable in this course does. It goes into yakhni, it is cooked with praan and chilli, and it is sliced thin and fried into crisps.

Tabak maaz is made from ribs cut into flat pieces and cooked in two stages. First they simmer gently with spices until tender. Then they are drained and fried in ghee until the outside is crisp and golden. The dry pieces arrive on the trami before the gravies.

Everyday tea in the valley is not kahwa. It is noon chai, salt tea, and it is pink. Green tea leaves are boiled a long time with a little bicarbonate of soda, which drives the colour from green through brown to red, and then milk is added and the whole thing turns pink. It is salted rather than sweetened, drunk at breakfast and in the afternoon, and it is served with bread from the neighbourhood bakery.

Kahwa is the other cup, commonly offered to guests and after a feast. Green tea is brewed with cardamom and often cinnamon or saffron, lightly sweetened and served with almonds. A samovar can keep it hot over coals through a long gathering.
