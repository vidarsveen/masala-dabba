Saffron, ver and the chilli that colours.

In the last week of October the flat brown ground south of Srinagar turns purple overnight. The crocus comes up out of bare soil with no leaf in sight, and by seven in the morning whole families are down the rows, dropping flowers into baskets before the sun gets high. By noon the fields are brown again. The work has moved indoors, where the flowers are pulled apart one at a time and three red threads are lifted out of each one.

The karewa, and why saffron grows on it.

Pampore sits on a karewa, one of the flat-topped terraces of old lake silt that stand above the Jhelum on the floor of the valley. The soil there is deep and it drains, and drainage is what a saffron corm needs, because a corm left in wet ground rots. The climate then arrives in the order the plant wants it: a hot dry summer while it sleeps, a cool wet autumn to wake it, and a hard winter afterwards.

Crocus sativus flowers in autumn and cannot set seed, so every plant in the field is a clone lifted from a corm and replanted by hand. Each flower carries three bright red stigmas joined to a yellow style, and only the red part is the spice. The flowers are picked at dawn and stripped the same day, because a flower left overnight loses colour.

The arithmetic explains the price. A kilogram of dried saffron takes something on the order of a hundred and fifty thousand flowers, and a person picks, opens and strips every one of them. Nobody has found a way to do it that does not involve fingers.

How much of it is Kashmiri.

Very little, and it is worth being honest about that. Iran grows around nine tenths of the world's saffron, and Kashmir is a small producer beside it. The valley's own harvest has fallen a long way since the 1990s, because the planted area has shrunk, the autumn rains have been unreliable and parts of the karewa have been built over. Cheap saffron from elsewhere then arrives in Srinagar, gets sold as Kashmiri and undercuts the growers who are actually there. A geographical indication was granted in 2020, which is a legal answer to that rather than a solved problem.

A buyer has a simpler test. Real saffron gives up its colour slowly in warm water, over several minutes, and the thread keeps its shape. Anything that bleeds red at once and evenly has been dyed, and anything that looks like a loose orange fluff is safflower, which is a different plant at a different price.

What saffron actually does.

Three compounds do three separate jobs. Crocin gives the colour, picrocrocin gives a faint bitterness, and safranal gives the smell, which develops while the threads dry rather than while they grow. Two of those three are water-soluble and slow, so saffron has to be bloomed: a pinch is crushed, steeped in a spoonful of warm milk or water for a quarter of an hour, and the whole coloured liquid goes into the dish. Threads dropped dry into a pot do almost nothing.

Then the surprise. Saffron is not the everyday seasoning of the valley, and it is not what makes rogan josh red. It goes into kahwa, into modur pulav, the sweet rice with dried fruit that is the one dish built around it, and into a rich meat dish or two at a wedding. A Kashmiri household cooks with saffron a few times a year and sells the rest.

The chilli that colours rather than burns.

The red that people associate with this kitchen comes from somewhere else. The Kashmiri chilli is long, wrinkled, thin-fleshed and deep red, and it has been selected for colour rather than for heat. A cook can use it by the spoonful, get a gravy the colour of brick and leave nobody at the table sweating, which is the whole point of it.

Traditionally the chilli had help. Ratan jot is the dried root of a plant in the borage family, usually alkanet, and it is steeped in hot fat rather than in water. Its colour is fat-soluble, so it rises with the oil and sits on the surface of the dish as a red film. Some Kashmiri Pandit households use dried cockscomb flowers, mawal, for the same purpose. Neither adds a flavour worth naming, and both are there to do one job.

So the red is a decision rather than a warning, and reading three takes the name rogan josh apart to show that it never promised heat at all.

Ver: a spice mixture stored as a cake.

Nothing else in this course is kept the way ver is. Once a year, usually in high summer, a household grinds garlic and shallot with dried chilli, cumin, cloves, cardamom, black pepper and salt, works the paste with a little mustard oil and presses it into rings or thick discs. Those dry hard on the roof over several days and are then strung on a cord and hung in the kitchen. When a dish needs seasoning, the cook snaps a piece off a ring and crushes it into hot oil.

The form is the clever part. A ground spice mixture goes flat within weeks, because the aromatic oils sit exposed on every particle. Pressed into a solid cake with salt and oil, the same mixture keeps for a year and only begins to age when somebody breaks a piece off, so a kitchen with one grinding day a year gets a seasoning that is fresh at the point of use. Ver goes into fish, into haak, into lotus root and into dum aloo, and its base is garlic and shallot, which is one reason it belongs to the Muslim kitchen rather than the Pandit one.

Fennel and dry ginger, where the onion should be.

A Kashmiri Pandit kitchen cooks meat freely and uses no onion and no garlic at all. That is the reverse of what most people expect from a Brahmin kitchen, and it leaves a real hole, because onion and garlic are what give a gravy its sweetness and its background. Three things fill the hole, and two of them are on this region's spice list.

Saunf, ground fennel, is used by the spoonful rather than the pinch. It is sweet and faintly of aniseed, and it thickens as well as flavours, which is the job the onion would have done. Sonth is dried ginger ground to a pale powder, and it is not a stand-in for the fresh root. Drying changes the chemistry, so sonth is drier and hotter and does not taste of fresh ginger, and swapping one for the other in either direction gives you the wrong dish. Asafoetida completes the set. Bloomed in hot fat for a couple of seconds it turns from something appalling into a savoury smell very close to fried onion.

The sixth item is praan, the valley's own shallot, small and purple and growing in clusters. The Muslim kitchen slices it, fries it dark and grinds it into the base of a wazwan gravy, and it turns up whole beside knol-khol as monje praan. A Pandit kitchen will not touch it, which tells you how much of this cuisine is decided by whose kitchen you are standing in.

In the pantry: saffron (kong).

 Aroma: Hay and honey with a metallic edge, and it grows stronger as the threads dry and age a little.

 Flavour: Faintly bitter, faintly medicinal, and never sweet by itself.

 What it does: Colours a dish gold from within and perfumes it; it does not season it.

 When to add: Crushed and steeped in warm milk or water first, then poured in near the end.

 Instead of it: Nothing. Turmeric gives colour and a different taste, and safflower gives colour and nothing else.
