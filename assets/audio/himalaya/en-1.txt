Saffron, ver and the chilli that colours.

In the last week of October the flat brown ground south of Srinagar turns purple overnight. The crocus comes up out of bare soil with no leaf in sight, and by seven in the morning whole families are down the rows, dropping flowers into baskets before the sun gets high. By noon the fields are brown again. The work has moved indoors, where the flowers are pulled apart one at a time and three red threads are lifted out of each one.

Pampore sits on a karewa, one of the flat-topped terraces of old lake silt that stand above the Jhelum on the floor of the valley. The soil there is deep and it drains, and drainage is what a saffron corm needs, because a corm left in wet ground rots. The climate then arrives in the order the plant wants it: a hot dry summer while it sleeps, a cool wet autumn to wake it, and a hard winter afterwards.

Crocus sativus flowers in autumn and cannot set seed, so every plant in the field is a clone lifted from a corm and replanted by hand. Each flower carries three bright red stigmas joined to a yellow style, and only the red part is the spice. Growers pick the flowers in the morning and separate the stigmas promptly to protect their quality.

The labour helps explain the price. A kilogram of dried saffron takes roughly a hundred and fifty thousand flowers. Harvesting the flowers and separating their stigmas remain painstaking manual work.

Iran produces more than nine tenths of the world's saffron, so Kashmir is a small producer by comparison. When India's National Saffron Mission was announced in 2010, official figures showed Kashmir's planted area had fallen from about 5,700 to 3,800 hectares and yield from 3.13 to 2.50 kilograms per hectare. Unreliable rain, construction on the karewas and saffron sold under a false Kashmiri name continue to trouble growers. Kashmir saffron received a geographical indication in 2020, which protects the name but cannot by itself restore the crop.

Threads that release colour gradually in warm water while keeping their shape can raise a buyer's confidence, while loose orange fluff may be safflower. Neither observation proves origin or purity. For an expensive purchase, certified provenance is more useful than a kitchen test.

Three compounds have different jobs. Crocin gives the colour, picrocrocin gives a faint bitterness, and safranal supplies much of the aroma that develops as the threads dry. Crushing a pinch and steeping it in warm milk or water before adding the liquid extracts and distributes those qualities more evenly through a dish.

Saffron is a special-occasion ingredient in the valley, and it is not what makes rogan josh red. It perfumes kahwa, modur pulav with dried fruit, and selected wedding dishes rather than every pot on the stove.

The red that people associate with this kitchen comes from somewhere else. Kashmiri chillies are valued for their deep colour and comparatively mild heat, although varieties and batches differ. A brick-red gravy therefore does not tell you by itself how hot the dish will taste.

Traditionally the chilli had help. Ratan jot is the dried root of a plant in the borage family, usually alkanet, and it is steeped in hot fat rather than water. Its fat-soluble pigment rises with the oil as a red film. Some Kashmiri Pandit households use dried cockscomb flowers, mawal, for a similar effect. Both are used chiefly for colour, though they may contribute a subtle aroma.

Rogan josh makes the distinction clear: its red colour is part of the dish, but its name does not promise chilli heat.

Nothing else in this course is kept the way ver is. Once a year, usually in high summer, a household grinds garlic and shallot with dried chilli, cumin, cloves, cardamom, black pepper and salt. A little mustard oil binds the paste before it is pressed into rings or thick discs. Those dry hard for several days and are then strung on a cord and hung in the kitchen. When a dish needs seasoning, the cook snaps off a piece and crushes it into hot oil.

Drying the paste into a hard cake makes a large seasonal batch practical to store for months in dry, airtight conditions. The cook breaks off only what a dish needs. Ver seasons fish, haak, lotus root and dum aloo; versions based on garlic and shallot belong to Muslim cooking, while Pandit versions use asafoetida instead.

Kashmiri Pandit cooking includes meat but traditionally excludes onion and garlic. That removes two common sources of sweetness and depth from a gravy. Fennel, dry ginger and asafoetida help build the flavour in their place.

Ground fennel, or saunf, is used by the spoonful and brings sweetness, a light aniseed note and some body. Sonth is dried ginger ground to a pale powder; drying changes its aroma and pungency, so fresh ginger is not an exact substitute. Asafoetida completes the set. A brief bloom in hot fat softens its raw sulphurous smell into a savoury aroma.

Praan, the valley's small purple shallot, marks the other tradition. Muslim cooks slice it, fry it dark and grind it into the base of wazwan gravies, or cook it whole with knol-khol as monje praan. Pandit cooking generally excludes it, so the ingredient list often reveals which Kashmiri tradition a recipe follows.
