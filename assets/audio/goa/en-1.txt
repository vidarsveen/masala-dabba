Vinegar, chilli and the recheado masala.

Open a cupboard in a Catholic house in Salcete and two things will be standing at the front. One is a bottle of palm vinegar with a cloudy raft drifting in it. The other is a screw-top jar of dark red paste, thick as jam and smelling of garlic and scorched chilli. Neither of them is a garnish. The vinegar is what keeps meat edible in a climate that ruins it in a day, and the paste is most of a fortnight's dinners already made.

The chilli came ashore here.

This is the most important sentence in the module, so it is worth saying plainly. The chilli is American. Capsicum was domesticated in the Americas, Portuguese ships carried it east across the Atlantic from Brazil, and it entered India on this coast in the sixteenth century. Everything the rest of this course calls heat descends from that landing. Kashmiri chilli, Guntur chilli, the bhut jolokia of Nagaland, the Rajasthani mathania: all of them come from seed that arrived by sea, and all of them have been here for about five hundred years and no longer.

What did an Indian kitchen use before that? Black pepper, mostly, and long pepper, the small dried catkin the Sanskrit texts call pippali. Both are warming rather than burning, and both were expensive enough to be traded to Rome. The chilli was neither. It grew in any backyard, it dried without skill, and within two or three generations it had pushed long pepper almost out of the cooking pot altogether. The Portuguese physician Garcia da Orta, writing in Goa in 1563, already treats it as a common garden plant.

Goan cooks then did something the rest of India mostly did not. They kept choosing chillies for colour instead of for heat. The Kashmiri chilli in a Goan masala is there to turn the gravy that deep brick red, and it burns far less than its colour promises. A cook who wants more heat adds a hotter variety on purpose, in a measured quantity, rather than getting it by accident from the colouring chilli.

Vinegar, and the tree it comes from.

The second Portuguese idea took root even more completely. A tapper climbs a coconut palm before dawn, cuts the tip of the unopened flower spathe and hangs a pot under it. The sap that drips out is sweet for an hour or so and then starts to ferment on its own, because the wild yeasts are already on the tree. Left a few hours it is toddy, the mild palm wine. Left for weeks in a warm corner it goes past alcohol and turns to vinegar.

That vinegar does three jobs at once. It sours, which a wet coastal kitchen needs as much as a dry inland one. It preserves, because acid stops the bacteria that ruin pork in a Goan May. And it tenderises, which is why a tough cut can be cubed, drowned in vinegar and left overnight without turning to mush. Portugal brought the technique and the reason for it; the coconut palm supplied the raw material; and the result is a souring agent that belongs to nowhere else in India.

The Hindu kitchens of the same villages sour their food quite differently. They use kokum, the dried purple rind of Garcinia indica, and tamarind, and neither of them tastes remotely like vinegar. Kokum is clean, fruity and faintly astringent, and it stains whatever it touches a dusty pink. Kerala, one coast south, uses a cousin of the same fruit for exactly the same job, which tells you how old the habit is.

Two kitchens under one name.

Goan food is not one cuisine, and the distinction matters more here than almost anywhere in this course. The Saraswat or Hindu kitchen works with fresh coconut, kokum, tamarind, asafoetida and a great deal of jaggery, and it uses neither vinegar, nor beef, nor pork. The Goan Catholic kitchen uses all three, plus cured pork, and it builds on techniques that came off a Portuguese ship. Both buy the same mackerel from the same market in the same hour of the same morning, and then take it home and do entirely different things to it.

Neither is a watered-down version of the other. A reader who learns only the Catholic half will think Goa means pork and vinegar, and will miss the vegetable stews that most Goans actually eat most days.

Recheado: the wet paste.

Recheado is Portuguese for stuffed, and the word names both the paste and what you do with it. Dried Kashmiri chillies are soaked soft in palm vinegar, then ground with garlic, ginger, cumin, cloves, cinnamon, peppercorns, a little tamarind and a little sugar. The crucial rule is that no water goes in at any point. Vinegar is the only liquid, which is why a jar of it keeps in the fridge for months while the same paste made with water would spoil in a week.

The classic use is a whole mackerel. You slit it along the backbone, pack the cavity with paste and dust the outside with semolina. Then you shallow-fry it until the coating crisps and the vinegar in the stuffing cooks down to a sweet-sour jam against the bone. It takes eight minutes and it is the single best argument for keeping a jar of the stuff.

Xacuti: the dry one.

Xacuti works the opposite way round. Grated fresh coconut is dry-roasted in a heavy pan until it goes from white to the colour of strong tea. Whole spices roast alongside it: coriander seed, cumin, peppercorns, cloves, cinnamon, star anise, nutmeg, poppy seed and dried red chilli. The lot is then ground with just enough water to move the stone. The result is dark, gritty and intensely nutty, and it smells more of toasted coconut than of any one spice.

The spelling deserves a word. In Portuguese orthography an initial x is pronounced like the English sh, so the dish is roughly sha-KOO-tee, and you will also see it written chacuti. That one letter is a good reminder of how this food got written down: by Portuguese clerks, in a Konkani-speaking kitchen.

In the pantry: palm vinegar.

 Aroma: Sharp and slightly yeasty, with a coconut sweetness behind the acid.

 Flavour: Softer and rounder than wine vinegar, and much less aggressive than malt.

 What it does: Sours, preserves and tenderises at the same time, which is why one bottle runs the kitchen.

 When to add: Early, as the marinade or the grinding liquid, so it has hours to work on the meat.

 Instead of it: Cider vinegar cut with a little water is closest. Malt vinegar is wrong and tastes it.

What the pastes are really for.

Both masalas solve the same problem, which is that Goa is hot and wet and has no winter. Meat spoils, fish spoils faster, and a cook who could not preserve had to shop twice a day. So the coast learned to cure pork in vinegar and chilli, to hang sausages in the smoke above the hearth, and to cook a pot of something acid that would be better on Thursday than it was on Monday. The two pastes are the tools for that, and every famous Goan dish is one of them applied to an animal.
