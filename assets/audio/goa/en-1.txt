Vinegar, chilli and the recheado masala.

Open a cupboard in a Catholic home in Salcete and you may find palm vinegar beside a jar of dark red recheado paste. One supplies the clean sourness; the other combines chilli, garlic and spice for fish or meat. Together they show how imported and local ingredients became Goan pantry staples.

The chilli is American. Capsicum was domesticated in the Americas, and Portuguese maritime trade carried it into India in the sixteenth century. The precise first landing is harder to prove than the broad route, but Garcia da Orta's book printed in Goa in 1563 shows that chilli was already established on the west coast.

Indian cooks had long used black pepper and long pepper, the dried spike called pippali in Sanskrit sources. Chilli spread quickly because it grew well, dried readily and supplied both heat and colour. It joined rather than simply erased the older peppers, which remain in regional cooking and medicine.

Many Goan masalas separate colour from heat by combining a mild red chilli with a hotter variety. Kashmiri or other low-heat red chillies can deepen the colour, while the cook adjusts heat with a stronger chilli. The exact varieties depend on household and availability.

A tapper cuts the unopened flower spathe of a coconut palm and collects its sap. The sweet liquid begins fermenting quickly into toddy; with further controlled fermentation, acetic-acid bacteria turn the alcohol into vinegar. Production methods vary, and food vinegar should come from a reliable maker.

Palm vinegar supplies sourness and helps cured or cooked foods keep, but it does not make meat safe at room temperature. Marinades belong in the refrigerator. Acid can also change the surface texture of meat, while cooking provides the safety step.

Kokum, the dried rind of Garcinia indica, and tamarind provide other kinds of sourness. Kokum is fruity and astringent and can tint coconut dishes pink. Use crosses religious and household lines, even though vinegar is especially prominent in Goan Catholic cooking.

Goan food contains several overlapping traditions. Saraswat and other Hindu kitchens often emphasise coconut, kokum, tamarind, asafoetida, vegetables and fish, with practice varying by caste and household. Catholic cooking made pork, beef, vinegar, bread and Indo-Portuguese techniques especially visible. Both draw on the same coast, rice fields and markets.

Neither tradition is a reduced version of the other, and neither is uniform. Pork and vinegar are only part of Goa's table; fish, rice, pulses and vegetables cross many community boundaries.

Recheado comes from the Portuguese word for stuffed, and the name is used for both a masala and the fish filled with it. Dried red chillies are softened in vinegar and ground with garlic and a variable set of spices, tamarind and sugar. Many recipes avoid water for a concentrated paste. Refrigerate it promptly, use it within a few days or freeze portions; vinegar alone does not guarantee months of safe storage.

The classic use is a whole mackerel. You slit it along the backbone, pack the cavity with paste and dust the outside with semolina. Then you shallow-fry it until the coating crisps and the vinegar in the stuffing cooks down to a sweet-sour jam against the bone. It takes eight minutes and it is the single best argument for keeping a jar of the stuff.

Xacuti masala takes a different route. Coconut and a variable set of whole spices are roasted, then ground with enough liquid to make a paste. Coriander, cumin, pepper, cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg, poppy seed and dried chilli are common, but no single list defines every household version.

The spelling deserves a word. In Portuguese orthography an initial x is pronounced like the English sh, so the dish is roughly sha-KOO-tee, and you will also see it written chacuti. That one letter is a good reminder of how this food got written down: by Portuguese clerks, in a Konkani-speaking kitchen.

These masalas serve different purposes. Recheado gives fish or meat a sharp red stuffing or coating; roasted-coconut masalas give gravies depth and body. Vinegar, salt, drying and smoke also shaped Goan pork traditions, but modern food safety still requires reliable curing, refrigeration and thorough cooking.
