Vindaloo, xacuti and sorpotel.

Several dishes carry Goa's name around the world, often in forms far from the versions cooked there. Vindaloo begins with vinegar and garlic; xacuti with roasted coconut and spice; sorpotel with pork, offal and time. Their recipes vary, but their techniques remain distinct.

The name is traced to Portuguese vinha d'alhos, a wine-and-garlic marinade used for meat. In Goa, vinegar replaced wine and cooks added chilli and local spice combinations. The resulting dish is an adaptation rather than a word assembled from vind and Hindi aloo.

Potato is absent from many Goan vindaloo recipes but appears in others, so it is better treated as a variation than as a test of authenticity. Vinegar and garlic remain more useful identifiers than extreme chilli heat.

British curry-house vindaloo developed its own reputation as a very hot menu category. Goan versions commonly foreground acidity, garlic and spice, with heat varying by cook.

Like many stews, vindaloo can taste more integrated after a refrigerated rest. Cool it promptly, keep it cold and reheat it thoroughly rather than relying on vinegar for safety.

Xacuti commonly builds a thick gravy from carefully roasted coconut and spices. Chicken, goat, mushrooms or vegetables can simmer in the paste. The number of spices and the degree of roasting vary, and the coconut should brown deeply without burning.

Poppy seed may thicken the gravy, while small amounts of nutmeg or star anise add aromatic notes in some versions. No one ingredient list proves a single trade route or defines every xacuti.

Sorpotel is strongly associated with Christmas and other Catholic celebrations. Recipes combine small pieces of pork and offal with chilli, garlic, spice and vinegar; the balance and choice of liver, heart or blood vary by family.

Many cooks prepare it ahead so the flavours settle, then serve it with sannas. Safe preparation means cooling it quickly, refrigerating it, and reheating only what will be served until piping hot. A fixed four-day schedule is a household custom, not a definition.

Cafreal is the only green dish in the set. Coriander leaf, green chilli, garlic, ginger, peppercorns and a little cinnamon are ground with vinegar and lime into a bright paste. Chicken marinates in it for some hours and is then griddled rather than stewed. It comes to the table nearly dry, very green and very sharp.

The name is often connected to Portuguese cafre, derived from Arabic and now a serious racial slur in southern Africa. Accounts also connect the dish to Portuguese Africa, especially Mozambique. The exact culinary route is difficult to document, so the connection should be presented as an etymology and migration history rather than a single proven invention.

Balchão is a concentrated, pickle-like relish rather than one fixed curry category. Prawns or fish are cooked with chilli, aromatics, vinegar and sometimes sugar until little free liquid remains. Cool it promptly, refrigerate it in a clean container and follow the recipe's tested storage guidance.

The name probably arrived from further east. Macau and Malacca make a fermented shrimp paste called balichão, Portuguese ships ran between all three ports, and a preserved shrimp relish travelling along that route is the likeliest explanation. Some Goan recipes still add dried shrimp to the paste, which supports it.

Bebinca uses coconut milk, egg yolks, flour, sugar, ghee and nutmeg. A thin layer of batter is set and browned under top heat, brushed with ghee, then covered with the next layer. Layer counts vary; the repeated baking creates alternating dark and pale bands.

The finished cake is dense, rich and closely associated with Christmas, though it is sold and served beyond the festival.
