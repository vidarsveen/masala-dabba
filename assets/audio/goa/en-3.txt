Vindaloo, xacuti and sorpotel.

Four dishes carry Goa's name around the world, and three of them are widely misunderstood by the people ordering them. This reading is mostly a set of corrections, and the first one is the biggest.

Vindaloo is a vinegar dish.

The name comes from Portuguese. Carne de vinha d'alhos means meat in wine and garlic, and it was a way of keeping pork alive on a long voyage. Cube it, drown it in wine with a great deal of crushed garlic, and it will last. On this coast there was no wine, but there was palm vinegar, and vinegar does the job better anyway. Goan cooks kept the garlic, swapped the wine for vinegar, and added the things they had to hand, which were Kashmiri chilli, cumin, cloves, cinnamon and peppercorns.

So the word breaks into vin, from vinho, and alho, garlic. It does not break into vind and aloo. Aloo is potato in Hindi and Marathi, the resemblance is an accident, and a Goan vindaloo contains no potato at all. Some households add one now, and nobody is going to stop them, but it is a late arrival and not the dish.

The other correction is about heat. A vindaloo in Goa is sour first and hot second. It is thick, dark and almost black-red, it tastes of vinegar and garlic before anything else, and it is nowhere near the hottest thing on a Goan table. The restaurant vindaloo of Britain, ranked between the madras and the phaal, is a different dish that borrowed the name and kept only the chilli.

It is also better on the second day, for the same reason sorpotel is. The vinegar keeps working on the pork overnight, and a vindaloo cooked on Saturday for Sunday lunch is the way most Goan kitchens do it.

Xacuti.

Xacuti is the counterweight: no vinegar in it, dark from roasted coconut rather than from chilli, and complicated in a way the others are not. Fifteen or so spices are dry-roasted with grated coconut until the coconut is nearly black, and the resulting paste is deep brown and smells of nutmeg and toasted nut. Chicken is the usual meat, on the bone, and it simmers in the paste until the gravy is thick enough to sit on the spoon.

Two ingredients make it unmistakable. Poppy seed thickens the gravy and gives it body, and a whole grated nutmeg gives it that sweet, slightly medicinal top note. Star anise appears in many versions and is the one spice that tells you where the trade routes ran.

Sorpotel, and why it improves.

Sorpotel is the Christmas dish, and it is not a dish for beginners. Pork belly, pork liver and often heart are boiled first, then diced very small and fried in their own fat. The masala is chilli, cumin, cloves, cinnamon, garlic and ginger ground in palm vinegar, and the whole thing is cooked down until the fat rises to the surface in a red slick. Traditionally a little pig's blood goes in too, which is where the dish gets its Portuguese ancestor sarapatel and its dark colour.

The important thing about it is time. Sorpotel is made three or four days before it is eaten, and reheated once every day in between. Each reheating drives off a little water and pushes the vinegar further into the meat, and the fourth-day version is a different and better dish from the first-day one. A Goan household makes it on the twenty-first of December for the twenty-fifth, and eats it with sannas.

Cafreal, the green one.

Cafreal is the only green dish in the set. Coriander leaf, green chilli, garlic, ginger, peppercorns and a little cinnamon are ground with vinegar and lime into a bright paste. Chicken marinates in it for some hours and is then griddled rather than stewed. It comes to the table nearly dry, very green and very sharp.

The name has an uncomfortable history and it is better to say so than to skirt it. It comes through Portuguese from cafre, a word for Africans that derives from Arabic and has become a serious racial slur in southern Africa. The route it records is real: Goans served throughout Portuguese Africa as soldiers, clerks and cooks, and they brought this way of grilling marinated chicken back from Mozambique. The same ancestry runs to the peri-peri chicken of Portugal and South Africa, which is a cousin of this dish.

Balchao is a preserve.

Balchão is the one most often mislabelled on a menu. It is not a curry. It is a pickle, made to be kept and eaten a spoonful at a time beside rice or bread. Small prawns are cooked down in a paste of dried red chilli, garlic, ginger and a good deal of vinegar until almost no free liquid is left. The finished relish goes into a jar under a layer of oil, and it keeps for weeks in a fridge.

The name probably arrived from further east. Macau and Malacca make a fermented shrimp paste called balichão, Portuguese ships ran between all three ports, and a preserved shrimp relish travelling along that route is the likeliest explanation. Some Goan recipes still add dried shrimp to the paste, which supports it.

Bebinca.

The sweet is bebinca, and it is made of coconut milk, egg yolks, flour, sugar, ghee and nutmeg, which is to say of exactly the things a Goan Catholic kitchen has at Christmas. What makes it worth the trouble is the method. A thin layer of batter is poured into a tin and baked under heat from above until it sets and browns. Then ghee is brushed over it, another layer is poured on, and it goes back. Seven layers is the usual count and sixteen is the boast, and each one takes its own eight or ten minutes.

Cut across, it looks like a slab of plywood, pale and dark in alternating bands, and it is dense and very rich. A household makes one for Christmas and it is still being sliced thin at New Year.

End of this reading.
