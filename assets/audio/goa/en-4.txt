1510: what Portugal brought, and what it took.

On 25 November 1510 Afonso de Albuquerque captured the island city of Goa from forces of the Bijapur Sultanate on his second attempt that year. Portugal retained territory in Goa until December 1961, one of Asia's longest European colonial tenures. Food records exchange, adaptation and coercion across those centuries.

Goa became the capital of the Estado da Índia rather than only a fortified trading station. Its island position, river access and established horse market helped make Old Goa a naval, administrative and commercial centre linked to Portuguese possessions from East Africa to East Asia.

Old Goa expanded rapidly and early modern travellers compared it with Lisbon, though population estimates vary. Epidemics and changing river and administrative conditions contributed to its decline. The government shifted progressively to Panaji, officially making it the capital in 1843.

Colonial religious policy reshaped settlement and worship. Temples in the Old Conquests were destroyed or displaced, and communities moved deities into areas such as Ponda beyond early Portuguese control. Goa government history still identifies Ponda's temple concentration with that displacement.

The Goa Inquisition operated from 1560 to 1812, apart from a suspension between 1774 and 1778. It prosecuted heresy and enforced Christian orthodoxy among converts, while wider colonial rules restricted Hindu practice and Konkani. A 1684 decree sought to replace Konkani with Portuguese, although enforcement and outcomes varied.

These policies influenced community boundaries, but present Goan food cannot be divided into two sealed religious menus. Catholic, Hindu and Muslim cooks share ingredients and techniques while preserving distinct ritual and household practices.

Maritime exchange carried chilli, cashew and other American crops into South Asia. Goa became one important place where these crops and new techniques were adapted to local conditions.

Portuguese networks brought cashew from tropical America. Its nut became a crop, while fermented cashew-apple juice could be distilled into feni. Production methods and the number of distillations vary. Cashew feni has a protected Indian geographical indication registered to Goa.

Portuguese-era baking also left a strong bread tradition. Goan bakers, known as poders, developed breads including bran-coated poee, crusty undo and pull-apart forms of pão. Toddy was a traditional leaven; commercial yeast is common today. Marathi and Hindi pav is commonly linked to Portuguese pão, though a word history alone does not explain Mumbai's later dishes.

Portuguese writers rendered South Indian words related to kari as caril. European use of that form contributed to the later English word curry, whose broad colonial meaning came to cover many unrelated dishes.

Culinary exchange did not balance the violence of conquest. Portuguese rule extracted trade and political control, displaced temples, constrained language and religion, and created hierarchies that shaped who could live and cook openly. Goan adaptations such as vindaloo later travelled widely, but popularity does not erase that history.

The end came quickly. India had been negotiating since 1947 and getting nowhere, Portugal insisted Goa was not a colony but a province of Portugal, and on 18 December 1961 the Indian army moved in. It was over in about thirty-six hours. Portugal refused to recognise the transfer until 1974, when its own dictatorship fell. Goa became a full Indian state in 1987, with Konkani as its official language, three centuries after a viceroy had tried to abolish it.

Goan migrants worked in Bombay, on steamships, in East Africa and in the Gulf as cooks, stewards, clerks and musicians. Their movement carried breads, meat dishes and kitchen skills beyond Goa and made the cuisine part of a wider Indian Ocean history.
