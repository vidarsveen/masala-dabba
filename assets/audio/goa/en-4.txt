1510: what Portugal brought, and what it took.

On 25 November 1510 Afonso de Albuquerque took the island city of Goa from the Sultan of Bijapur. It was his second attempt that year; he had taken it in March and been driven out in May. This time he kept it, and Portugal held some part of Goa without a break until December 1961. Four hundred and fifty-one years is longer than any European power held any territory in Asia, and the kitchen shows it.

Why a city and not a warehouse.

Portugal's other Indian holdings were trading posts. Goa was different because Albuquerque wanted a capital, and the site gave him one. It was an island between two tidal rivers, defensible, with deep water for a fleet and a standing market for the war horses coming from Hormuz. Old Goa became the seat of the Estado da Índia, the government of everything Portuguese from Mozambique to Macau.

It grew fast and enormously. By the end of the sixteenth century travellers were comparing it to Lisbon, and estimates of its population run to well over a hundred thousand. It was called the Rome of the East, mostly for the churches. Then cholera and malaria and the silting of the Mandovi emptied it, the capital moved down to Panaji in the 1840s, and the site today is a handful of very large churches standing in scrub.

The Inquisition, and what conversion did to the food.

This is the part a tourist brochure leaves out. Temples in the territory Portugal held first, the Old Conquests around Bardez, Tiswadi and Salcete, were demolished from the 1540s onwards. Villagers carried their deities across the river into Ponda, which was then outside Portuguese control, and rebuilt there. The Mangeshi, Shanta Durga and Mahalsa temples that Goan Hindus worship in today stand where the refugees put them, not where they began.

The Goa Inquisition was established in 1560 and ran, with one short interruption, until 1812. Its formal target was heresy among converts rather than Hinduism itself. In practice it policed how a converted household lived, and that reached the kitchen: cooking with certain oils, refusing to eat pork, keeping a fast on the wrong day. A decree of 1684 went further and ordered Konkani abandoned in favour of Portuguese. So when this module says that one half of a Goan village eats pork and the other half does not, it is not describing a difference of taste.

The result is a state where roughly a quarter of the population is Catholic, and where churches and temples stand within sight of each other. Two full culinary traditions grew up side by side out of the same market and the same fish.

What the ships brought.

The chilli is the famous one, and reading one dealt with it. But the same trade brought a great deal else, and Goa is where much of it was planted first.

The cashew is the clearest case. Portuguese ships brought it from Brazil in the sixteenth century, and it was planted on Goa's bare laterite slopes to hold the soil together, which it does very well. The nut was the point for export. The fleshy false fruit it hangs from, the cashew apple, rots within a day and could not be sold, so Goa did the obvious thing and fermented it. Crushed underfoot in a stone trough, left to ferment, then distilled twice, it becomes feni. Goa has held a protected geographical indication for it since 2009, and it is one of very few Indian spirits with one.

Bread is the other lasting import, and it changed more than Goa. Portuguese bakers taught the technique, Goan bakers took it over, and the leavening was local: toddy, again, rather than brewer's yeast. The baker is the poder, he still comes round twice a day, and he still announces himself by squeezing a rubber horn on the handlebars. What he sells are poee, a flat hollow wheat-bran bun, undo, a hard round roll, and katre pão, the four-cornered one that pulls apart. And the Portuguese word pão travelled inland into Marathi and Hindi as pav, which is why Mumbai eats vada pav and pav bhaji today.

Portugal also gave English a word it uses constantly. Traders on the Malabar coast wrote the Tamil and Kannada kari down as caril, the Portuguese form travelled into Dutch and English, and it is the reason a European says curry at all.

What it took.

Set against that, the traffic out of the Goan kitchen was almost nothing. Portugal took pepper and it took profit. It did not take Goan cooking home in any serious way, and there is no Goan dish on a Lisbon menu the way there is an Indian one on a London menu. What it took instead was four and a half centuries of self-government, several hundred temples, and the right of a large part of the population to live as it had lived.

The end came quickly. India had been negotiating since 1947 and getting nowhere, Portugal insisted Goa was not a colony but a province of Portugal, and on 18 December 1961 the Indian army moved in. It was over in about thirty-six hours. Portugal refused to recognise the transfer until 1974, when its own dictatorship fell. Goa became a full Indian state in 1987, with Konkani as its official language, three centuries after a viceroy had tried to abolish it.

One more thing left with the diaspora. Goans went out as cooks, stewards and bandsmen to Bombay, to the British India Steam Navigation ships, to East Africa and the Gulf, and Goan cooking travelled with them. The Anglo-Indian club kitchen, the ship's galley and the Bombay Irani restaurant all carry Goan fingerprints, which is a large footprint for a state of a million and a half people.
