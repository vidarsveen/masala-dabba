Ukda rice and the daily fish curry.

Ask a Goan what the food of Goa is and you will very rarely hear the word vindaloo. You will hear xit kodi, which means rice and curry, and it arrives at about one o'clock in a house or a canteen or a bus-stand eating house, and it looks the same in all three. A mound of fat reddish rice. A bowl of thin orange gravy. A fried fish laid across the rim. A vegetable, a pickle, and a small glass of something pink.

Ukda rice, and why the coast parboils it.

The rice is ukda, which simply means par-boiled. The paddy is soaked, steamed while it is still in the husk, dried in the sun and only then milled. Kerala does the same thing to its matta rice for the same reasons, and the reasons are worth repeating once because they are not obvious. Steaming in the husk drives nutrients inwards from the bran, so the grain keeps what polishing would strip. It hardens the starch, so a sack survives a monsoon without going mouldy or dusty. And it stops the grain collapsing when it is drowned in a thin gravy, which a Goan grain is going to be, twice a day, for life.

Goa grows its rice in an unusual place. Along the Mandovi and the Zuari the fields lie below the level of the tide, behind earth embankments called khazans with wooden sluice gates in them. The gates open on the ebb to drain the field and shut on the flood to keep the salt out, and the system has been maintained by village associations for several centuries. The rice bred for that ground, korgut, tolerates salt that would kill an ordinary variety, and it is coarse, red and exactly the texture the curry needs.

Xit kodi: the curry itself.

The everyday curry is ground fresh, and that is the whole of its character. Grated coconut goes on the stone with dried Kashmiri chillies, coriander seed, turmeric, a few peppercorns, garlic and a knob of tamarind, and the lot is ground wet into a smooth orange paste. The paste is loosened with water, brought just to a simmer with onion and green chilli, and the fish goes in for the last five minutes. Nothing browns and nothing fries. It takes twenty minutes from stone to table.

There is no vinegar in it. This is the Hindu and the Catholic kitchen agreeing, more or less, on one dish, and it is soured with kokum or tamarind in both houses. Which of the two you use is a household habit rather than a rule, though kokum is commoner south of Panaji and it keeps the gravy a cleaner orange.

The fish varies with what came ashore. Mackerel, bangda, is the everyday one and is often fried separately rather than cooked in the gravy, so the plate carries both a wet fish and a crisp one. Kingfish is the Sunday version, prawns the expensive one, and dried fish takes over in the monsoon when the boats stay in.

The pink glass at the end.

Solkadhi closes the meal. Dried kokum rinds are steeped in warm water until the water turns the colour of watermelon, and that liquid is mixed with thin coconut milk, a little crushed garlic, salt and coriander. It is drunk from a small glass, or poured over the last of the rice, and the colour is entirely the fruit. Nothing is added to make it pink.

It is not a decoration. A Goan lunch is a lot of coconut fat and a lot of chilli, and solkadhi is acid and cool and cuts straight through both. People here will tell you it settles the stomach, and whether or not that is pharmacology, it is certainly what it feels like.

The vegetarian half of the state.

Most Goans eat vegetables most days, and the Saraswat kitchen is where those dishes live. Khatkhate is the one to know: eight or nine vegetables, plus a pulse, simmered in a ground coconut masala and finished with triphal, a little spiky husk that numbs the tongue faintly, the way Sichuan pepper does. Tondak is a thicker dish of sprouted beans and coconut. Tambdi bhaji is red amaranth wilted with garlic, and it takes four minutes.

One point of ordinary confusion is worth clearing up. Many Goud Saraswat Brahmin families eat fish, which surprises people who expect Brahmin cooking to be strictly vegetarian. They class fish apart from meat, the coast has always been poor in other protein, and the practice is old and unembarrassed. Pork and beef, however, stay out of those houses entirely.

Bread, and the steamed cake that goes with pork.

Sannas are the local steamed rice cake, and they are the direct cousin of Kerala's appam. Rice is soaked and ground with a little coconut, and the batter is raised overnight with fresh toddy, because toddy is already fermenting when it comes out of the tree. The cakes are steamed in small rings, they come out white, spongy and slightly sweet, and they exist mainly to be eaten with sorpotel. Most kitchens now use yeast, and something is lost: toddy leaves a faint sourness that yeast does not.
