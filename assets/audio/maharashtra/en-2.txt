Bhakri on the plateau, rice on the coast.

On the plateau, a cook pats warm sorghum dough into a broad disc with a wet palm. Without gluten, the dough cracks more easily than wheat dough and rewards gentle handling. West of the Ghats, rice becomes more prominent, though neither staple stops neatly at the mountain line.

The Western Ghats intercept the southwest monsoon. Much of the Konkan is very wet, while interior districts lie in a rain shadow and face greater rainfall variability. This pattern influences crops without dictating every household meal.

Basalt-derived black soils cover large parts of the plateau and can retain moisture. Cotton, sorghum and pearl millet are among the crops grown there. In Marathi culinary language, sorghum is jowar and pearl millet is bajra.

For one common bhakri method, flour is mixed with hot water, patted or pressed flat and cooked on a dry griddle. Some cooks finish it directly over the flame so steam inflates parts of the bread. Other flours and methods produce different textures.

Jowar bhakri is pale and mild. Bajra bhakri is greyer and more robust and is especially associated with cooler weather. Both are best fresh because they firm as they cool.

Bhakri may be served with pithla, gram flour cooked with water and a tempering into a soft paste or sauce. Thecha pounds green chilli and garlic with salt and sometimes peanut. Onion, chutney or jaggery can complete a simple meal.

Maharashtra grows a great deal of sugarcane, most of it in the belt around Kolhapur and Sangli, and the co-operative sugar factories there have shaped the politics of the state as much as its cooking. What reaches the kitchen is jaggery, the whole unrefined cane sugar boiled down and set in blocks.

Some amti recipes add a little jaggery to balance chilli and souring ingredients. The amount varies, and many dals omit it. Jaggery also appears in chutneys, stuffed vegetables and, in much larger proportion, puran poli.

Puran poli is associated with festivals including Holi and Gudi Padwa, as well as family celebrations. Chana dal is cooked with jaggery and aromatics into a smooth filling called puran, enclosed in wheat dough, rolled and cooked on a griddle. It may be served with ghee.

The dal cooking liquid can become katachi amti, seasoned with spices and sour and sweet elements, then served beside the filled bread. Recipes differ, but the pairing turns a cooking liquid into a second dish.

On the Konkan side, rice, coconut, mango, cashew and seafood become more prominent. Farming and fishing differ along the long coast, and inland ingredients continue to travel down the passes.

Malvani cooking around Malvan in Sindhudurg uses coconut, chilli, coriander and varied spice mixtures. Tirphal is the dried fruit of a Zanthoxylum, related to Sichuan pepper. Its citrus aroma and mild numbing quality suit some fish curries.

A Malvani meal may pair rice with coconut-and-kokum fish curry, fried fish and solkadhi. With chicken or goat, vade made from mixed flours are one possible bread. Bhakri is also eaten in coastal Maharashtra, so the contrast is one of emphasis rather than a border.

One fish on that coast has a name that needs correcting. Bombay duck is a fish. It is Harpadon nehereus, called bombil in Marathi, a soft translucent lizardfish with almost no structure to it, and how the English name arose is not settled. Some accounts derive it from bombil itself and some from the Bombay Dak, the mail train that carried the dried fish inland with a smell that travelled.

Fresh bombil can be coated and shallow-fried until crisp outside and soft within. Dried bombil is firmer and much more pungent, used in chutneys or cooked with onion. The popular story connecting its English name to the Bombay mail train is only one disputed etymology.

Festive and restaurant thalis may arrange chutneys, salads, vegetables, breads and rice in recognisable groups, but placement and sequence vary. Varan-bhat, plain dal with rice and often ghee, is a widely loved comfort combination without being the mandatory close to every meal.
