Bhakri on the plateau, rice on the coast.

A woman on the plateau makes bread by hand in the most literal sense. She wets her palm, drops a ball of sorghum dough onto a wooden board, and turns and presses it outwards with the heel of her hand until it is a disc the width of a dinner plate. She cannot roll it, because there is no gluten in the flour to hold it together. Sixty kilometres west, over the mountains, nobody is doing this at all. They are eating rice.

The mountains take the rain.

The Western Ghats run north to south down the state a little inland of the sea, and they wring the monsoon out on their western side. Below them the Konkan strip gets two and a half to three metres of rain a year. Above and behind them the Deccan plateau sits in the rain shadow and parts of Marathwada get well under seven hundred millimetres, which is not enough for rice and not reliably enough for wheat.

What that dry ground does have is soil. The plateau is built on old basalt lava flows, and the black cotton soil they weather into holds water like a sponge. It grows cotton, which matters in reading four, and it grows the two millets this reading is about: jowar, which is sorghum, and bajra, pearl millet.

Bhakri, and what goes with it.

Bhakri is the bread of that country, and the absence of gluten governs everything about it. The flour is mixed with hot water into a stiff dough, patted flat by hand, and cooked on a dry griddle. Then comes the part that makes it work: the cook lifts it off the griddle and lays it straight on the flame, where the trapped steam blows it up like a balloon for a few seconds. That puff is the only leavening it gets.

Jowar bhakri is pale, almost white, and tastes faintly of hay. Bajra bhakri is grey-brown, heavier, sweeter and noticeably oilier, and it belongs to winter, when a household that can afford the choice switches over to it. Neither keeps well. A bhakri is eaten within the hour or it turns to board.

What arrives beside it is plain and extremely good. Pithla is gram flour whisked into salted water with onion, green chilli and a phodni, cooked for four minutes into something between a porridge and a sauce. Thecha is green chillies and garlic pounded in a stone mortar with roasted peanuts and salt, and a teaspoon of it is enough. Add a raw onion cracked open with a fist and a lump of jaggery, and that is a field lunch that has fed this plateau for centuries.

Jaggery in the dal.

Maharashtra grows a great deal of sugarcane, most of it in the belt around Kolhapur and Sangli, and the co-operative sugar factories there have shaped the politics of the state as much as its cooking. What reaches the kitchen is jaggery, the whole unrefined cane sugar boiled down and set in blocks.

A piece of it goes into the dal. That surprises people, and it is not a sweet dal: the jaggery is there in the same quantity you would use salt, and its job is to round off the chilli and the sourness so that neither one stands alone. Once you have eaten an amti made properly, a version without it tastes unfinished. The same instinct puts jaggery into the tamarind chutney, into the stuffing of stuffed aubergines, and into puran poli by the kilogram.

Puran poli, the bread that is a sweet.

Puran poli is what a Maharashtrian household makes for Holi and for Gudi Padwa, the Marathi new year. Chana dal is boiled soft, drained, cooked down with jaggery and cardamom and nutmeg, and then pushed through a mill until it is a smooth paste called puran. A ball of that is sealed inside a disc of soft wheat dough and rolled out flat, which is harder than it sounds, because the filling wants to tear through. It is cooked on a griddle and served with a pool of ghee.

The part worth stealing is what happens to the water the dal was boiled in. It is not thrown away. It is seasoned with goda masala, tamarind, jaggery and a phodni and served as katachi amti, a thin, sharp, dark soup that goes alongside the sweet bread. A festival dish and its by-product, eaten off the same plate, and nothing wasted.

Down the ghat: rice, coconut and fish.

Now cross the mountains. The Konkan is a strip thirty to sixty kilometres wide between the Ghats and the Arabian Sea, and it is a different country. Rice grows in terraces and paddies, coconut grows along every road, and the sea supplies the protein. Mango and cashew grow here too, and the Alphonso mangoes of Ratnagiri and Devgad are the most expensive fruit in India.

The best-known cooking of the far south of that strip is Malvani, from around Malvan in Sindhudurg. Its blend is chilli and coriander and a long list of warm spices, and one ingredient that turns up almost nowhere else in this course: tirphal, the dried berry of a Zanthoxylum, a relative of Sichuan pepper. It is faintly numbing and strongly citrus, and it goes into fish curry to cut the oiliness of mackerel.

A Malvani meal puts rice at the centre, a thin coconut-and-kokum fish curry over it, a fried fish beside it and a glass of solkadhi at the end. With chicken or mutton the bread is vade, small puffed rounds fried from a flour of rice, urad and chana. Bhakri belongs up the hill; down here the bread is fried and made of rice.

Bombil, which is not a duck.

One fish on that coast has a name that needs correcting. Bombay duck is a fish. It is Harpadon nehereus, called bombil in Marathi, a soft translucent lizardfish with almost no structure to it, and how the English name arose is not settled. Some accounts derive it from bombil itself and some from the Bombay Dak, the mail train that carried the dried fish inland with a smell that travelled.

Fresh bombil is coated in semolina and shallow-fried until the outside is crisp and the inside collapses like custard, which is the dish this course lists. Dried bombil is another thing entirely: hung on racks in the sun until it is stiff and reeking, then broken into a chutney or fried hard with onion. Plenty of coastal households love it. Plenty of their neighbours will not have it in the building.

How a plate is put together.

A full Maharashtrian thali reads clockwise and has an order in time as well as in space. The salads and chutneys sit on the left, the cooked vegetables to the right of them, the bread at the top and a mound of rice at the bottom. You eat the bread first with the vegetables and the dal, and the rice comes last, with varan poured over it, a spoonful of ghee, a squeeze of lime and a pinch of salt. Plain dal, rice and ghee to close a meal is the comfort dish of the entire state, and it is what a Maharashtrian eats when they are ill or homesick.
