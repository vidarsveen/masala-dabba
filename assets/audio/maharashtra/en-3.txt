Misal, vada pav and the Kolhapuri red.

A plate of misal arrives half built. A bowl of dark bean curry carries fried savoury bits, with raw onion, lime and two soft rolls alongside. A small jug holds thin red liquid that nobody has poured yet. Pouring it is your job, and the amount decides what kind of morning you are going to have.

The base is usal, often made with sprouted matki, or moth bean. The soaked and drained beans are kept moist until short sprouts emerge, then cooked tender with a regional masala. Onion, ginger or garlic depend on the recipe.

On top goes farsan, the catch-all word for fried savoury snacks: crushed chivda, sev, fried gram, whatever the shop had. Then raw onion, coriander and a wedge of lime. The whole point of the assembly is that the crunchy layer stays crunchy until it meets the wet layer, so it is built at the last second.

The liquid may be called rassa, kat or tarri. Some versions draw seasoned liquid and oil from the usal; others prepare a separate, thin chilli gravy. Heat and sweetness vary across Kolhapur, Pune, Nashik and individual shops. Pav is a common companion, useful for gathering the layers and tempering the chilli.

A batata vada is a ball of boiled potato mashed with a phodni of mustard seed, turmeric, green chilli, ginger and garlic, rolled, dipped in gram-flour batter and deep fried. On its own it is a snack. Split a pav, smear the cut faces with dry garlic chutney, push the vada in and hand it over with a fried green chilli, and it becomes the thing a whole city eats standing up.

The word pav is commonly linked to Portuguese pão, and Portuguese-era baking helped establish leavened bread on the west coast. Mumbai's joined tray rolls became a cheap, soft vehicle for many dishes.

A widely repeated origin story credits Ashok Vaidya near Dadar station in the late 1960s or early 1970s, but contemporary documentation is thin and other vendors make claims. The secure point is the food's fit with Mumbai: inexpensive, portable and easy to eat near stations and workplaces.

The other dish built on the same bread starts with mixed vegetables such as potato, cauliflower, peas, capsicum and tomato. A cook mashes them on a griddle while working in butter and pav bhaji masala. The pav is split, browned cut-side down in butter, and served with raw onion and lime.

One popular story links pav bhaji to textile workers who needed a fast meal, while another emphasises vendors using available vegetables. Neither can be tied securely to one inventor or date. The griddle method does make the dish quick to cook, portion and eat.

Poha is parboiled rice flattened and dried. The flakes rehydrate quickly, but their thickness determines whether they need rinsing, soaking or brief steaming. For kanda poha they are combined with tempered onion, turmeric, chilli and often peanut, then heated through and finished with lime and coriander.

Get the moisture wrong and you will know at once: too little and the flakes stay hard in the middle, too much and the dish turns to paste. Kanda simply means onion. The phrase also has a second life, because kande pohe is shorthand for a meeting where two families consider a marriage. The everyday food gave its name to this domestic occasion.

Kolhapur in southern Maharashtra is especially known for robust meat dishes. On many Indian menus, mutton means goat rather than mature sheep. A cooked meat stock can underpin the gravies served with the meal.

Pandhra rassa is pale from coconut and other seeds or nuts, with ginger, garlic and pepper in many recipes. Tambda rassa is red from chilli and masala. Restaurants often serve both beside the meat and bhakri, but recipes, heat and whether they share one stock vary.

Ganesh Chaturthi is one of Maharashtra's largest festivals. Maharashtra Tourism dates Bal Gangadhar Tilak's expansion of the public celebration to 1893, when collective worship also created civic and nationalist space.

Modak is a characteristic offering. A rice-flour wrapper encloses coconut cooked with jaggery and cardamom, then is pleated into a pointed shape and steamed. Some observances offer twenty-one modak; that number describes the offering, not a required count of folds on each dumpling.
