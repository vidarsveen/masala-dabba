Misal, vada pav and the Kolhapuri red.

A plate of misal arrives half built. There is a bowl of dark bean curry with a heap of fried savoury bits on top, a saucer of raw onion and a lime, two soft bread rolls, and a small jug of thin red liquid that nobody has poured yet. Pouring it is your job, and how much you pour decides what kind of morning you are going to have.

Misal, from the bottom up.

The base is usal: matki, the moth bean, soaked overnight, drained, tied in a cloth and left for a day until each bean pushes out a short white sprout. Sprouting softens the bean, shortens the cooking and makes it easier to digest, which matters in a dish eaten at eight in the morning. The sprouts are then cooked with onion, ginger, garlic and goda or kala masala into a thick brown curry.

On top goes farsan, the catch-all word for fried savoury snacks: crushed chivda, sev, fried gram, whatever the shop had. Then raw onion, coriander and a wedge of lime. The whole point of the assembly is that the crunchy layer stays crunchy until it meets the wet layer, so it is built at the last second.

The red liquid is rassa, also called kat or tarri, and it is skimmed off the top of the usal pot where the chilli oil has risen. It is thin, it is fiery and it floats a visible film of red. In Kolhapur it is served generously and expected to hurt; in Pune the usal itself is sweeter and the rassa gentler; in Nashik the tarri is the whole reputation of the town. The pav beside it is not optional, because bread is the only thing on the table that will put the fire out.

Vada pav.

A batata vada is a ball of boiled potato mashed with a phodni of mustard seed, turmeric, green chilli, ginger and garlic, rolled, dipped in gram-flour batter and deep fried. On its own it is a snack. Split a pav, smear the cut faces with dry garlic chutney, push the vada in and hand it over with a fried green chilli, and it becomes the thing a whole city eats standing up.

The pav is Portuguese by descent. Goa's module covers where that bread came from and who baked it; what happened in Bombay is the next chapter of the same story, because the city turned a colonial loaf into an industrial one. Pav is baked in a tray so the rolls fuse together in a sheet, sold by the dozen and pulled apart by hand, which is why the sides are pale and soft and only the top is crusted.

The dish is young. Most accounts date it to Bombay in the nineteen sixties or seventies and credit Ashok Vaidya, who is said to have sold the first one from a stall outside Dadar station, across the road from a mill gate. That is a credited story rather than a documented fact, and other stalls have claimed it since. What is not in doubt is the setting: it appeared beside the mills and the stations, it cost a few paise, and it needed no plate, no cutlery and no sitting down. Reading four is about why a city wanted exactly that.

Pav bhaji.

The other dish built on the same bread takes mixed vegetables — potato, cauliflower, peas, capsicum, tomato — and mashes them flat on a griddle with a blunt masher while a cook works butter and pav bhaji masala into them. The pav is split, laid face down in the same butter until it browns, and served with raw onion and lime.

The story told about it is that it fed the textile mills, either as a fast lunch during a short break or as a way of using up the day's vegetables. That story cannot be pinned to a date or a person, so treat it as tradition rather than record. What the dish plainly is, though, is mill food in design: one griddle, one pan, everything mashed together, ready in three minutes and eaten with the hands.

Kanda poha.

Poha is rice that has been parboiled, flattened between rollers and dried, so it needs no cooking at all — only moisture. That is the whole trick of the dish. The flakes are rinsed in a sieve and left for five minutes while onion is fried with mustard seed, turmeric, curry leaf, green chilli and peanuts, and then folded in and warmed through for two minutes. Lime, coriander and fresh coconut finish it.

Get the moisture wrong and you will know at once: too little and the flakes stay hard in the middle, too much and the dish turns to paste. Kanda simply means onion. The phrase also has a second life, because kande pohe is the shorthand for the meeting where two families look each other over with a marriage in mind. The food gave its name to the occasion, which tells you how ordinary and how domestic it is.

Kolhapur, and the two gravies.

Kolhapur, in the south of the state, is where Maharashtrian meat cooking is at its most serious. Mutton here means goat, as it does across this whole course. The meat is simmered with whole spices until tender, and the stock is kept, because the stock is the point.

That stock becomes two entirely different things. Pandhra rassa, the white one, is the stock with ground coconut, ginger, garlic and pepper, thin enough to drink and containing no chilli at all. Tambda rassa, the red one, is the same stock with kala masala and Bedgi and Lavangi chillies, equally thin and genuinely punishing. Both come to the table at once, in small steel bowls, beside the meat itself and a bhakri.

Alternating between them is the meal. A mouthful of red, then a mouthful of white to put it out, then bread, then red again. Two gravies from one pot, doing opposite jobs, is a better piece of design than any single sauce could manage, and it is the best argument in this reading for taking Kolhapur seriously.

Modak, and the god they are made for.

Ganesh Chaturthi is Maharashtra's festival above all others, ten days of it, with clay Ganpatis in every street and a procession to the sea at the end. Bal Gangadhar Tilak turned what had been a household observance into a large public one from 1893 in Pune, partly because a religious gathering could assemble where a political one could not.

The offering is modak. Rice flour is scalded with boiling water and kneaded hot into a dough soft enough to press out into a thin cup on the palm. The cup is filled with coconut cooked down with jaggery and cardamom, and the rim is pleated closed into a point, traditionally with twenty-one folds. They are steamed until the shell turns translucent and eaten warm with ghee. Twenty-one of them go to the god, which is a great deal of pleating, and the household eats what is left.
