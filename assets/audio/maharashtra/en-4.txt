Bombay: mills, Iranis and the food of arrival.

Mumbai has no indigenous cuisine to speak of, and that is the interesting thing about it. The islands it stands on were fishing grounds belonging to the Koli, and everything else on the plate arrived by boat or by train with somebody who was looking for work. A city assembled out of arrivals eats what arrivals eat: fast, cheap, portable, and borrowed from whoever got there first.

Seven islands and a dowry.

Bombay began as seven islands, separated at high tide and joined by mud at low. Portugal held them from the early sixteenth century and handed them to England in 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza on her marriage to Charles II. The Crown found them expensive and leased them to the East India Company in 1668 for ten pounds a year. Reclamation over the next two hundred years welded the islands into one, so the modern city sits on a shape that did not exist when it was named.

Cotton, and the village of mills.

The thing that made the city was cotton. The black soil of the Deccan grows it, the railway could bring it down, and the harbour could ship it out. The first steam-powered cotton mill was set up in 1854 by Cowasjee Nanabhai Davar, a Parsi merchant, and was spinning by 1856. When the American Civil War cut off supplies from the southern states between 1861 and 1865, Lancashire turned to India, and Bombay had a boom that built half its public architecture.

The mills clustered in Parel, Lalbaug and Byculla, in a district everybody called Girangaon, the village of mills. At its height it held around a hundred and thirty mills. The men who worked them came down from the Konkan coast and in from the dry plateau, and they lived in chawls: four or five storeys of single rooms opening onto a shared corridor, with the taps and the lavatories at the end of it. A room like that has no kitchen worth the name, and a shift leaves no time to use one.

So the district ate out, and it ate standing. Some men bought a monthly place at a khanaval, a mess run out of a front room by a woman who cooked for twenty. The rest ate at stalls. Every characteristic Bombay dish answers the same brief: no plate, no cutlery, no sitting down, and ready before the siren goes. The great textile strike of 1982 brought a quarter of a million people out, and when it collapsed more than a hundred thousand of them had no job to go back to. Most of the mills closed over the years that followed. The land they stood on now carries office towers, and the food outlived the industry that designed it.

The trains.

India's first passenger railway ran from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853, thirty-four kilometres of it. By the end of the century the lines reached the cotton fields of the Deccan and the wheat of the north, and they carried people in the other direction. Victoria Terminus, finished in 1888 to Frederick Stevens' design and now called Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, was built to look like a cathedral and functions as a funnel.

The suburban network still moves several million people a day, and it imposes a hard rule on eating: whatever you buy has to be dealt with between the ticket barrier and the platform. A vada pav takes one hand and ninety seconds. That is not a coincidence, it is a specification.

The Iranis.

Late in the nineteenth century and into the twentieth, Zoroastrians began arriving from Yazd and Kerman in Iran, driven out by persecution and by drought. They are not the same community as the Parsis, who had reached Gujarat around a thousand years earlier, though the two share a religion and a good deal else. To tell them apart in Bombay is easy enough: the newcomers opened cafés, and everyone called them Irani cafés.

They took the corner plots that Hindu traders avoided as inauspicious, which is why so many of them face two streets at once. Inside there were marble-topped tables, bentwood chairs, tall mirrors and a handwritten list of things not permitted, which in some cafés ran to twenty lines. What they sold was simple and is still the best breakfast in the city: bun maska, a soft sweet bun split and buttered; brun maska, the same thing with a hard crust, dunked until it gives; Irani chai, brewed strong and long with a lot of milk; mawa cake; and kheema pav for anybody who wanted meat. There were several hundred of these cafés once, and there are a few dozen now.

The Parsi kitchen, arrived from Gujarat.

The Gujarat module left the Parsis at Sanjan, where they landed and were given leave to stay. What happened afterwards belongs to this city. The community moved to Bombay in numbers during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, went into shipbuilding, trade and then cotton, and the kitchen changed with the money. It kept Gujarati sweetness and Gujarati sourness, took eggs and meat and vinegar in quantity, and borrowed freely from British and Iranian tables.

Dhansak is the dish everybody names. Several kinds of dal and a few vegetables are cooked to a purée, mutton is cooked in it, and it is served with rice browned in caramelised sugar and a raw onion salad. It is not festival food. Parsi households eat it on Sundays, and by long custom on the fourth day after a death, and never on a day of celebration, so ordering it at a wedding would be a mistake.

Sali boti is the other one, and it is on this region's list. Boti is the meat, cubed goat or mutton, cooked down in a thick gravy made sweet with jaggery and sour with vinegar, often with dried apricots in it. Sali are the potato straws: potato cut to matchsticks, rinsed of starch and fried hard until they snap. They are piled on top at the last possible moment and never stirred in, because the whole contrast of the dish is a crisp dry topping on a sticky dark stew. The dish is named after its garnish, which tells you the garnish is not negotiable.

The Gateway, and everybody else.

George V and Queen Mary landed at Apollo Bunder in 1911, and a foundation stone went down to mark it. George Wittet's design was approved in 1914, the arch was finished in 1924, and Viceroys arriving by sea came ashore under it for the next two decades. On 28 February 1948 the last British troops in India, the First Battalion of the Somerset Light Infantry, marched out through the same arch to their ship. An arch built to receive an empire was used to see it off, and it took twenty-four years.

Everyone else kept coming. Restaurants run by families from Udupi in coastal Karnataka brought idli and dosa to Bombay and made south Indian breakfast a Bombay habit. Gujarati and Marwari traders brought their sweets and their vegetarian food, the East Indian Catholics of Bandra and Vasai brought their own spice blend, and Bohra and Konkani Muslim kitchens brought theirs. The city was renamed Mumbai in 1995, after Mumbadevi, the goddess of the fisherfolk who were there before any of it.
