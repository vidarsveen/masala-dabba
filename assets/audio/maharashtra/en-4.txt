Bombay: mills, Iranis and the food of arrival.

Mumbai's food begins before the modern city, with Koli fishing communities and the East Indian Catholics of the islands and neighbouring coast. Mills, railways and migration later added Parsi, Irani, Gujarati, Marathi, Konkani, south Indian and many other kitchens. Portable street food grew within that layered city.

Bombay began as seven islands, separated at high tide and joined by mud at low. Portugal held them from the early sixteenth century and handed them to England in 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza on her marriage to Charles II. The Crown found them expensive and leased them to the East India Company in 1668 for ten pounds a year. Reclamation over the next two hundred years welded the islands into one, so the modern city sits on a shape that did not exist when it was named.

Cotton helped transform the colonial port. Deccan production, rail connections and the harbour supported trade and manufacturing. Cowasjee Nanabhai Davar established a steam-powered mill in 1854, and production began in 1856. The American Civil War disrupted United States supplies and intensified Bombay's cotton boom.

Mills clustered in Parel, Lalbaug and nearby districts known as Girangaon, the village of mills. Workers arrived from the Konkan and interior Maharashtra and often lived in dense chawls with shared facilities. Homes still cooked, while khanavals, canteens and street stalls met the needs of shifts and crowded housing.

The 1982–83 textile strike involved roughly a quarter of a million workers and preceded the industry's long decline. Mill districts helped create markets for inexpensive food near gates and stations, but no single industry designed every characteristic Mumbai dish.

India's first passenger railway ran from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853, thirty-four kilometres of it. By the end of the century the lines reached the cotton fields of the Deccan and the wheat of the north, and they carried people in the other direction. Victoria Terminus, finished in 1888 to Frederick Stevens' design and now called Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, was built to look like a cathedral and functions as a funnel.

The suburban network moves millions of passengers on busy days. Food sold around stations benefits from being quick, inexpensive and easy to carry, which helps explain the continuing success of vada pav without reducing it to railway engineering.

From the nineteenth century, migrants from Iran—including Zoroastrians from Yazd and Kerman and members of other religious communities—settled in Bombay. The Zoroastrian Iranis had arrived much later than the established Parsi community. Irani cafés became one visible part of these migrations.

Many cafés occupied well-lit corner premises and used marble-topped tables, bentwood chairs, mirrors and painted rules to create a recognisable room. Menus varied but commonly included bun maska or crusty brun with butter, strong milky tea, cakes, eggs and kheema pav. Hundreds once operated across Bombay; only a small share survives.

Parsis moved from Gujarat to Bombay in growing numbers during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and became prominent in shipbuilding, trade and industry. Their food combined Gujarati techniques and sweet-sour balances with meat, eggs, vinegar and influences from Iranian and British tables.

Dhansak combines several pulses and vegetables with meat in many household versions and is often served with browned rice and onion salad. It is associated with Sunday meals and with a memorial meal after a death, so many Parsi families avoid it on festive occasions. Practice is customary rather than universal.

Sali boti cooks pieces of meat in a sweet-sour gravy that may include jaggery, vinegar and dried fruit. Crisp potato straws, sali, are added just before serving so they contrast with the soft stew.

George V and Queen Mary landed at Apollo Bunder in 1911, and a foundation stone went down to mark it. George Wittet's design was approved in 1914, the arch was finished in 1924, and Viceroys arriving by sea came ashore under it for the next two decades. On 28 February 1948 the last British troops in India, the First Battalion of the Somerset Light Infantry, marched out through the same arch to their ship. An arch built to receive an empire was used to see it off, and it took twenty-four years.

Migration continued to reshape the table. Udupi restaurants helped make idli and dosa everyday city food; Gujarati, Marwari, Bohra and Konkani Muslim cooks added other repertoires. East Indian Catholic and Koli foodways remained part of the city's own foundation. Maharashtra approved the official name Mumbai in 1995, invoking Mumbadevi.
