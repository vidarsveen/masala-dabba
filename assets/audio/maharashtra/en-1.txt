Goda masala and the stone flower.

Open a spice tin in Pune or Nashik and you may find a brown blend smelling of roasted coconut, sesame and warm bark. It is goda masala. Goda can mean sweet in Marathi, but here it also signals a mellow aromatic blend rather than a sugary one.

Roasting gives the blend its colour and depth. Coriander and cumin commonly form the base; sesame, dried coconut, cinnamon, clove, bay, black cardamom and dagad phool appear in many versions. Ingredients, proportions and the depth of roasting vary, so there is no single household formula.

Kala masala means black masala and is another family of dark regional blends. It may be roasted more deeply and contain chilli, but the boundary between names is not fixed everywhere. Goda masala is valued for warm, toasted flavour in amti, stuffed aubergines, masale bhat and usal.

Dagad phool means stone flower, but it is a dried edible lichen, commonly identified among Parmotrema species. The traded material looks like grey-brown paper and can come from bark or rock. Tamil cooks know a related product as kalpasi.

Used sparingly, the lichen adds earthy, woody aromas beneath roasted coconut, sesame and chilli. It is subtle enough that recipes differ on whether to include it, and too much can make a blend bitter or dusty.

Hotter blends associated with Kolhapur and Vidarbha follow other formulas. Kanda-lasun masala includes dried onion and garlic. Bedgi chilli is prized for colour with moderate heat, while small hot chillies can raise the burn. Cooks adjust the mixture to the dish rather than following one statewide scale.

Groundnut is an important crop and pantry ingredient on parts of the Deccan plateau. Peanuts provide fat, texture and body, while coastal cooking has readier access to coconut. Both ingredients cross that geographic boundary.

Roasted peanuts are scattered over kanda poha and stirred into some koshimbir salads. Ground finer, they thicken bharli-vangi stuffing with coconut and sesame. They also give sabudana khichdi flavour and structure during Hindu fasts that permit its ingredients.

Kokum is the dried rind of Garcinia indica, a tree of the Western Ghats and Konkan. It gives fruit acidity to coastal curries and drinks in Maharashtra as well as neighbouring Goa and Gujarat.

Whole rind can sour Malvani fish curry without the dark pulp of tamarind. In solkadhi, kokum extract colours and sharpens coconut milk, often with garlic. Bottled kokum extract, known as agal in some coastal kitchens, provides the same souring ingredient outside the fresh season.

The Marathi word for a hot-fat tempering is phodni. A common version pops mustard seed before adding asafoetida, turmeric and curry leaves. Other seeds, chillies or garlic may enter, and the order changes to protect ingredients that scorch quickly.

Mustard, hing, turmeric and curry leaf form a familiar Maharashtrian combination, but no four-part sequence defines every phodni. The hot fat carries their aroma through dal, poha and vegetable dishes.
