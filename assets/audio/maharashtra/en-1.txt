Goda masala and the stone flower.

Open a spice tin in a kitchen in Pune or Nashik and one compartment will hold something almost black. Rub a little between your fingers and it smells of roasted coconut, burnt sesame and damp stone. The jar it came out of said goda masala, and goda means sweet in Marathi. The blend is not sweet. Clearing that up is the quickest way into this kitchen, because the name has been confusing outsiders for as long as anyone has been writing recipes down.

Why a dark blend is called sweet.

Goda masala is dark because almost everything in it is roasted before it is ground. Dry coconut is grated and taken to brown in a pan. Sesame is roasted until the seeds jump. Coriander seed, cumin, cinnamon, clove, bay and black cardamom go the same way, and the whole lot is ground with a little oil into a coarse, oily, near-black powder. The colour comes from the coconut and the sesame rather than from chilli, and there is very little chilli in it.

The name records a comparison, not a taste. The other blend on the same shelf is kala masala, black masala, which is roasted harder and carries a great deal of chilli. Set the two side by side and one of them is obviously the gentler, so the gentler one became the sweet one. No sugar goes in. A cook who expects goda masala to sweeten a dish will be baffled by what it actually does, which is to make a bowl of dal taste of woodsmoke and toasted coconut.

That blend seasons the everyday food of the plateau: varan and amti, the plain and the spiced dal; bharli vangi, small aubergines stuffed with coconut, peanut and sesame; masale bhat, a spiced rice cooked with vegetables; and the sprouted-bean usal that reading three turns into misal. One blend, a dozen dishes, and each household grinds it slightly differently.

The stone flower, met once already.

Inside that blend sits the strangest thing in the Maharashtrian pantry. Dagad phool means stone flower, from dagad, stone, and it is not a flower at all. It is a lichen of the genus Parmotrema, scraped off bark and rock in the hills, dried, and sold looking like a handful of grey paper shavings.

If you have worked through the Tamil Nadu module, you have met it before. There it is called kalpasi and it goes into the Chettinad roast. Same organism, two kitchens eight hundred kilometres apart, two entirely unrelated names, and neither kitchen knows or cares what the other calls it. Noticing that is the course doing its job.

What it does here is specific. Goda masala is built on roasted coconut, and roasted coconut is sweet and rounded and can easily turn soft. The lichen gives the blend a floor of damp earth and old timber that keeps the coconut honest. In the hot blends of Kolhapur it does the same work underneath the chilli, which is why a Kolhapuri gravy tastes deep rather than merely hot. Leave it out and nothing in the dish will announce its absence; the whole blend simply becomes thinner.

Kala masala and the Kolhapur heat.

Kala masala is the same idea taken much further. The whole spices are fried in oil until they are close to black, and dried chillies go in by the handful rather than the pinch. Kolhapur's own hot blend is kanda lasun masala, fried dark with onion and garlic, and it is built on two chillies with different jobs: Bedgi is wrinkled, mild and enormously red, so it colours, while the small Lavangi supplies the burn. A Kolhapuri cook adjusts the ratio and thereby adjusts the dish, which is more control than a single chilli powder can give.

Vidarbha, in the east of the state, grinds its own Varhadi blend and is if anything fiercer. Reading three is where both of these arrive on a plate.

Peanut, the fat of a dry country.

The Deccan plateau is dry, and the groundnut does well in dry ground. Gujarat grows more of it, but Maharashtra grows enough that peanuts are a kitchen staple here rather than something bought for a treat, and the kitchen leans on them in a way no coastal kitchen needs to.

Roasted, skinned and crushed coarse, peanuts are scattered over kanda poha at breakfast and stirred into koshimbir, the cucumber and curd salad. Ground finer, they thicken the stuffing of bharli vangi along with coconut and sesame. On fasting days they carry sabudana khichdi, the sago dish that would be a plate of slippery pearls without them. Groundnut oil is also the frying fat of the plateau, so a kitchen sixty kilometres inland smells quite different from one on the coast.

Kokum, and what it does here.

Kokum is the dried rind of Garcinia indica, a tree of the Konkan hills, and this course has already met it twice: Goa sours its Hindu kitchen with it, and Gujarat steeps it for a summer drink. Maharashtra gets the third use, and it is the one nearest the tree.

In Malvani fish curry the rind goes in whole and gives a clean acidity that holds up against coconut and chilli without muddying the colour the way tamarind does. In a Konkan house it also arrives as solkadhi, kokum steeped until the water turns pink and then mixed with thin coconut milk, garlic and salt. Goa drinks it too. What is particular here is the concentrate: rinds are packed down with salt into agal, a dark syrup kept in a bottle, and a spoonful of that sours a pot in winter when no fresh rind is to be had.

Curry leaf, and the Marathi word for tempering.

Every Indian kitchen starts a dish by seasoning hot fat, and every region has its own name for the moment. Hindi says tadka, Tamil says thalippu, Bengal says phoron, Gujarat says vaghar. Marathi says phodni, and the sequence is fixed: oil, then mustard seed until it pops, then a pinch of asafoetida, then turmeric, then a handful of curry leaves that crackle and go quiet.

The turmeric and the asafoetida are what make a phodni sound Maharashtrian rather than Keralan. Both go into the oil before anything else, so the finished dal carries a faint savoury bitterness underneath the goda masala. Curry leaf here is not a flourish and not a garnish. It is one member of a four-part opening, and a cook who has run out will usually go on without it rather than substitute.

In the pantry: goda masala.

 Aroma: Roasted coconut and toasted sesame over warm bark, with damp stone underneath.

 Flavour: Deep, faintly bitter, barely hot. Sweet only next to kala masala, and never sweet on its own.

 What it does: Gives dal, stuffed vegetables and sprouted-bean usal their colour and their whole savoury weight.

 When to add: Into the pan after the onion has softened, so the oil takes it up before any liquid goes in.

 Instead of it: No blend on a European shelf is close. Garam masala makes a different dish rather than a weaker one.
