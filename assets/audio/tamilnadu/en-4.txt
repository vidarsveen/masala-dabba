Cholas, Romans and the Chettiars who went east.

The inscriptions at Brihadisvara temple record gifts, land, personnel and resources that sustained worship and administration. Foodstuffs and lamp oil appear within that larger institutional record, linking the Chola monument to the delta economy around it.

Long before the Cholas, Tamil ports traded with the Mediterranean. Mortimer Wheeler excavated Arikamedu, just south of Puducherry, in 1945. He found Italian Arretine tableware, wine amphorae and glass in a settlement that also made beads and dyed cloth. Kaveripattinam at the mouth of the Kaveri and the Pandya pearl centre at Korkai belonged to the same trading world. Tamil Sangam poetry from roughly this period mentions yavanar, western foreigners, arriving with wine and leaving with pepper.

Roman coins, amphorae, ceramics and glass help document exchange with the Mediterranean world. Some coins were cut or countermarked, but scholars debate how they circulated and whether every altered coin served as bullion. Pliny's complaint about eastern luxury trade is evidence of Roman concern, not a complete trade account.

The Kaveri helped make the Chola state possible. The river crosses the peninsula and spreads into a delta around Thanjavur. Kallanai, a dam near Tiruchirappalli, has diverted water into that delta since roughly the second century. Reliable irrigated rice supported the state. The delta remains a major rice-growing area, and its harvest shapes everyday cooking built around rice, lentils and souring ingredients.

Rajaraja I consecrated Brihadisvara in 1009–1010. Under Rajendra I, a Chola fleet attacked Srivijaya in 1025, while Tamil merchant networks also operated across the Bay of Bengal. Nineteenth-century Chettiar expansion used a much older maritime sphere without forming one continuous organization.

The Nattukottai Chettiars, also called Nagarathar, are a banking community from a dry patch of country south-east of Thanjavur, traditionally counted as ninety-six villages and now nearer seventy. Their land grows very little. From the early nineteenth century their business was credit, and they took it abroad: to Ceylon, to Burma, to Malaya, to Saigon and Singapore. A Chettiar firm ran on a network of kittangi, small counting houses where a young man was posted for a three-year term before coming home, and the family's capital moved between them.

In Burma, Chettiar firms financed much of the Irrawaddy delta's expanding rice economy. By the 1930s they held a large share of agricultural land after the depression ruined many borrowers. The outcome is remembered bitterly in Myanmar. Profits also funded houses at home. Mansions in Kanadukathan and Karaikudi combine Burma teak, Belgian mirror glass, Italian marble and local Athangudi tiles around long courtyards.

Overseas links influenced architecture, consumption and access to imported goods. They offer a plausible context for ingredients such as star anise, but the exact route and date by which each spice entered Chettinad cooking remain undocumented.

French rule also left a mark. France held Puducherry, with interruptions and alongside other small enclaves, from 1674 until its 1954 transfer to India. The town's Creole cooking combines French and Tamil influences. Its street plan and architecture also preserve a French quarter beside Tamil neighbourhoods. Arikamedu, the earlier trading site, lies a short way down the same river.

A Tamil meal contains ingredients with different histories. Rice, pulses, tamarind, pepper and sesame have long regional records. Chillies arrived after the Columbian exchange; coffee expanded under plantation economies; potatoes and traded aromatics entered through other routes. Exact dates for individual dishes remain harder to prove.
