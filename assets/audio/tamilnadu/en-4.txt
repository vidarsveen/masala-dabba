Cholas, Romans and the Chettiars who went east.

Walk round the base of the great temple at Thanjavur and you are reading a ledger. The granite plinth is covered in Tamil inscriptions, and a large part of what they record is food and the money to buy it. They list measures of paddy endowed to the temple, oil for the lamps and ghee. They also name the villages whose revenue would pay for all of it, year after year. A thousand years later it is one of the most detailed accounts anywhere of what a large south Indian institution actually consumed.

Rome, on this coast.

Long before the Cholas, Tamil ports were selling to the Mediterranean. Arikamedu, on the Ariyankuppam river just south of Puducherry, was excavated in 1945 by Mortimer Wheeler, who found Italian Arretine tableware, wine amphorae and glass in a working settlement that had also been making beads and dyeing cloth. Kaveripattinam, the Chola port at the mouth of the Kaveri, and Korkai, the Pandya pearl town further south, were doing the same kind of business. Tamil Sangam poetry from roughly this period mentions the yavanar, the Greek-speaking westerners, arriving with wine and leaving with pepper.

The coins are the hardest evidence. Roman gold and silver hoards turn up across the far south, in Pudukottai, Karur and Coimbatore district, more densely than almost anywhere outside the empire itself. Many of the pieces carry a deliberate chisel cut across the emperor's head, which suggests they were being handled as bullion by weight rather than spent as currency. Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century, complained that India alone drained fifty million sesterces a year out of the Roman economy. The grumbling of a Roman gentleman about the balance of payments is part of why the trade can be dated so confidently.

The Cholas, and a kingdom built on a delta.

What made the Chola state possible was the Kaveri. The river comes off the Western Ghats, crosses the width of the peninsula and spreads into a delta around Thanjavur. A dam thrown across it near Tiruchirappalli, the Kallanai, has been diverting water into that delta since roughly the second century. Reliable irrigated rice paid for everything else. The delta is still the rice bowl of the state, and the fact that the everyday cooking of Tamil Nadu is built around rice, lentils and something sour rather than around wheat or meat begins there.

Rajaraja I finished the Brihadisvara temple in 1010, and his son Rajendra did something that matters more for this reading. In 1025 a Chola fleet crossed the Bay of Bengal and raided Srivijaya, the maritime power in what is now Sumatra and the Malay peninsula. Tamil merchant guilds were already trading into south-east Asia and went on doing so for centuries. The sea road east is not a colonial invention; it is old, and the Chettiars in the nineteenth century were travelling a route their region had used for a thousand years.

The Chettiars who went east.

The Nattukottai Chettiars, also called Nagarathar, are a banking community from a dry patch of country south-east of Thanjavur, traditionally counted as ninety-six villages and now nearer seventy. Their land grows very little. From the early nineteenth century their business was credit, and they took it abroad: to Ceylon, to Burma, to Malaya, to Saigon and Singapore. A Chettiar firm ran on a network of kittangi, small counting houses where a young man was posted for a three-year term before coming home, and the family's capital moved between them.

In Burma they financed most of the rice frontier of the Irrawaddy delta, and by the 1930s Chettiar firms held a very large share of the agricultural land there after the depression put their borrowers under. That ended badly and is remembered bitterly in Burma. What came home, though, was money, and it was spent on houses. The mansions of Kanadukathan and Karaikudi are built of Burma teak, Belgian mirror glass, Italian marble and local Athangudi tiles. They are laid out around courtyards long enough to seat several hundred people at a wedding.

Food came home too. A community that kept men in Rangoon, Penang and Saigon for decades had a standing supply line to places where star anise, cassia and whole spice were ordinary. Chettinad is the one Indian regional kitchen that treats star anise as routine. The trade is thoroughly documented; the precise route each spice took into the masala is not, and it would be overstating things to claim a single import date. What is clear is that the spice cupboard of this district looks eastward in a way that no neighbouring district's does.

Puducherry, and the French.

One more arrival left a mark. The French held Puducherry, with a few other small enclaves, from 1674 until 1954, and this course counts it with Tamil Nadu because the food does. The Creole cooking of the town puts French technique onto Tamil ingredients and is a genuinely separate small cuisine, and the town itself still runs on a grid of French street names next to a Tamil quarter. Arikamedu, the Roman site, is a short way down the same river.

What is old on the plate and what is not.

Sort a Tamil meal by age and it comes apart neatly. Rice, tamarind, pepper, sesame oil, urad dal and the whole idea of souring a dal are the old layer, and they were here when the Roman ships were. Chilli came with the Portuguese in the sixteenth century and was named against pepper rather than replacing it. Star anise arrived with Chettiar money out of the east. Coffee is a nineteenth-century plantation crop grown in the state's own hills, and chicory was added to stretch it. Potato in a masala dosa is younger still. A plate that looks timeless is in fact a stack of arrivals, and that is the honest way to read any regional kitchen.
