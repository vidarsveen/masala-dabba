Pepper, kalpasi and the Chettinad roast.

Stand in a Chettinad kitchen while the masala is being made and the first thing you notice is that nothing is wet. A heavy iron pan goes on the flame with no oil in it, and coriander seed, dried chilli, fennel, pepper, a star anise and a grey curl of lichen go in dry. The cook keeps them moving until the room smells scorched, and stops a few seconds before a European would have said the spices were burnt. That last few seconds is the whole difference between this kitchen and the one next door.

Milagu came first.

Tamil has a word for pepper, milagu, and a word for chilli, milagai. The second was built out of the first when the Portuguese brought chilli to the coast in the sixteenth century, because the new thing was obviously a kind of pepper and needed naming quickly. The etymology points at something this module keeps returning to. For most of Tamil history the heat on the plate was pepper, and everything about how pepper is used here still reflects that.

Kerala, on the other side of the Western Ghats, grows the vine and explains it in its own module. Tamil Nadu's business was selling it. What matters in the kitchen is that a Tamil cook treats pepper as a flavour in its own right rather than as a background. Whole peppercorns are crushed coarse and go in late, so they arrive in the mouth one at a time. Milagu rasam is a broth built on nothing but pepper, cumin and tamarind. Milagu kozhi, pepper chicken, is dry-fried until the meat is coated in cracked pepper and almost nothing else. Chilli is used too, freely, but it never took pepper's job away in the way it did further north.

The everyday cupboard is short.

The surprise of Tamil cooking, once you get past its reputation, is how restrained most of it is. A house in the Kaveri delta cooks rice, toor dal, a vegetable, something sour and a tempering. The tempering is almost the same every day. Mustard seed goes into hot sesame oil until it pops. Then come a pinch of urad dal and chana dal, a dried red chilli, a handful of curry leaf and a lump of asafoetida the size of a lentil. Turmeric and tamarind go into the pot, and that is most of it.

Two details in that list are properly Tamil. The lentils in the tempering are there as a spice rather than as food. They toast in the oil, turn nutty and go crisp, and you bite one in a mouthful of rice the way you would bite a seed. And the fat is gingelly oil, cold-pressed sesame, which has a toasted-seed smell that coconut oil does not and that a neutral oil cannot fake.

Then Chettinad, which is the opposite.

Inland, in the dry country around Karaikudi, the Chettiar merchant families cook in a way that would look excessive anywhere else in the south. The method is a dry roast. Whole spices go into an unoiled pan over a hard flame and are taken most of the way to black, then ground, usually with a little roasted coconut or poppy seed to bind the paste. Roasting that far drives off the volatile top notes and leaves behind something darker and heavier, more like coffee than like fresh spice. It also makes the masala keep, which mattered in a community whose men spent years abroad.

The standing ingredients are fennel, black pepper, dried chilli, coriander, cinnamon and clove, and then three that mark this kitchen out from every other one in India: star anise, kalpasi and marathi mokku. Reading four explains how they got here, because the answer is not botanical.

Kalpasi is a lichen.

Kalpasi is sold as black stone flower, and it is neither black nor a flower. It is a lichen, Parmotrema perlatum, which grows as a ruffled grey-green crust on tree bark and rock across the hills of southern India, and it belongs to the same family as the oakmoss that perfumers use. Dried, it looks like a handful of dirty paper shavings.

Taste a piece raw and you will conclude it is a waste of money, because it tastes of very little. It only works in fat and heat, and then it releases a smell that people describe as damp earth, old wood and smoke. What it does in a masala is not to add a flavour you can name but to put a floor under everything else, so the blend reads as deep rather than merely strong. Leave it out of a Chettinad masala and the result is recognisably thinner.

In the pantry: kalpasi.

 Aroma: Almost nothing cold; damp earth, old timber and smoke once it hits hot oil.

 Flavour: No sharp flavour of its own, faintly bitter and mineral at the edges.

 What it does: Gives a ground masala depth and length, so the other spices sit on top of something.

 When to add: Dry-roasted with the whole spices, or dropped into the oil at the start of the tempering.

 Instead of it: Nothing behaves like it. Leave it out and accept a lighter masala rather than substituting.

Star anise, and a bud nobody can quite name.

Star anise is the fingerprint. The tree, Illicium verum, grows in southern China and northern Vietnam, and it has never been an Indian crop. Its presence in a village masala in Tamil Nadu is therefore a historical fact rather than an agricultural one. It brings a sweet liquorice weight that sits oddly beside pepper on paper and works very well in the pan. It is also strong enough that one point of one star will often do a whole dish.

Marathi mokku is harder to write about honestly. It is a small, hard, dark bud, roughly the shape of a clove but woodier, and it tastes of clove and pepper with a mustardy edge. Most Indian spice writing identifies it as the dried flower bud of a caper, a Capparis, and a minority calls it a kapok bud instead. The plant has never been settled in print the way cardamom or clove has, which is a reminder that a regional spice can stay in daily use for centuries without anybody writing down what it is.

Sambar podi, and the blend that is not garam masala.

Every Tamil household keeps one ground blend, and it is sambar podi. Coriander seed dominates it, with dried red chilli, fenugreek, a little pepper and cumin, turmeric and asafoetida. One further move gives the blend away as a southern one. Toor dal and chana dal are roasted and ground in with the spices. The lentils thicken as well as flavour, so a spoonful of the powder does two jobs at once.

Notice what is missing. There is no cinnamon, no clove, no cardamom, none of the warm sweet spices that make a north Indian garam masala. The two blends are built for different problems, and using one where the other belongs is the commonest mistake an outsider makes with south Indian food. Podi is also a whole family rather than a single thing: milagai podi, the coarse chilli and lentil powder eaten with idli under a spoonful of gingelly oil, is a podi too.
