Pepper, kalpasi and the Chettinad roast.

A Chettinad masala often begins in a dry pan. Coriander, chilli, fennel, pepper and selected aromatics are roasted until fragrant and darker, then removed before they scorch. The roast builds a deeper flavour, but recipes and spice lists vary from household to household.

Tamil calls black pepper milagu and chilli milagai, names that preserve their perceived relationship. Chillies spread through South Asia after the Columbian exchange, while pepper had a much older place in regional trade and cooking.

Black pepper is treated as a leading flavour in dishes such as milagu rasam and milagu kozhi. Coarsely crushed pepper can be added late for a fresh aroma and distinct bursts of heat. Chilli also matters, and the balance between the two changes with the dish.

Tamil cooking contains many distinct community and regional traditions. A Kaveri-delta meal may combine rice, dal, vegetables, a souring ingredient and a tempering. Mustard seed, split pulses, dried chilli, curry leaf and asafoetida form one common pattern, with sesame oil among several fats used.

Two details in that list are properly Tamil. The lentils in the tempering are there as a spice rather than as food. They toast in the oil, turn nutty and go crisp, and you bite one in a mouthful of rice the way you would bite a seed. And the fat is gingelly oil, cold-pressed sesame, which has a toasted-seed smell that coconut oil does not and that a neutral oil cannot fake.

In the dry country around Karaikudi, Chettinad recipes often roast whole spices before grinding them. The cook takes them to a fragrant brown without scorching, sometimes adding coconut or poppy seed. The blend is usually made fresh; roasting changes its aroma but does not by itself make storage indefinite.

Fennel, pepper, dried chilli, coriander, cinnamon and clove appear in many versions. Star anise, kalpasi and marathi mokku are also strongly associated with Chettinad, although none is exclusive to it.

Kalpasi is sold as black stone flower, and it is neither black nor a flower. It is the lichen Parmotrema perlatum, which grows as a ruffled grey-green crust on bark and rock across the hills of southern India. It belongs to the same family as the oakmoss used in perfumery. Dried, it looks like a handful of grey paper shavings.

Taste a piece raw and you will conclude it is a waste of money, because it tastes of very little. It only works in fat and heat, and then it releases a smell that people describe as damp earth, old wood and smoke. What it does in a masala is not to add a flavour you can name but to put a floor under everything else, so the blend reads as deep rather than merely strong. Leave it out of a Chettinad masala and the result is recognisably thinner.

Star anise, Illicium verum, is native to southern China and northern Vietnam and reached Indian kitchens through trade. Its liquorice-like aroma makes it distinctive in some Chettinad blends, although the spice is used elsewhere in India too.

Marathi mokku is a small, hard dried bud with clove-like and peppery notes. Retail and reference sources disagree over the plant identity, often describing a kapok-related bud and sometimes a caper. The course therefore keeps the trade name and avoids assigning a species without stronger evidence.

Sambar podi is one important Tamil spice blend, but formulas vary. Coriander seed and dried chilli commonly lead, with fenugreek, cumin, pepper or asafoetida in changing proportions. Some versions include roasted pulses, while others leave them for the dish itself.

Notice what is missing. There is no cinnamon, no clove, no cardamom, none of the warm sweet spices that make a north Indian garam masala. The two blends are built for different problems, and using one where the other belongs is the commonest mistake an outsider makes with south Indian food. Podi is also a whole family rather than a single thing: milagai podi, the coarse chilli and lentil powder eaten with idli under a spoonful of gingelly oil, is a podi too.
