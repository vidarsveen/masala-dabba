Idli, sambar and the Chettinad kitchen.

A breakfast shop in Madurai may serve idli, vada and dosa with sambar, chutneys and chilli-and-lentil podi. Fermented rice-and-urad batter supports several of those dishes, showing how one prepared base can become different meals.

An idli is fermented batter steamed in shallow moulds. Plain versions contain little beyond rice, urad, salt and water, while seasoned forms also exist. Its soft, porous crumb takes up sambar and chutney.

A dosa uses a related batter spread thin on a hot griddle and cooked until crisp. Recipes differ in rice, pulses, grinding and fermentation. Masala dosa is strongly associated with the Udupi restaurant tradition, while plain and ghee dosas are common across Tamil Nadu.

Where the idli comes from is genuinely unsettled. A steamed cake called iddalige appears in a Kannada text of about 920, and iddarika in a Sanskrit encyclopaedia of 1130. Both are described as ground urad dal alone, with no rice and no mention of fermenting. The food historian K. T. Achaya argued that the rice-and-dal version arrived with cooks travelling from the Indonesian archipelago, where similar fermented batters exist. It is a plausible suggestion rather than a settled fact, and it is fair to say only that the idli we eat is younger than the word for it.

Outside India these two are sometimes collapsed into one idea, a lentil soup made thick or thin. Sambar is a stew. Toor dal is cooked soft, then combined with vegetables, tamarind and sambar podi. A tempering of mustard seed, fenugreek, dried chilli, curry leaf and asafoetida can finish the pot. It is thick enough to hold a spoonful of rice together.

Rasam is a thinner broth with many regional formulas. The course version uses tamarind, garlic, pepper, cumin, curry leaf and coriander with optional dal water. Gentle heating preserves its fresh spice aroma.

Milagu rasam places pepper and cumin at the centre and is often served as comforting food during a cold. It does not treat or cure infection, and versions may include tomato, chilli or other seasonings.

The origin story you will be told for sambar is that Sambhaji, son of the Maratha king Shivaji, improvised it at the Thanjavur court when the kokum ran out and tamarind was used instead. It is a good story with no early evidence behind it, and dal soured with tamarind is certainly older than the Marathas in Thanjavur.

Chettinad food has a strong restaurant reputation, although restaurant labels cover many formulas. The course chicken roasts whole spices to a fragrant brown, then grinds them with coconut. Shallots, tomato and curry leaf create a masala that clings to the meat.

Chettinad includes substantial vegetarian traditions alongside meat and seafood dishes. Kootu, kara kuzhambu and rice-and-pulse paniyaram deserve a place beside the restaurant-famous chicken.

Coastal fish curries vary. One Tamil meen kuzhambu style combines tamarind, chilli, coriander, fenugreek, shallots and curry leaf in sesame oil without coconut milk. If saved for another day, cool it promptly, refrigerate within two hours and reheat thoroughly.

Parotta is a flaky wheat bread associated with the Malabar coast and now embedded in Tamil street food. For kothu parotta, cooked bread goes onto a hot griddle with egg, onion, chilli and a ladle of thin salna. Two blunt iron blades chop and mix it in alternating strokes. The loud, rhythmic sound carries down the street and advertises the stall.

A meal ends with payasam, which is milk or thin coconut milk cooked down with rice, vermicelli or moong dal and sweetened with sugar or jaggery. It is looser than a European pudding, close to drinkable, and at a wedding it arrives on the leaf while the savoury dishes are still there rather than afterwards.
