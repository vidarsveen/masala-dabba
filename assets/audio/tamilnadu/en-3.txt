Idli, sambar and the Chettinad kitchen.

A Tamil breakfast is not a small meal, and it is almost never sweet. At seven in the morning a mess in Madurai is serving steamed cakes, fried lentil rings and a folded griddle bread, with a pot of sambar, two chutneys and a saucer of chilli powder mixed into sesame oil. Everything on that table came out of two ingredients and a night on the counter, and that economy is the through line of the whole kitchen.

Idli and dosa.

An idli is batter dropped into shallow moulds and steamed for about ten minutes. It contains no fat, no sugar and no spice, and it is not meant to. Its whole job is texture: a warm, faintly sour, open crumb that soaks up whatever it is dipped in. That is why it is never served alone, and why a single idli usually arrives with three different things to put on it.

A dosa is the same batter spread thin on a hot iron griddle, oiled at the edges and cooked on one side only until it lifts away crisp. The masala dosa, folded round a soft spiced potato, is the version the rest of the world knows. It is worth saying that it is generally credited to Udupi cooks in coastal Karnataka rather than to Tamil Nadu. The plain dosa and the ghee roast are the everyday ones here.

Where the idli comes from is genuinely unsettled. A steamed cake called iddalige appears in a Kannada text of about 920, and iddarika in a Sanskrit encyclopaedia of 1130. Both are described as ground urad dal alone, with no rice and no mention of fermenting. The food historian K. T. Achaya argued that the rice-and-dal version arrived with cooks travelling from the Indonesian archipelago, where similar fermented batters exist. It is a plausible suggestion rather than a settled fact, and it is fair to say only that the idli we eat is younger than the word for it.

Sambar and rasam are not two thicknesses of the same thing.

Outside India these two get collapsed into one idea, a lentil soup thick or thin, and they are not related in that way at all. Sambar is a stew. Toor dal is boiled until it collapses, vegetables go in, tamarind extract sours it, sambar podi seasons it, and a tempering of mustard seed, fenugreek, dried chilli, curry leaf and asafoetida is poured over at the end. It is thick enough to hold a spoon of rice together, and one pot feeds a family through a meal.

Rasam is a broth, and most of it is water. Tamarind extract, tomato, crushed garlic, coarsely pounded pepper and cumin, curry leaf and coriander go into a pan, and at most a ladle of thin dal water joins them for body. The rule is that rasam is taken off the flame as soon as it froths at the edges, because boiling it hard dulls the pepper and kills the fresh coriander. Half of it goes over the rice and the other half is drunk from a tumbler beside the leaf.

Milagu rasam, pepper rasam, is the plainest and oldest version: pepper, cumin, garlic, tamarind and nothing else, no tomato and no chilli. It is what a Tamil household makes for someone with a cold, and it is the clearest single demonstration of the point reading one made about pepper doing a job that chilli never took over.

The origin story you will be told for sambar is that Sambhaji, son of the Maratha king Shivaji, improvised it at the Thanjavur court when the kokum ran out and tamarind was used instead. It is a good story with no early evidence behind it, and dal soured with tamarind is certainly older than the Marathas in Thanjavur.

The Chettinad kitchen.

Chettinad food is the one Tamil style with an international reputation, and what reaches a menu abroad is usually just heat. The real distinction is the dry roast described in reading one. A Chettinad chicken starts with whole spices taken almost to black in a dry pan and ground with a little coconut. That paste is cooked down with small shallots, tomato and a great deal of curry leaf, until the masala clings to the meat rather than swimming around it. Fennel is the note that hits first, star anise and kalpasi are underneath, and pepper rather than chilli carries most of the burn.

Worth knowing: the Chettiar families themselves eat vegetarian on ritual days and for much of the week. The vegetarian Chettinad dishes, kootu and kara kuzhambu and the rice-and-lentil kuzhipaniyaram, are cooked far more often than the chicken is. The meat dishes are the ones that travelled, which is a common and slightly misleading pattern.

Meen kuzhambu, on the coast.

Tamil Nadu has a thousand kilometres of coast, and the fish curry along it is sour rather than creamy. Meen kuzhambu is built on tamarind, chilli, coriander and fenugreek in gingelly oil, with small shallots and a handful of curry leaf, and the strict version has no coconut milk in it at all. It is cooked in a clay pot when a household still has one, it is deliberately thin, and like the fish curry on the other coast it is better on the second day. Sardine, mackerel, seer fish and small anchovies all go into it, and in Norway mackerel is the closest match on both fat and texture.

Kothu parotta, and the noise of the evening.

The parotta is a flaky wheat bread that came down from the Malabar coast and settled here, and around Madurai somebody worked out what to do with yesterday's. A cooked parotta is thrown onto a hot griddle with egg, onion, chilli and a ladle of salna, the thin spiced gravy that lives in a pot beside every parotta stall, and then it is chopped. Two blunt iron blades go at it in alternating strokes until the bread is in shreds and everything is mixed through it. The sound carries down the street, it is rhythmic and very loud, and it is the advertisement. Cooks compete on it.

A meal ends with payasam, which is milk or thin coconut milk cooked down with rice, vermicelli or moong dal and sweetened with sugar or jaggery. It is looser than a European pudding, close to drinkable, and at a wedding it arrives on the leaf while the savoury dishes are still there rather than afterwards.
