Fermented batter and the banana-leaf order.

A covered pot of rice-and-urad batter can rise overnight into a lightly sour base for idli and dosa. Temperature controls the pace. Once the batter is visibly aerated and smells cleanly sour, it should be refrigerated until cooking.

The batter has two main ingredients and they are handled separately at first. Parboiled rice is soaked in one bowl. Urad dal, split black gram, is soaked in another, often with fenugreek seed. After four or five hours each is ground on its own, the dal first. The two are then folded together with salt and left somewhere warm for eight to twelve hours.

A changing community of lactic-acid bacteria and yeasts from the ingredients, equipment and environment drives fermentation. Studies identify several organisms rather than one species living only on urad. Rice supplies starch, while ground urad contributes proteins and polysaccharides that help the batter hold gas.

Many recipes use roughly three or four parts rice to one part urad by volume, but ratios vary. A wet grinder aerates the urad while limiting heat. A blender can also work if used in short pulses with cool water.

Many idli recipes use parboiled rice, often sold as idli rice, while others mix it with raw rice. Parboiling changes the grain's starch and hydration, but final texture also depends on variety, grinding, ratio and fermentation.

The same two ingredients then go two ways. Idli batter is ground a little coarse and kept thick, so it sits on the steaming plate rather than running off it. Dosa batter is ground finer and let down with water until it pours, and the fenugreek matters more in it, because fenugreek browns readily and a dosa is judged on its colour.

After the initial warm fermentation, refrigerate the batter. Freshly fermented batter is well suited to idli; older, more acidic batter is often thinned for dosa or used for uthappam and paniyaram. This is a useful progression rather than a fixed three-day calendar.

The drink is coffee, which surprises people who expect chai. Coffee was planted in the Nilgiri and Shevaroy hills in the nineteenth century, and it is grown within the state. In a Tamil house it is made twice a day, in a piece of equipment that does one thing.

The filter is two stainless cylinders that sit one on top of the other with a perforated plate between them. Finely ground coffee goes in the upper cylinder, the plate presses it flat, boiling water goes on top, and the lid goes on. It then drips for fifteen or twenty minutes, unhurried, into the lower cylinder, and what collects there is decoction rather than coffee: thick, bitter and far too strong to drink. A small measure of it goes into a tumbler with boiling milk and sugar. The mixture is then poured back and forth between the tumbler and the wide-lipped bowl called a dabara, held at arm's length, until it has cooled to drinking temperature and carries a head of foam. The pouring is not showmanship. It cools and aerates at the same time, and the foam is what everybody is judging.

Most of the coffee sold for this has chicory ground into it, commonly a fifth to a third, which makes the decoction darker and heavier and stretches the beans. Kumbakonam degree coffee is the most famous local version. The usual explanation of the name is that the milk was tested with a lactometer and its degree recorded, which is a good story but not a documented one.

Banana-leaf meals follow recognizable placement customs, but orientation and exact positions vary by community, event and host. Small accompaniments often line the upper half, while rice sits nearer the eater with room for mixing.

A common sequence moves from dal and sambar to rasam and then curd or buttermilk, with sweets placed according to the occasion. It is a convention, not a statewide rule. When eating as a guest, follow the host's arrangement and folding custom.
