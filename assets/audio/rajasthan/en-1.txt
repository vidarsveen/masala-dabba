Cooking without water: chilli, ghee and yoghurt.

Rajasthan is the largest state in India and one of the driest, and for most of the life of this kitchen the household's water arrived on somebody's head. It was carried up from a well or out of a covered tank, and it was counted. A cook who counts water does not boil vegetables in it and does not simmer a stew for an hour. Almost everything that looks strange about Rajasthani food follows from that one sum.

What a desert kitchen cannot spend.

The Thar covers most of the western half of the state, and the rain that reaches it is both slight and unreliable. Jaisalmer averages under two hundred millimetres a year, roughly a fifteenth of what falls on the Kerala coast, and in a bad year it gets far less. Official figures give Rajasthan about a tenth of India's land and close to one per cent of its surface water. That ratio is not new, so the state spent a thousand years building around it.

The stepwells are the visible half of that effort. At Abhaneri, east of Jaipur, Chand Baori drops thirteen storeys into the ground on some three and a half thousand steps, so that whatever the water level had fallen to, somebody could still walk down and reach it. Villages built covered tanks called tanka to catch whatever rain landed on a roof, and they kept them covered, because an open pool in that heat is a pool that leaves.

The animals did better than the crops. Goats, sheep, camels and cattle walk to their fodder and turn scrub that no person can eat into milk, so a household that could not count on a harvest could usually count on a herd.

Ghee is not a luxury here.

Milk in forty degrees lasts hours. Butter lasts days. Ghee is butter simmered until the water has boiled away and the milk solids have toasted, and it lasts months in a covered jar without refrigeration. So a household turned the surplus of a good week into a fat that would still be there in a bad one.

Then that fat does the work water does elsewhere. Vegetables here are fried rather than boiled, and gram-flour dumplings are browned before they go near a gravy. A bati, which is a hard wheat ball baked in embers, is broken open and drowned in ghee at the table, because without it the thing would be very hard to swallow. Ghee is how a kitchen with no water still produces something rich.

Yoghurt instead of stock.

The second liquid is soured milk, and it turns up wherever a European would expect water or stock. Kadhi, the everyday gravy of the region, is buttermilk thickened with gram flour and sharpened with chilli, and nothing else. Gatte, the gram-flour dumplings, are simmered in a yoghurt gravy. Even the meat is braised in curd. Souring is also how milk is kept, because curd sets overnight in that heat without any help and then lasts longer than the milk did. Churn it, lift off the butter, and what remains is chaas, the thin salted buttermilk drunk at the end of every summer meal.

One technique matters more than the rest. Yoghurt splits the moment it meets a hot pan. A cook therefore beats it smooth first, usually with a spoonful of gram flour stirred through it. It then goes into a warm pan and is stirred steadily in one direction until it comes up to a simmer. Do that and the gravy stays glossy. Tip cold curd straight into hot fat and it curdles at once, and nothing done later will bring it back.

Mathania, and what the chilli is actually for.

Laal maas is usually introduced as the fiercest thing in India, and the introduction is misleading. The chilli in it is the Mathania, grown around a village about thirty kilometres north of Jodhpur, and it goes in by the dozen rather than by the pinch. What the cook buys with all those pods is colour and a smell of dried fruit, not pain. Mathania chillies are deep red and thin-skinned, their heat is moderate rather than savage, and the yoghurt and the ghee hold down whatever heat they do carry.

Learn that as a habit rather than as one recipe's quirk. An Indian cook who wants a red gravy reaches for a lot of a mild chilli instead of a little of a hot one, because colour and pungency sit in different proportions in different varieties.

In the pantry: Mathania chilli.

 Aroma: Dried fruit and warm paprika, with a faint smokiness in sun-dried pods.

 Flavour: Moderate heat carried on real sweetness, nearer a paprika than a bird's eye.

 What it does: Gives laal maas its colour and most of its smell, and is used a dozen pods at a time.

 When to add: Soaked and ground to a paste, or bloomed in warm ghee off the flame so it colours the fat without burning.

 Instead of it: Kashmiri chilli is closest and easiest to find; mild paprika with a little hot chilli gets near enough.

Coriander by the handful.

The spice used in the greatest quantity here is not chilli but coriander seed, and Rajasthan grows a great deal of it. Much of the trade runs through markets such as Ramganj Mandi in Kota district, and the cumin comes from around Jodhpur, Nagaur and Jalore.

Ground coriander does two jobs at once. It gives a warm flavour somewhere between citrus peel and dry wood, and it thickens, because the ground seed swells and lends a gravy body. In a kitchen with no tomatoes, no fried onion and no stock, that body has to come from somewhere, and it comes from coriander, gram flour and yoghurt.

What stands in for what is missing.

Three more things fill gaps. Asafoetida, hing, is the dried resin of a Ferula root, almost all of it imported from Iran and Afghanistan, and a lump the size of a pea will perfume a whole pot. It smells appalling raw and turns savoury and faintly oniony the moment it hits hot fat, which is why it matters here: in the many Rajasthani households that eat no onion and no garlic, hing does their work.

Kachri is a small wild melon of the desert, dried hard and ground to a grey-brown powder. It sours a dish the way a lemon would, and it tenderises meat as well, because the fruit carries enzymes that break protein down.

Clove finishes the list. It grows nowhere near here, but a spice that arrives dry and stays strong for years suits a place where fresh aromatics are scarce, and it does much of the smelling in laal maas. Take the three together and the shape of the pantry appears: nothing fresh, nothing perishable, and every item chosen because it keeps.
