Cooking without water: chilli, ghee and yoghurt.

Rajasthan is India's largest state by area and one of its driest. In many settlements, household water once had to be carried from a well or stored in a covered rainwater tank. Scarcity did not dictate every dish, but it helped favour cooking methods that used little water and ingredients that kept through heat and drought.

The Thar covers much of western Rajasthan, where rain is slight and unreliable. Jaisalmer receives less than two hundred millimetres in an average year. State sources put Rajasthan at 10.4 per cent of India's area but about 1.15 per cent of its surface-water resources. Water harvesting therefore became part of the built landscape.

At Abhaneri, east of Jaipur, Chand Baori uses roughly 3,500 steps to reach water as its level rises and falls. Villages also built covered tanks called tanka to collect roof runoff and limit contamination and evaporation.

Livestock offered another way to use the arid landscape. Goats, sheep, camels and cattle could turn local fodder into milk, though herds were no guarantee against drought. Dairy nevertheless became an important source of fat and flavour.

Fresh milk spoils quickly in intense heat. To make ghee, butter is simmered until its water evaporates and its milk solids brown; the clear fat is then strained. Stored clean and dry, it keeps far longer than milk or butter.

Ghee also supplies richness and cooking fat. Some vegetables and gram-flour dumplings are fried before they enter a gravy, while bati is cracked open and dressed generously with ghee at the table. These practices conserve water in some contexts, but they also persist because people value their texture and flavour.

Soured milk supplies liquid and acidity in many Rajasthani dishes. Kadhi combines yoghurt or buttermilk with gram flour, spices and a tempering. Gatte simmer in a yoghurt gravy, and versions of laal maas use curd around the meat. Chaas, a thin salted buttermilk, is a common cooling drink.

Yoghurt can separate when heated too quickly. Cooks beat it smooth, often whisk in gram flour, and warm it gradually while stirring. The flour helps stabilise the mixture; gentle heat matters more than stirring in any particular direction.

Laal maas is often introduced only as a fiery curry, which misses what its chilli contributes. Mathania chilli, named for a village near Jodhpur, is prized for deep colour and aroma as well as pungency. Heat varies by harvest and pod, so cooks adjust the quantity. Yoghurt can soften the sensation of chilli, while ghee carries its colour and aroma through the dish.

Learn that as a habit rather than as one recipe's quirk. An Indian cook who wants a red gravy reaches for a lot of a mild chilli instead of a little of a hot one, because colour and pungency sit in different proportions in different varieties.

The spice used in the greatest quantity here is not chilli but coriander seed, and Rajasthan grows a great deal of it. Much of the trade runs through markets such as Ramganj Mandi in Kota district, and the cumin comes from around Jodhpur, Nagaur and Jalore.

Ground coriander does two jobs at once. It gives a warm flavour somewhere between citrus peel and dry wood, and it thickens, because the ground seed swells and lends a gravy body. In a kitchen with no tomatoes, no fried onion and no stock, that body has to come from somewhere, and it comes from coriander, gram flour and yoghurt.

Three more ingredients fill particular roles. Asafoetida, hing, is the dried resin of a Ferula root. A small amount turns savoury in hot fat, which makes it useful in households that avoid onion and garlic.

Kachri is a small desert melon, dried and ground to a grey-brown powder. It adds tartness, and studies of the fruit's protease activity support its traditional use as a meat tenderiser.

Clove finishes the list. It grows nowhere near here, but arrives dry and stays strong for years. That suits a kitchen with few fresh aromatics, and clove provides much of the aroma in laal maas.
