Rajputs and Marwaris: two kitchens, one desert.

Two households a street apart in Jodhpur could eat completely different food. In one, goat braised in curd and chilli; in the other, no meat, no egg, no onion and no garlic, and on many days no root vegetable either. Both kitchens fry rather than boil, both sour with curd, both keep a shelf of dried pods. The desert set the technique, and the two communities differ about the ingredients.

A fort that could not count on rain.

Rao Jodha began Mehrangarh in 1459, on a rock that rises a hundred and twenty metres above the plain, and the position that made it defensible also made it awkward to feed. Everything went up the hill, including water, which was drawn from tanks and stepwells cut into the rock and from the reservoirs below. A fort that expected to be besieged had to hold food that would not spoil, so its stores were grain, ghee, dried pulse and dried vegetable — the same list a village kept, at a different scale.

Rajasthan was never one kingdom. Marwar ruled from Jodhpur, Mewar from Chittor and later Udaipur, Amber and then Jaipur in the east, with Bikaner and Jaisalmer out in the sand. They fought each other as readily as they fought anyone else, and the courts competed in hospitality, which is where a good deal of the elaborate cooking comes from.

What a Rajput ate.

The Rajput kitchen is a hunting kitchen. Game was cooked where it was killed, often in the open and often in a pit, and several dishes still carry the method in their names. Sula is meat threaded on skewers, marinated in curd and smoked; it is the grandparent of every tandoori-style kebab in a north Indian restaurant. Khad refers to a pit, and khad khargosh is hare wrapped, sealed and buried under embers to cook for hours without anybody watching it.

All of it is built on the same short list: curd, ghee, chilli, whole spice, and no water. That is not a stylistic choice but the packing list of a camp, and once a technique proves itself in the field it tends to come home with the household.

The other kitchen.

The Marwaris are the trading community of Marwar, and by the eighteenth century they were financing and moving goods far beyond it. Their kitchen is vegetarian without exception, and in many households it goes further and leaves out onion and garlic as well. Take meat, fish, egg, onion and garlic off the table and most cuisines would have very little left. This one has gatte, kadhi, papad ki sabzi, mangodi, ker sangri, bhujia and a long list of sweets, and the interesting point is how it manages without the two ingredients that carry savoury flavour almost everywhere else.

It manages with three substitutions. Asafoetida takes over the job of onion and garlic. Yoghurt and buttermilk supply the sourness and the liquid. Gram flour supplies the body that a fried onion paste would otherwise give, which is why besan turns up in the dumplings, in the gravy and in the thickening of the kadhi all at once.

What Jainism took off the table.

Jainism has been strong in Rajasthan for a very long time, and its temples are among the finest buildings in the state. The temple at Ranakpur, built in the fifteenth century, stands on more than fourteen hundred carved pillars, and the Dilwara temples at Mount Abu are older still. Many Marwari families are Jain, and many more who are not have absorbed the food rules from neighbours.

The central principle is non-violence extended further than most traditions take it. Pulling up a root kills the whole plant and disturbs the organisms in the soil, so strict practice avoids potato, carrot, onion, garlic and ginger. Eating after dark is avoided as well, because insects are drawn to a lamp. A kitchen under those rules builds from what grows above ground and from what can be dried: pulses, gram flour, dairy, and the pods and berries of desert trees. That is a very close description of Rajasthani vegetarian food, and the overlap is not a coincidence.

The Marwaris left and took the food with them.

From the late eighteenth century the Marwaris moved out in numbers, to Calcutta, Bombay, Hyderabad and the tea districts of Assam, and they took the kitchen with them because the kitchen travelled well. A cuisine of dried pods, gram flour and ghee is exactly the kind of food that survives a long journey and can be reproduced a thousand kilometres from home. Bikaneri bhujia went the same way and turned into a national snack.

Hunger drove some of that movement. The famine of 1899 and 1900 is still named in Rajasthan after its year in the Vikram calendar, chhappania akaal, and it killed livestock on a scale that took a generation to replace. When the herd dies, the milk goes, the ghee goes and the curd goes, and a kitchen built on all three has nothing to fall back on. The shelf of dried food was insurance against precisely that.

Water arrives, and the kitchen stays.

In 1958 India began digging what is now the Indira Gandhi Canal, which carries water from the Punjab rivers some six hundred kilometres into the north-western desert. It reached the Jaisalmer district in the 1980s, and where it runs, wheat and mustard now grow on land that grew bajra or nothing. Groundwater pumps have done something similar elsewhere. The scarcity that designed this kitchen is not what it was.

The cooking has not followed. Households with a tap still fry their vegetables rather than boiling them, still sour with curd, still keep sangri in a sack and still drown a bati in ghee. That is the ordinary way of things with food: a technique outlives the problem it solved, and by then people want it for its own sake. Rajasthan is simply a clearer case than most, because you can point at the problem, name the year it eased, and taste that nothing much changed.
