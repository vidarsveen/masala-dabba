Rajputs and Marwaris: two kitchens, one desert.

Food in Rajasthan reflects region, caste, religion, occupation and household choice. Rajput court cooking includes celebrated meat dishes, while Jain and other vegetarian traditions developed wide repertoires without meat. They share techniques and ingredients, but neither label describes a single uniform kitchen.

Rao Jodha began Mehrangarh in 1459, on a rock that rises a hundred and twenty metres above the plain. The position that made it defensible also made it awkward to supply. Water came from tanks, rock-cut stepwells and reservoirs below. Stores included grain, ghee, dried pulses and dried vegetables.

Rajasthan was never one kingdom. Marwar ruled from Jodhpur, Mewar from Chittor and later Udaipur, Amber and then Jaipur in the east, with Bikaner and Jaisalmer out in the sand. They fought each other as readily as they fought anyone else, and the courts competed in hospitality, which is where a good deal of the elaborate cooking comes from.

Hunting and courtly hospitality influenced some Rajput meat dishes. Sula is marinated meat cooked on skewers, while khad names a pit-cooking method associated with dishes such as khad khargosh. Their exact histories are difficult to separate from later culinary storytelling.

Curd, ghee, chilli and whole spices recur in these dishes. Outdoor cooking and limited water help explain the pattern, alongside court wealth, access to game and the preferences of individual royal kitchens.

Marwari can describe people and trading communities connected with Marwar, not one religion or diet. Many Marwari households are vegetarian, and some Jain and Vaishnav households also avoid onion and garlic. Their repertoires include gatte, kadhi, papad ki sabzi, mangodi, ker sangri, bhujia and many sweets.

Where onion and garlic are absent, asafoetida contributes a savoury aroma. Yoghurt and buttermilk add acidity and liquid, while gram flour thickens kadhi and becomes the substance of dumplings. These ingredients do more than imitate what has been omitted.

Jainism has been strong in Rajasthan for a very long time, and its temples are among the finest buildings in the state. The temple at Ranakpur, built in the fifteenth century, stands on more than fourteen hundred carved pillars, and the Dilwara temples at Mount Abu are older still. Many Marwari families are Jain, and many more who are not have absorbed the food rules from neighbours.

Ahimsa, or non-violence, is central to Jain ethics. Jain food is vegetarian, while stricter observance also excludes root vegetables such as potato, onion, garlic and ginger and may avoid eating after dark. Practice differs among lay households. Pulses, grain, dairy, fruits and vegetables that grow above ground support a varied cuisine within those rules.

From the late eighteenth century the Marwaris moved out in numbers, to Calcutta, Bombay, Hyderabad and the tea districts of Assam, and they took the kitchen with them because the kitchen travelled well. A cuisine of dried pods, gram flour and ghee is exactly the kind of food that survives a long journey and can be reproduced a thousand kilometres from home. Bikaneri bhujia went the same way and turned into a national snack.

Drought and famine also shaped migration. The famine of 1899–1900 is remembered in Rajasthan as chhappania akaal, after Vikram Samvat 1956. It devastated people and livestock, but merchant migration had begun earlier and also followed credit, trade and employment networks.

Construction of the Rajasthan Canal, later renamed the Indira Gandhi Canal, began in 1958. Its 445-kilometre main canal was completed in 1986 and ends at Mohangarh in Jaisalmer district. Irrigation brought wheat, mustard and other crops to parts of the north-west, while also creating new ecological and social pressures.

Water access has changed unevenly, and culinary practice has changed too. Yet ghee-dressed bati, yoghurt gravies and dried sangri remain valued foods. Techniques can survive the conditions that encouraged them because they become markers of taste, memory and identity.
