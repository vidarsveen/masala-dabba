Laal maas, ker sangri and gatte.

Three dishes carry most of what Rajasthan has to teach. One is meat cooked with no water and no vegetables, one is a vegetable that grows on a thorn tree and is eaten months after it was picked, and one is a vegetable invented out of flour because there was nothing else. Learn those three and the rest of the region's food follows.

Laal maas comes from a hunting camp.

Laal maas means red meat, and the meat is goat. The dish belongs to the Rajput courts and, before that, to their hunting parties, which is the detail that explains its shape. A shikar camp carried what would survive a journey in heat: ghee in a jar, dried chilli, whole spices, garlic, and curd from the herd travelling with them. It carried no vegetables and no water to spare, so the dish has none.

What that leaves is a braise in which yoghurt is the entire liquid. The curd tenderises the goat, which needs it, and it buffers the chilli, which is why a dish built on a dozen or more pods does not come out unbearable. The ghee is not a garnish either. There is enough of it that as the yoghurt reduces the fat separates and rises, and a slick of red ghee on the surface tells the cook the dish is finished. That test is more reliable than a clock.

How the red is built.

The chillies are stemmed, soaked in warm water and ground to a smooth paste. That paste, not powder shaken from a tin, is what gives the colour its depth. Garlic goes in whole or crushed, the whole spices are clove, cardamom and bay, and the ground spice is coriander, a good deal of it.

Two local moves finish the dish. Kachri powder goes in with the marinade, so the enzymes in the dried melon start breaking the goat down before any heat is applied. And many cooks smoke the finished pot: a live coal is set in a small steel bowl in the middle of the meat, a spoonful of ghee is poured over it, and the lid goes on while the smoke works through. That trick is called dhungar, it takes two minutes, and it is the difference between a good laal maas and a memorable one.

The counterpart dish is safed maas, white meat, from the same kitchens. Same goat, same curd, but the body comes from almonds, cashew and white poppy seed, and there is no red chilli at all. A court that could make one could make the other, and serving both at one meal was the point.

The tree that feeds the desert.

Sangri is the long thin pod of the khejri, Prosopis cineraria, and the khejri is the reason people could live in the Thar at all. It roots deep enough to find water no crop can reach, it keeps its leaves through the hot months, and it fixes nitrogen into the soil, so a millet field with khejri trees standing in it yields better than one without.

In 1730 the Maharaja of Jodhpur sent men to cut khejri wood for a new palace, and a Bishnoi woman named Amrita Devi refused to let them, on the grounds that her faith forbade the felling of a living tree. She was killed, and so were the villagers who followed her example by clasping the trunks. The number usually given is three hundred and sixty-three dead across the surrounding villages before the order was withdrawn. The place is called Khejarli, after the tree, and the story is why the khejri is protected today.

Ker sangri.

Ker is the berry of Capparis decidua, a leafless thorn bush of the same family as the caper, and raw it is sharply bitter. Sangri is the khejri pod. Both are picked, boiled briefly and dried, and then they sit in a sack for as long as anyone needs them to.

To cook them, both are soaked overnight, sangri in salted water or buttermilk and ker in water changed once or twice, and then boiled until they soften. The bitterness leaves with the soaking water, and a cook who skips that step gets a dish nobody will finish. After that they are fried in a generous amount of oil or ghee with red chilli, coriander, turmeric, hing and amchur, and some houses finish with yoghurt.

The finished dish is chewy, sour, hot and dry, and it keeps for a week or more without a fridge, because nothing wet is left in it. That is not a happy accident. Ker sangri was food for a journey and for a wedding feast cooked in quantity a day ahead, and it is still the dish Rajasthanis post to relatives in other cities.

Gatte: a vegetable made of flour.

Gatte ki sabzi answers a simple question. What do you serve when there is no vegetable in the house and no prospect of one? The answer is gram flour. Besan is kneaded stiff with yoghurt, oil, chilli, turmeric, coriander and a good pinch of ajwain, rolled into ropes the thickness of a finger, and boiled for about fifteen minutes until they firm up. The ropes are then sliced into discs, which are the gatte.

Nothing is thrown away. The water the gatte boiled in carries flour and spice out of the dough, so it becomes the base of the gravy, which is then built up with beaten yoghurt and a spoon of besan. Many cooks fry the sliced discs in ghee before they go back in, for flavour and to stop them dissolving. The finished dish is savoury, sour and substantial.

What is eaten at the end.

Rajasthani sweets are ghee-heavy for the same reason everything else is. Ghevar is a flat lattice disc, made by trickling a thin flour batter into a deep pan of very hot ghee so it sets in threads around a ring, then soaked in syrup. It belongs to Teej and Rakshabandhan, and it is not a home recipe: it wants a tall narrow mould, a great deal of ghee held at an exact temperature, and practice. Mawa kachori, Jodhpur's own, is a fried pastry stuffed with milk solids and nuts and drowned in syrup.

Both are shop food, and both are worth ordering rather than attempting. What a home kitchen makes instead is churma, bati pounded with ghee and gur, which sits on the same plate as the dal rather than arriving after it.
