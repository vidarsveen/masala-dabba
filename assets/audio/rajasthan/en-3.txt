Laal maas, ker sangri and gatte.

Laal maas, ker sangri and gatte reveal three different parts of Rajasthan's pantry. One braises meat with curd and chilli, one preserves wild berries and pods, and one turns gram flour into substantial dumplings.

Laal maas means red meat and usually refers to goat or mutton. It is commonly linked to Rajput royal kitchens and hunting camps, although the origin story is better documented as culinary tradition than as a precise historical event. Curd, ghee, dried chilli, garlic and whole spices make a plausible travelling pantry.

Many versions add little or no water, relying on curd and moisture from the meat. Gentle cooking reduces the yoghurt while the fat rises to the surface. That visible separation is a useful sign that the masala has cooked, though tenderness remains the final test.

The chillies are stemmed, soaked in warm water and ground to a smooth paste. That paste, not powder shaken from a tin, is what gives the colour its depth. Garlic goes in whole or crushed, the whole spices are clove, cardamom and bay, and the ground spice is coriander, a good deal of it.

Two optional moves deepen the dish. Kachri powder in the marinade adds acidity and protease enzymes that can tenderise meat. Some cooks also use dhungar: a hot coal sits in a small bowl inside the pot, ghee is poured over it, and the lid traps the smoke briefly.

The counterpart dish is safed maas, white meat, from the same kitchens. Same goat, same curd, but the body comes from almonds, cashew and white poppy seed, and there is no red chilli at all. A court that could make one could make the other, and serving both at one meal was the point.

Sangri is the long, thin pod of the khejri, Prosopis cineraria. The drought-tolerant tree provides pods, fodder, shade and wood, and farmers often retain it in crop fields. Research on arid agroforestry shows that it can support soil fertility and farm resilience.

At Khejarli in 1730, Amrita Devi and other Bishnoi villagers resisted an order to cut khejri trees. Rajasthan's official account records 363 deaths before the felling stopped. The event remains a powerful symbol of Bishnoi environmental ethics and of the tree's place in the region.

Ker is the berry of Capparis decidua, a leafless thorn bush of the same family as the caper, and raw it is sharply bitter. Sangri is the khejri pod. Both are picked, boiled briefly and dried, and then they sit in a sack for as long as anyone needs them to.

Dried ker and sangri need washing, soaking and boiling before they are fried with spices. Recipes vary in soaking time and liquid; changing the water helps soften the ingredients and reduce bitterness. Red chilli, coriander, turmeric, hing and amchur are common seasonings, and some cooks add yoghurt.

The finished dish is chewy, sour, hot and comparatively dry. Its preserved ingredients and low-moisture cooking suit travel and advance preparation, but cooked ker sangri still requires safe storage like any prepared food.

Gatte ki sabzi answers a simple question. What do you serve when there is no vegetable in the house and no prospect of one? The answer is gram flour. Besan is kneaded stiff with yoghurt, oil, chilli, turmeric, coriander and a good pinch of ajwain, rolled into ropes the thickness of a finger, and boiled for about fifteen minutes until they firm up. The ropes are then sliced into discs, which are the gatte.

Some cooks reserve part of the starchy cooking water for the gravy, then add beaten yoghurt and a little besan. Others begin with fresh water. The sliced gatte may also be fried in ghee before they return to the sauce, which adds flavour and helps them hold their shape.

Rajasthani sweets are ghee-heavy for the same reason everything else is. Ghevar is a flat lattice disc, made by trickling a thin flour batter into a deep pan of very hot ghee so it sets in threads around a ring, then soaked in syrup. It belongs to Teej and Rakshabandhan, and it is not a home recipe: it wants a tall narrow mould, a great deal of ghee held at an exact temperature, and practice. Mawa kachori, Jodhpur's own, is a fried pastry stuffed with milk solids and nuts and drowned in syrup.

Both are shop food, and both are worth ordering rather than attempting. What a home kitchen makes instead is churma, bati pounded with ghee and gur, which sits on the same plate as the dal rather than arriving after it.
