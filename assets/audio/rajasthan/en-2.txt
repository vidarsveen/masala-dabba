Bajra, bati and the desert pantry.

A bajra roti is not rolled. The dough has no gluten to speak of, so a pin would tear it, and the cook slaps it from palm to palm until it is a thick disc the size of a hand. It goes on a griddle, then straight onto the embers, where it puffs and blisters. A hollow in the middle takes a lump of butter or a spoon of ghee, and that is the daily bread of the western districts.

The grain that grows on nothing.

Bajra, pearl millet, is the crop that made settlement in the Thar possible. It germinates within a few days of the first rain, ripens in about two and a half months, and it will set seed on a couple of hundred millimetres of water in soil hot enough to kill a wheat seedling. No other cereal of any consequence does that. India grows more pearl millet than any other country, and Rajasthan grows more of it than any other Indian state.

The grain itself is grey-green and slightly bitter, with far more oil than wheat, which is why bajra flour goes stale quickly and is ground in small amounts. It also means the bread sits heavily enough to carry a person through a working day. Rajasthan eats wheat too, in the irrigated east around Kota and Bharatpur, and maize in the hills of the south. The dry west eats millet.

The bread, and what goes on it.

Bajre ki roti is eaten hot, and it is eaten with something strong, because on its own it is plain and a little coarse. The usual partner is lehsun ki chutney, a paste of garlic and red chilli pounded together with salt, which looks alarming and is mostly garlic. In winter the bread goes with gur, raw cane sugar, and a knob of butter, and that combination is breakfast for a good part of rural Rajasthan.

The timing matters more than the recipe. A millet roti is good for about ten minutes and then turns hard, so the cook works at the fire and the family eats as the breads come off it, one at a time. Nobody stacks bajra rotis and carries them to the table, and that tells you how the kitchen is organised: the fire is the room, and the meal happens around it.

Bati, and why it is drowned.

Bati belongs to the wheat half of the state and to the road. It is a stiff dough of coarse wheat flour, salt and ghee, rolled into balls and baked hard in the embers of a dung fire, or buried in hot sand, or in a modern kitchen roasted in an oven. Nothing is added to make it rise, so it comes out dense, cracked and dry, and it keeps for days. A shepherd or a soldier could carry a bag of them.

The eating repairs the dryness. Each bati is cracked in half, dipped or drowned in melted ghee, and pushed into a thick dal. The third part of the plate is churma, which is bati crumbled and pounded with more ghee and with gur or sugar, and eaten with the same fingers as the savoury food rather than afterwards. A Rajasthani plate does not separate sweet from savoury the way a European one does, and that habit is worth noticing early, because it comes back at every meal.

Five pulses in one pot.

The dal under those batis is panchmel, which means five mixed, and the five are chana, moong, urad, toor and moth. Cooking them together is not showing off. Each pulse breaks down at a different rate, so the pot ends up with some lentils dissolved into the body of the dal and others still holding their shape, and the result has a texture no single pulse gives.

The last of the five is the local one. Moth bean is a sprawling desert pulse that will crop where almost nothing else will, and Rajasthan grows nearly all of India's supply. Sprouted, dried and fried in fine strands, it becomes Bikaneri bhujia, which is now sold across India in packets and carries a geographical indication naming the city it comes from.

Vegetables made months in advance.

Here is the part that has no European equivalent. A Rajasthani kitchen keeps a shelf of vegetables that were manufactured in advance, in the dry weeks before the monsoon, and they are treated as vegetables rather than as store-cupboard emergencies.

Mangodi are small dumplings of ground moong paste, dropped onto a cloth and dried in the sun until they rattle. Papad, the same discs a restaurant serves as a crisp, are simmered in a gravy here and eaten as a dish in their own right, papad ki sabzi, which is genuinely a main vegetable and not a joke. Ker, sangri, kumatia and gunda are gathered wild off desert trees and shrubs, dried, and stored in sacks. All of them go into the pot dry and come out as the vegetable course.

The logic is the same one running through the whole region. Nothing green lasts, so the vegetables are made when there is a surplus and eaten when there is not, and the kitchen learned to make dried pulse and dried berry taste like a meal rather than like a shortage.

How a meal is put together.

A working plate is bread, a wet dish, a dry dish and something sharp. The bread is bajra in the west and wheat in the east. The wet dish is dal or kadhi. The dry dish comes off the shelf just described, or it is a gram-flour dumpling. The sharp thing is chutney, chilli pickle or raw onion, and buttermilk arrives at the end, cold and salted.

Ghee runs through all of it, poured over the dal, rubbed into the bread, spooned onto the churma. A visitor often reads that as indulgence, and it is closer to the opposite. Where the calories in a meal could not be relied on to come from the fields, they came from the herd, and a household measured its comfort in how much ghee it could put on the table.
