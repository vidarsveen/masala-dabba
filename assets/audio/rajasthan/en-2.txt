Bajra, bati and the desert pantry.

A bajra roti is not rolled. The dough has no gluten to speak of, so a pin would tear it, and the cook slaps it from palm to palm until it is a thick disc the size of a hand. It goes on a griddle, then straight onto the embers, where it puffs and blisters. A hollow in the middle takes a lump of butter or a spoon of ghee, and that is the daily bread of the western districts.

Bajra, pearl millet, is exceptionally well suited to the Thar. Early cultivars can mature in roughly 65 to 90 days, and the crop tolerates heat and drought better than wheat. It is therefore central to rain-fed farming across western Rajasthan.

The grain itself is grey-green and slightly bitter, with far more oil than wheat, which is why bajra flour goes stale quickly and is ground in small amounts. It also means the bread sits heavily enough to carry a person through a working day. Rajasthan eats wheat too, in the irrigated east around Kota and Bharatpur, and maize in the hills of the south. The dry west eats millet.

Bajre ki roti is eaten hot, and it is eaten with something strong, because on its own it is plain and a little coarse. The usual partner is lehsun ki chutney, a paste of garlic and red chilli pounded together with salt, which looks alarming and is mostly garlic. In winter the bread goes with gur, raw cane sugar, and a knob of butter, and that combination is breakfast for a good part of rural Rajasthan.

The timing matters. Bajra roti firms as it cools, so it is often eaten hot and made during the meal. Some cooks stack the breads under a cloth or add ghee to keep them supple, but the freshest roti still has the softest texture.

Bati is a stiff dough of coarse wheat flour, salt and ghee, rolled into balls and traditionally baked in embers; modern kitchens use an oven. With no leavening, it emerges dense, cracked and dry, a form that travels and keeps better than soft bread.

The eating repairs the dryness. Each bati is cracked in half, dipped or drowned in melted ghee, and pushed into a thick dal. The third part of the plate is churma, which is bati crumbled and pounded with more ghee and with gur or sugar, and eaten with the same fingers as the savoury food rather than afterwards. A Rajasthani plate does not separate sweet from savoury the way a European one does, and that habit is worth noticing early, because it comes back at every meal.

The dal under those batis is panchmel, or a mixture of five pulses. A common combination is chana, moong, urad, toor and masoor, while some Rajasthani versions use local moth bean. Because the pulses soften at different rates, the finished dal can be both creamy and textured.

Moth bean is a drought-hardy pulse of the dry west. In Bikaneri bhujia, its flour is commonly mixed with gram flour and spices, worked into a dough, pressed into fine strands and fried. The snack's registered geographical indication links it to Bikaner.

A Rajasthani pantry may hold ingredients prepared well before they are needed, especially during dry weather. They become everyday building blocks rather than emergency substitutes.

Mangodi are small drops of seasoned pulse paste dried in the sun. Papad may be simmered in gravy as papad ki sabzi instead of served crisp. Ker, sangri, kumatia and gunda can be gathered from desert trees and shrubs, then dried and stored for later cooking.

Fresh produce is seasonal and unevenly available, especially in the dry west. Drying pulses, pods and berries turns a brief harvest into ingredients that can be cooked throughout the year.

A working plate is bread, a wet dish, a dry dish and something sharp. The bread is bajra in the west and wheat in the east. The wet dish is dal or kadhi. The dry dish comes off the shelf just described, or it is a gram-flour dumpling. The sharp thing is chutney, chilli pickle or raw onion, and buttermilk arrives at the end, cold and salted.

Ghee appears throughout the meal: over dal, in bread and in churma. It supplies concentrated energy and a flavour associated with hospitality, although access to dairy has always differed by place, season and household wealth.
