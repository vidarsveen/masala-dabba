Ilish, kosha mangsho and the sweets.

In late June the monsoon breaks over the Bay of Bengal, the rivers rise and the hilsa start to run. They spend their lives in salt water and swim upstream to spawn, and a fish caught in the river after weeks of that journey is fat, silver and worth a great deal of money. The first ilish of the season is news. People telephone each other about it.

Ilish, and the bones.

Ilish, or hilsa, is Tenualosa ilisha, and it is the most prized fish in the region by a distance. It is also one of the boniest fish anybody eats anywhere, full of fine forked bones that run through the flesh rather than sitting neatly along a spine. Bengal tolerates this because the fish is extremely oily, and that oil carries flavour in a way no lean fish can.

The cooking follows from the fat. Ilish is never washed once it has been cut, because the oil that makes it worth eating rinses straight off. It is not fried hard. The two famous treatments are shorshe ilish, steaks in a raw mustard and green chilli paste, and bhapa ilish, the same idea sealed in a covered tin and steamed. Both cook the fish gently in its own oil, and both take about fifteen minutes. Nothing is browned and almost nothing is added.

The season and the supply are now genuinely fragile. Bangladesh registered the hilsa of the Padma as a geographical indication in 2017, and enforces closed seasons and a ban on catching juveniles, because the stock has been fished hard for decades. Prices in Kolkata track the politics of the border as much as the catch.

There is no honest substitute in Norway. Hilsa is not sold here, frozen or fresh, and the fish it most resembles in fat content is mackerel. Mackerel in a mustard paste is a good dish. It is not shorshe ilish, and the recipe in this course says so plainly.

Macher jhol, and the potato in it.

The everyday fish, though, is not hilsa. It is rui or katla, two big carp raised in ponds all over the delta, and the everyday dish is macher jhol, which simply means fish in gravy. Steaks are rubbed with turmeric and salt, fried briefly in mustard oil, and lifted out. Nigella, green chilli and ginger go into the same oil, then turmeric, then water, and the fish goes back in for a few minutes at the end.

The gravy is thin on purpose. It is meant to soak into rice rather than coat anything, and a Bengali cook judges a jhol by its clarity rather than its body. There is nearly always a potato in it too, cut into thick wedges and cooked until it breaks under a finger.

That potato is worth noticing. Bengal puts potato in fish curry, in biryani, in luchi's companion dish and in the vegetable dishes, more consistently than anywhere else in India. The potato is a South American plant, and it reached this coast on European ships about four hundred years ago. The next reading follows that story. Bengal took the potato up more thoroughly than the Europeans who brought it.

Kosha mangsho: a verb, not a noun.

Kosha means to fry something down, slowly, with very little liquid, until the moisture has gone and the fat separates. Kosha mangsho is goat cooked that way, and the name describes the technique rather than the ingredients. Onion, ginger, garlic and yoghurt go in with the meat. Then the cook stands over the pan for the best part of an hour, adds a splash of water whenever it threatens to catch, and fries it off again.

Each of those cycles browns the onion a little further and builds another layer, and the finished dish has almost no free liquid: what is left is dark, thick and stuck to the meat. Kolkata treats it as a Sunday dish, cooked slowly through the morning and eaten with luchi or with plain rice. It is also one of the few Bengali dishes where a heavy garam masala is welcome.

The meat is goat, not lamb. Indian recipes in English call goat "mutton", which confuses everyone outside India at least once. Goat is leaner, firmer and stronger in flavour, and the long kosha is partly there to deal with that firmness.

The sweets, and the argument about them.

Now the part the region is most famous for. Most Indian sweets are built on khoa, milk boiled down to a solid, or on flour and sugar syrup. The east is built on something else: chhana, fresh milk curds, split with acid, drained and then worked by hand until the grain disappears and the mass turns smooth. Almost every sweet in this reading starts there.

Rasgulla is chhana rolled into balls and simmered in thin sugar syrup until the balls swell and turn spongy. Sandesh is chhana kneaded with sugar and set, often pressed into a carved wooden mould. The best of it is flavoured with nolen gur, a date-palm jaggery that arrives for a few weeks each winter, and Bengalis wait for it the way Europeans wait for asparagus. Chhena poda, from Odisha, mixes chhana with sugar and semolina and bakes it until the outside goes almost black. The name means burnt cheese, and the caramelised crust is the whole point.

Who invented the rasgulla is a live dispute between two states, and both have a case. Bengal credits Nobin Chandra Das, a confectioner in north Calcutta, with the modern white rosogolla in the 1860s. Odisha argues that chhana sweets were offered at the Jagannath temple in Puri long before that, and that its own browner, softer rasagola is the older thing. The dispute went through India's geographical indications registry and produced two separate tags: Banglar Rasogolla in 2017, Odisha Rasagola in 2019. They are registered as different products, which is the honest outcome, and this course does not pick a side.

The one sweet that is not chhana is mishti doi, and it works on a different principle entirely. Milk is boiled down, sweetened with caramelised sugar or with jaggery, cooled to blood heat, stirred with a spoon of live yoghurt and poured into unglazed clay pots to set overnight. The pot is doing real work: it wicks water out through its wall, so the curd thickens as it sets and ends up dense enough to hold a spoon upright.
