Ilish, kosha mangsho and the sweets.

Hilsa migrate between marine and river environments, and their movement, availability and price follow water conditions, fishing rules and trade. The fish carries strong cultural value in Bengal and Bangladesh, especially during monsoon-season celebrations.

Ilish, or hilsa, is Tenualosa ilisha. Its rich flesh contains many fine forked bones, so eating it requires attention. Its oiliness is central to the flavour, but claims that cut fish must never be washed confuse culinary preference with safe cleaning and handling.

Two well-known preparations are shorshe ilish, hilsa with mustard and green chilli, and bhapa ilish, cooked with mustard in a covered or wrapped preparation. Recipes differ in frying, steaming, coconut, yoghurt and mustard ratios. The fish should be cooked safely without drying out its fatty flesh.

Hilsa stocks and migration routes face fishing pressure and river barriers. Conservation measures protect juveniles and spawning fish, while availability in Kolkata also depends on catches and cross-border trade. These ecological and livelihood questions matter more than a single origin label.

Hilsa may be difficult to source in Norway. Oily fish such as mackerel can demonstrate the mustard technique, but it has a different texture and flavour. The recipe names that adaptation clearly.

Rui and katla, two widely farmed carps, often appear in macher jhol, a broad category of fish in a light gravy. In one common method the fish is seasoned and fried briefly, then returned to a gravy with vegetables and a tempering. Species and seasonings vary.

Jhol is often thinner than a sauce meant to cling to meat, because it is mixed with rice. Potato, aubergine, pointed gourd, tomato and other vegetables can appear according to the fish and household. Clarity and thickness are preferences rather than universal tests.

Potato became important across Bengali cooking after the American crop reached South Asia through European trade. It now appears in fish curries, vegetable dishes, alur dom and Kolkata biryani, but none of these dishes requires it in every version.

Kosha describes slow cooking and browning with limited liquid. In kosha mangsho, meat and aromatics are cooked down until a dark, clinging gravy develops. Onion, ginger, garlic, yoghurt and spice choices vary, as do pressure-cooker and stovetop methods.

Repeated reduction can deepen colour and flavour, while enough water remains to tenderise the meat. The dish is associated with celebratory or leisurely meals and may be served with luchi, paratha, pulao or rice. Garam masala formulas and intensity vary.

In Indian English, “mutton” often refers to goat, but it may also refer to sheep depending on place and seller. The course recipe specifies goat and gives lamb shoulder as an adaptation, with cooking time adjusted to the cut.

Fresh acid-set curds called chhana or chhena support many eastern sweets alongside traditions based on reduced milk, grains, pulses and flour. The curds are drained and worked to suit the sweet: smooth for some recipes, looser or firmer for others.

Rasgulla is chhana rolled into balls and simmered in thin sugar syrup until the balls swell and turn spongy. Sandesh is chhana kneaded with sugar and set, sometimes pressed into a carved wooden mould. Seasonal nolen gur, a date-palm jaggery, gives some versions a distinctive caramel and floral flavour. Chhena poda, from Odisha, mixes chhana with sugar and semolina and bakes it until a dark caramelised crust forms.

Origin stories for rasgulla are disputed. Bengal associates the modern white rosogolla with Nobin Chandra Das in nineteenth-century Calcutta, while Odisha points to its own rasagola tradition and temple narratives. India's registry recognises Banglar Rasogolla and Odisha Rasagola as separate geographical indications; registration defines products and regions, not a final verdict on invention.

Mishti doi shows another branch of the sweet repertoire. Milk is boiled down, sweetened with caramelised sugar or jaggery, cooled to about 43–45 °C and inoculated with live yoghurt. It cultures in a warm place until set, then goes promptly into the refrigerator. Food-safe unglazed clay may draw out some moisture, but clean glass or ceramic also works.
