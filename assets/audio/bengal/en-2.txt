Rice and the order of a Bengali meal.

Sit down to a proper Bengali lunch and nothing arrives all at once. A mound of rice goes down in the middle of the plate first. Then a server brings one dish and waits while you eat it with a portion of that rice, takes the bowl away and brings the next. The sequence is fixed. It starts with something bitter and ends with something sweet, and a Bengali host will correct you if you eat out of turn.

Why rice, and so much of it.

The Ganges and the Brahmaputra meet here and empty into the Bay of Bengal, and between them they have built the largest river delta on earth. The land is flat, the silt is renewed every year and the monsoon drops well over a metre and a half of rain on it. Those are the conditions rice wants, and West Bengal grows more of it than any other Indian state.

Three crops share the year. Aus is sown before the monsoon and cut in late summer, aman is the big monsoon crop harvested in November and December, and boro is grown in the dry season on irrigated land. A Bengali household therefore eats new rice at several points in the year, and an old harvest and a new one are not treated as the same thing.

Nor is one grain the same as another. Gobindobhog is a short, round, aromatic rice from Burdwan, used for khichuri and for the milk pudding called payesh. Atap rice is sun-dried and used for ritual cooking and for sweets. Sidhho rice is parboiled, which is what most people eat daily, and it stands up to a wet curry the way a polished grain does not.

Yesterday's rice, on purpose.

One dish deserves its own paragraph, because it is eaten across all four states and is almost unknown outside them. Cover last night's cooked rice with water, leave it overnight at room temperature, and by morning it has fermented very slightly and turned faintly sour. Bengal calls it panta bhat and Odisha calls it pakhala. It is eaten cool, with salt, raw onion, green chilli and a spoonful of raw mustard oil, and with a fried fish if there is one.

It began as a way of keeping cooked rice edible through a hot night without a fridge, and it turned into something people genuinely want. Odisha likes it enough to hold a day for it every March. It is also, incidentally, one of the few genuinely probiotic dishes in Indian cooking, which is a modern reason for a very old habit.

The order, and why it is that order.

A formal Bengali meal runs in a set order. It opens with something bitter, almost always shukto, a milky stew of vegetables built around bitter gourd. Dal follows, and a fried vegetable comes alongside it. Then the dry vegetable dishes arrive, the torkari and the ghonto. Fish comes next, with the light gravies ahead of the rich ones, and meat after the fish if the household is serving meat. A sour chutney and a papad close the savoury half, and a sweet ends the meal.

The logic is that bitterness opens the appetite and sourness closes it. That comes from Ayurveda rather than from any modern nutrition, but the sequence works on its own terms. A plain bitter dish eaten first makes everything after it taste stronger, and a sharp tamarind chutney at the end genuinely does stop you eating. Rice goes with every stage, in small handfuls, so the meal is really one ingredient with six or seven different partners.

Bengali frying deserves a note of its own. Bhaja means fried, and a Bengali meal usually carries one: a slice of aubergine dipped in turmeric and salt and fried in mustard oil, or slivers of potato fried until they rattle. It is eaten with the dal, not as a starter, and it exists to give the soft part of the meal something to crunch against.

Bread, where bread happens.

Rice does not have the east to itself. Luchi is a small puffed bread of refined wheat flour, fried until it balloons, and Bengal eats it at festivals and on Sunday mornings rather than daily. It is paler and softer than a north Indian puri because the flour is refined rather than wholemeal, and it comes with a spiced potato, alur dom, or with a sweetened chana dal.

Go west into Bihar and wheat stops being an occasional treat. The dish that defines the state is litti chokha. A cook stuffs balls of wheat dough with roasted gram flour, ajwain, garlic and mustard oil, then bakes them in the embers of a fire until the crust blisters and cracks. They are broken open, dipped in ghee, and eaten with chokha, a coarse mash of roasted aubergine, potato and tomato dressed with raw mustard oil and green chilli.

Litti travels well, keeps for a day and needs no pan, which is why it began as field food and market food. It is now sold on the pavement in Kolkata and Delhi as much as in Patna, and it is the one dish from Bihar that everybody in India can name.

How it is eaten.

With the fingers of the right hand, and with a specific technique. Rice and gravy are mixed in the fingertips, pressed into a loose ball against the plate and lifted with the thumb pushing from behind. A fish is stripped off its bones on the plate with the same hand, which is a skill Bengali children acquire early and visitors generally do not. Bones are pushed to the rim of the plate as you go.

None of this is ceremony for its own sake. Mixing by hand lets you choose the ratio of rice to gravy in every single mouthful, which is exactly what a meal of one staple and seven partners needs.
