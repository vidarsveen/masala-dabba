Panch phoron, mustard and the whole-spice rule.

Mustard oil is warmed until its raw aroma softens. A spoonful of whole spices goes in, and the pan answers at once: cumin crackles, fenugreek darkens and fennel releases its sweet scent. This tempering is one important Bengali technique, though dishes use many other beginnings too.

The course map groups West Bengal, Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha. Bengal supplies much of the vocabulary here, but the other states have distinct crop patterns, communities and food histories. Bihar's sattu, Odisha's temple food and pakhala, and Jharkhand's farmed and gathered foods need their own context.

Panch phoron means five spices. A common Bengali formula combines cumin, nigella, fenugreek, fennel and radhuni, while other formulas use mustard seed instead of radhuni or adjust the proportions. Odisha's related pancha phutana also varies. The mixture is normally kept whole for tempering.

Whole seeds release their aromas at different rates and remain distinct in the finished dish. Fenugreek can turn bitter quickly, while fennel stays sweet and aromatic. A cook therefore watches the pan and moves on before the smallest or most fragile seeds scorch.

The mixture seasons the cooking fat, which then carries its aroma into the ingredients that follow. It often goes in near the beginning, though Bengali dishes also use single-seed temperings and finishing temperings. The technique belongs to a wider South Asian family rather than one rigid regional rule.

Mustard appears both as oil and as ground seed. Cold-pressed mustard oil has a pungent aroma associated with isothiocyanates, compounds also found in horseradish and wasabi. Many cooks heat it before frying, while some preparations use a little raw oil as a finishing flavour.

Shorshe bata is a paste of mustard seed, green chilli, salt and water. Seed colour, soaking, grinding and cooking time all affect its heat and bitterness. This course grinds the paste briefly and cooks it gently, but household methods and mustard ratios vary.

Kasundi is a sharp mustard condiment with several regional and commercial forms. Historical accounts describe seasonal household preparation and ritual rules in some communities. Bottled versions are now served with fried fish, fritters and vegetables; their fermentation, souring and spice formulas are not all identical.

The small black seed in the mix is nigella, Nigella sativa, called kalonji in much of India and kalo jeere in Bengali. Its savoury, slightly bitter aroma appears in fish curries, pickles, breads and vegetable dishes, either alone or in panch phoron.

Shop labels can be unreliable. Nigella is distinct from black cumin, black sesame, caraway and onion seed, although those names sometimes appear on packets. Black sesame is smooth, flat and oval, and tastes nutty. Nigella is angular, matt and slightly resinous. Checking the seed itself prevents a very different flavour from entering the dish.

Another prominent ingredient is posto, white poppy seed, ground to a thick pale paste. It has a mild, nutty flavour and gives a dish body as well as seasoning. Aloo posto cooks potato in that paste, often with green chilli and mustard oil; recipes may add a tempering or other aromatics.

Posto is especially prominent in parts of Bengal and Bihar. The East India Company's opium system linked poppy cultivation to Bihar and eastern India, but that history does not by itself prove why culinary poppy seed became popular. The edible seed and the latex collected from an unripe capsule are different products of the same plant.

Many Bengali recipes favour whole tempering spices and wet pastes, while others use ground cumin, coriander or a garam masala. A familiar Bengali garam masala centres cardamom, cinnamon and clove, but its proportions and the moment it is added vary.

This gives cooks several ways to keep a leading ingredient clear. Aloo posto foregrounds poppy seed, shorshe ilish mustard, and a light macher jhol fish and its tempering. The useful lesson is choice and restraint, not a ban on dry masala.
