Panch phoron, mustard and the whole-spice rule.

A Bengali cook heats mustard oil until it stops smelling raw and a faint haze rises off it. Then a spoonful of five seeds goes in, and the pan answers at once: the mustard seeds jump, the fenugreek darkens, and the whole kitchen smells of fennel and something like burnt onion. That single moment is where most Bengali savoury dishes begin, and almost nothing in it has been ground.

This module covers the eastern quarter of India, which means West Bengal, Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha. Bengal anchors it, because Bengal's cooking is the most codified and the most written about, but the other three are not footnotes. Bihar eats wheat and roasted gram where Bengal eats rice. Odisha has a temple kitchen that feeds thousands a day. Jharkhand cooks forest food that has no equivalent anywhere else in the country. Where they diverge from Bengal, this course says so.

Five seeds, never ground.

Panch phoron means five spices, and the five are cumin, nigella, fenugreek, fennel and radhuni. They are mixed whole in roughly equal parts and stored that way, and no Bengali cook grinds them. Outside Bengal the mixture usually swaps black mustard seed for radhuni, which is easier to buy, and Odisha's version, pancha phutana, almost always does.

The rule against grinding is not fussiness. The five seeds behave completely differently in hot fat. Fenugreek turns bitter within seconds and has to be watched; fennel needs longer and stays sweet; nigella barely changes at all. Ground together they would all cook at the same rate and taste of nothing in particular. Left whole, they stay separate, and you find a fennel seed in a mouthful of potato three days later and get a burst of aniseed out of it.

The mix is also a seasoning of the fat rather than of the dish. The seeds give their oil-soluble aromatics to the oil in about thirty seconds, and the oil then carries that flavour into everything that follows. South India does the same thing with its tempering, but it uses a different pantry and a different moment. In Bengal the phoron almost always goes in first, at the bottom of the pan, before the vegetables.

Mustard, twice over.

Mustard enters a Bengali kitchen in two completely separate ways, and confusing them is the commonest mistake an outsider makes. The first is the oil. Cold-pressed mustard oil is pungent enough to catch the back of the throat, and that pungency comes from allyl isothiocyanate, the same compound that makes wasabi sting. Bengali cooks heat the oil hard before anything else goes in, which drives that raw edge off and leaves a nutty warmth behind. Skip that step and the finished dish tastes of the bottle.

The second is the seed, ground raw. Shorshe bata is a paste of mustard seed, green chilli and a little salt, ground with cold water, and it is what makes shorshe ilish taste the way it does. It turns viciously bitter if you mishandle it, and two things cause that. Grinding it too long heats it, and heat with water present sets off the enzyme that produces the bitter compounds. Cooking the paste hard does the same. So the paste is ground quickly, with salt and a pinch of turmeric to hold the bitterness down, and it goes into the dish near the end and is barely cooked at all.

A third form sits between the two. Kasundi is a fermented mustard condiment, sharp and salty, somewhere between a mustard and a fish sauce in its function. In the old practice it was made once a year in spring, by women, under strict rules of ritual purity, and the jars were watched for weeks. Today it is made in factories and sold in bottles, and it turns up beside fried fish, beside fritters, and stirred into a plate of cold greens. It has nothing to do with the tomato relish sold as kasundi in Australia, which borrowed the name and left the mustard behind.

Nigella, and what it is not.

The small black seed in the mix is nigella, Nigella sativa, called kalonji in most of India and kalo jeere in Bengali. It tastes of oregano and toasted onion with a faint bitterness underneath, and Bengal uses it far more than the rest of India does. It goes into fish curry, into pickles, into the dough for some breads, and into the oil at the start of almost any dish of potatoes or greens.

It is worth being blunt about the names, because shop labels are unreliable. Nigella is not black cumin, not black sesame, not caraway and not onion seed, though it is sold as all four somewhere in the world. Black sesame is smooth, flat and oval, and tastes nutty. Nigella is angular, matt and slightly resinous. Buy the wrong one and the flavour you were after will simply not be there.

Poppy seed, and where it came from.

The last of the region's signature ingredients is posto, white poppy seed, ground to a thick pale paste. It has almost no flavour of its own and a great deal of texture, and Bengal treats it as a body rather than a seasoning. Aloo posto is diced potato cooked in that paste with green chilli and mustard oil, and it is one of the plainest dishes in Indian cooking and one of the most loved.

Bengal and Bihar eat far more poppy seed than the rest of India, and the usual explanation is colonial. The East India Company ran its opium monopoly out of Patna and Benares, and for a century the poppy fields of Bihar and western Bengal supplied the trade with China. Opium comes from the unripe pod, and the seed is the leftover: cheap, local and legal. Historians argue about how much weight that explanation carries, but the geography lines up too neatly to ignore.

In the pantry: panch phoron.

 Aroma: Aniseed from the fennel, warm earth from the cumin, a bitter maple note from the fenugreek.

 Flavour: Mostly a background, until you bite a seed and get one flavour on its own.

 What it does: Seasons the oil in the first thirty seconds, so the fat carries the flavour into everything after.

 When to add: Whole, into hot oil, before anything else. Never ground, never at the end.

 Instead of it: Mix your own from equal parts of the five seeds; celery seed stands in for radhuni at a pinch.

Why so little dry masala.

A north Indian kitchen keeps jars of ground coriander, ground cumin and garam masala, and builds a gravy out of them. A Bengali kitchen keeps almost none of that. It keeps whole seed for the phoron, a stone for grinding wet pastes, and a small tin of what Bengal calls garam masala. That last one is only cardamom, cinnamon and clove, and it usually goes in whole and late.

The consequence is a set of dishes that each taste clearly of one or two things. Aloo posto tastes of poppy seed. Shorshe ilish tastes of mustard. Macher jhol tastes of fish, turmeric and nigella. The technique is built to keep flavours apart, which is the opposite of what a masala does, and it is the most useful idea to carry into the next three readings.
