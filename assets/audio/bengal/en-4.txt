Chhana, the Portuguese and a Nawab in exile.

Cross Howrah Bridge on foot at eight in the morning and the crowd carries you. Below it the Hooghly runs south towards the sea, and that river carried the people and goods behind this history. Ships came up it, and each set of arrivals left something in the kitchen that outlasted whatever they came for.

The Portuguese reached Bengal in the sixteenth century and settled at Hooghly, upriver from where Calcutta would later stand, with the Mughal court's permission from about 1579. They traded, they built a church at Bandel in 1599, they raided, and they dealt in slaves. In 1632 Shah Jahan's army besieged the settlement for three months and destroyed it. Within a year a new imperial order let them return, and the church was rebuilt.

Portuguese contact left traces in Bengali vocabulary and food history. Words including almari, janla and girja are commonly linked to Portuguese, while the exact history of words such as pauruti deserves caution. Bandel cheese, a salted fresh or smoked cheese associated with the Hooghly area, is also connected to this encounter.

A much-repeated claim says the Portuguese taught Bengal to split milk deliberately. In this account, curdling milk was ritually unacceptable in Hindu practice, so sweets relied on reduced milk. European cheesemakers then supposedly broke the taboo and introduced chhana.

The story is contested. The twelfth-century Manasollasa describes acid-curdled milk solids used in food, showing that the technique existed in India before Portuguese settlement in Bengal. That does not by itself trace modern chhana sweets. Portuguese contact may have influenced local cheesemaking, but the scale and mechanism remain debated.

Maritime exchange carried American crops including chilli, potato, tomato, papaya, cashew and guava into South Asia through several ports and over time. Portuguese networks were important in this movement. Bengal later made potato central to many curries, vegetable dishes, breads' accompaniments and its regional biryani style.

In 1856 the East India Company deposed Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, and annexed his kingdom. He later settled at Metiabruz near Calcutta and lived there until 1887. His household sustained Awadhi music, performance and food traditions, including cooks familiar with sealed-pot dum cookery.

Kolkata biryani developed from this Awadhi inheritance and is now strongly associated with a large potato; some servings also include egg. Stories that the potato stretched a reduced budget or represented a prized novelty are not documented by the Nawab's cooks. The connection to Metiabruz is firmer than any single explanation for the potato.

Many Kolkata versions use restrained spice and floral aromas such as kewra or rose water. Recipes differ across restaurants and homes. The dish records migration and local adaptation over generations rather than the unchanged invention of one household.

Bengal is not the whole of this region, and the older history belongs elsewhere. Pataliputra, now Patna, was the capital of the Mauryan empire in the fourth century BC, and the Buddha taught in what is now Bihar. Odisha's coast was Kalinga, whose conquest by Ashoka in about 261 BC was bloody enough that he said in his own inscriptions that it changed him.

Odisha's coast carried maritime trade across the Bay of Bengal, remembered in the boat-floating festival of Boita Bandana. At Puri, the Jagannath temple kitchen cooks offerings over wood fires in stacked earthen pots. The institution serves many pilgrims, but it should not stand in for all Odia food.

Jharkhand became a state in 2000 after separation from Bihar. Its many Adivasi and non-Adivasi communities combine rice, millets, pulses, cultivated vegetables, livestock and seasonal forest produce in different ways. Farming, gathering and markets overlap; no single forest menu represents the state.

The plate records exchange, migration and local choice. American crops travelled through maritime networks; Awadhi cooks helped shape Kolkata biryani; Bengal and Odisha maintain distinct rasgulla traditions. Mustard, whole-spice tempering and meal sequences also changed across communities and time, so none belongs to a timeless, uniform delta.
