Chhana, the Portuguese and a Nawab in exile.

Cross Howrah Bridge on foot at eight in the morning and the crowd carries you. Below it the Hooghly runs south towards the sea, and that river is the reason everything in this reading happened. Ships came up it, and each set of arrivals left something in the kitchen that outlasted whatever they came for.

The Portuguese, and the split-milk story.

The Portuguese reached Bengal in the sixteenth century and settled at Hooghly, upriver from where Calcutta would later stand, with the Mughal court's permission from about 1579. They traded, they built a church at Bandel in 1599, they raided, and they dealt in slaves. In 1632 Shah Jahan's army besieged the settlement for three months and destroyed it. Within a year a new imperial order let them return, and the church was rebuilt.

Their lasting mark is not military. It sits in the language and on the plate. Bengali took a set of everyday words from Portuguese and never gave them back. A loaf of bread is pauruti, from pão. A cupboard is an almari, a bucket is a balti, a window is a janla and a church is a girja. A pineapple is an ananas. A smoked, salty cheese called Bandel cheese is still made by a handful of families and sold in Kolkata's New Market, and it descends directly from Portuguese cheesemaking on the Hooghly.

And then there is the claim that gets repeated in every article about Bengali sweets: that the Portuguese taught Bengal to split milk on purpose. The argument runs that deliberately curdling milk was ritually unacceptable in Hindu practice, so Bengal made its sweets from reduced milk instead, and that European cheesemakers broke the taboo and handed the region chhana.

It is a good story and it is contested. The Manasollasa, a twelfth-century Sanskrit compendium written well before any Portuguese ship rounded Africa, describes making something very like chhana. Odisha's temple tradition treats chhana offerings as old. And food historians point out that Bengal's sweet-makers were a professional caste, with Muslim confectioners among them, who were not bound by the domestic rules the taboo argument depends on. So the safest version is this: chhana was known in India before the Portuguese arrived, and something about the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries made Bengal build an entire tradition on it. How much credit Europe deserves for that is not settled.

What else came off the ships.

What is not in doubt is the produce. The potato, the chilli, the tomato, the papaya, the cashew and the guava are all American plants, and they reached India on Portuguese ships during the sixteenth century. Bengal took the potato harder than almost anywhere. It went into the fish curry, into the dry vegetables, into the biryani, and into a dish of its own, alur dom, that a whole Sunday breakfast is built around.

A king, a suburb and a potato.

In February 1856 the East India Company deposed Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, and annexed his kingdom. He travelled to Calcutta to protest, was detained, and eventually settled at Metiabruz on the southern edge of the city. He stayed thirty years. He brought his court with him and rebuilt a small Lucknow on the riverbank, with musicians, poets, dancers, a menagerie and a season of kite-flying. He also brought his cooks, who were trained in the Awadhi method called dum pukht. They sealed meat and rice into a pot and let it cook slowly in its own steam.

Kolkata biryani comes from that kitchen, and one thing separates it from every other Indian biryani. A whole potato sits in it, soft and stained yellow, and usually a boiled egg beside it. Two explanations circulate. One says the exiled Nawab's purse was thin and potato stretched the meat. The other says potato was an expensive novelty in the Calcutta of the 1850s, and a deposed king with a pension was exactly the sort of person to put a novelty in his rice. Both are told confidently by people who cannot know, and the honest answer is that we do not have his cook's account of it.

The biryani that resulted is lighter than Hyderabad's, less fiery, and scented with rose water, kewra and a small amount of whole garam masala. It is the clearest example in the course of a cuisine arriving with a single household and staying for good.

Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha: the rest of the east.

Bengal is not the whole of this region, and the older history belongs elsewhere. Pataliputra, now Patna, was the capital of the Mauryan empire in the fourth century BC, and the Buddha taught in what is now Bihar. Odisha's coast was Kalinga, whose conquest by Ashoka in about 261 BC was bloody enough that he said in his own inscriptions that it changed him.

That coast traded east for centuries. Ships left Odisha for Java, Sumatra and Bali on the winter winds, and Odisha still marks the anniversary of their departure by floating small paper boats on the water. The kitchen most people encounter in Odisha, though, is the one at the Jagannath temple in Puri. Several hundred cooks there feed thousands of pilgrims a day, over wood fires, in unglazed clay pots stacked one above another. What they cook is sold afterwards in a market beside the temple.

Jharkhand, carved out of southern Bihar in 2000, cooks differently again, because a large part of its population is Adivasi and its food comes out of forest rather than farm. People there eat wild mushrooms that fruit after the first rains, bread made from finger millet, and leaves and tubers that never reach a city market. It is the least documented cooking in this module and the least like anything else in India.

Why any of this is in a cookery course.

Because the plate is a record of who came up the river and who stayed. The potato in the fish curry is Portuguese. The potato in the biryani is a deposed king's. The white rosogolla is a Calcutta confectioner's, or a Puri temple's, depending on whom you ask and which of the two geographical indications you are holding. And the mustard oil, the five whole seeds and the order of the courses are older than any of them, and belong to the delta itself.
