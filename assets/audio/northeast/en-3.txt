Tenga, khar and the smoked pork.

A pot of masor tenga can be spare: fish in a light broth sharpened with tomato, elephant apple, thekera or another souring ingredient. It is one Assamese approach among many, and its clear structure makes the sourness easy to understand.

Tenga means sour in Assamese, and masor tenga is a family of sour fish dishes. In this course version, fish is seasoned, fried briefly in mustard oil and returned to a light broth with fenugreek and tomato. Other recipes change the tempering, souring ingredient and thickness.

What sours it varies with the season and the household. Outenga, the elephant apple, is the classic: a heavy green fruit whose fleshy sepals are cut away and simmered in the broth. Thekera, dried slices of a garcinia, does the same job and keeps all year. Tomato is the everyday answer, lemon is the quick one, and a few households use fermented bamboo shoot or a wild sour leaf instead.

Brief cooking protects the fish from drying out. The course recipe omits a heavy onion paste, cream and garam masala so that fish, sourness and mustard oil remain clear, but it should not be mistaken for the only valid formula.

The alkaline liquid kolakhar gives its name to a family of dishes. Raw papaya, pulses, vegetables or fish head may cook with a small quantity of it. Strength varies by preparation, so the course uses a measured substitute rather than asking learners to make ash extract without a tested concentration.

Alkali softens plant tissue and gives the broth a smooth, faintly alkaline feel. Served with rice near the beginning of a formal meal, khar can provide a restrained contrast to later dishes. Baking soda reproduces part of the pH effect but not the flavour of banana ash.

Smoked pork appears in many Naga food traditions, though practices differ among communities and modern households. Hearth smoking can create a dark, firm ingredient for later cooking. Commercial smoked pork may be brine-cured and needs different soaking, storage and seasoning.

Fermented bamboo shoot adds acidity and aroma, while axone adds a strong savoury flavour. Both can cook with pork, but there is no single Naga formula. Some Ao dishes use anishi, fermented and dried colocasia leaf cakes. Ginger, chilli, herbs and other ingredients depend on the community and cook.

These dishes can concentrate attention on smoke, fermentation and the character of the meat. A short ingredient list still requires control of salt, sourness, chilli and tenderness; simplicity here describes the construction, not the skill or the cuisine.

In Meitei cooking, ngari seasons dishes including eromba. Boiled vegetables such as potato, beans or seasonal greens are mashed with chilli and fermented fish, then finished with herbs. Serve it promptly, or refrigerate it once cool rather than leaving fish and cooked vegetables out for long periods.

Some versions use yongchak, the aromatic tree bean. Eromba may share a meal with chamthong, a light vegetable stew, or singju, a shredded vegetable preparation with varied dressings. Recipes and the use of frying or chilli differ across households.

Meghalaya includes Khasi, Pnar/Jaintia, Garo and other food traditions. Khasi jadoh cooks rice with meat, often pork, and aromatics; formulas and rice varieties vary. Ja doh snam includes pig's blood. The course recipe is a blood-free version and names that choice clearly.

Other Khasi dishes include doh khleh, boiled pork tossed with raw onion and other seasonings, and tungrymbai, a fermented soybean preparation. Recipes for both vary. Black sesame may add body and a toasted flavour to fermented soybean dishes.

Mizoram's bai is a varied family of vegetable dishes that may use rice water, alkali, fermented soya, pork or sa-um. In Tripura, fermented fish called berma seasons dishes including gudok, whose preparation is not limited to one vessel or recipe.

Sikkim's foodways include Nepali, Bhutia, Lepcha and other traditions. Momo and thukpa sit alongside gundruk, fermented soybean kinema, rice, millet and churpi. These foods reflect Himalayan movement and exchange as well as local adaptation.
