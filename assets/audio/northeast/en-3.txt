Tenga, khar and the smoked pork.

A pot of masor tenga looks like nothing. The broth is thin and pale yellow, a few pieces of fish sit in it, there is a tomato breaking down at the edge, and that is all. Then you taste it, and it is sharply sour, clean and light, with no chilli heat and no grease anywhere. It is the dish that explains Assam, and it takes about twenty minutes.

Masor tenga: sour, thin and last.

Tenga simply means sour in Assamese, and masor tenga is sour fish. A freshwater fish is cut into steaks, turned in turmeric and salt and fried for a minute in mustard oil. Fenugreek seed goes into the same oil, then the souring agent, then water, and the fish goes back in at the end. The broth is never thickened and never reduced hard.

What sours it varies with the season and the household. Outenga, the elephant apple, is the classic: a heavy green fruit whose fleshy sepals are cut away and simmered in the broth. Thekera, dried slices of a garcinia, does the same job and keeps all year. Tomato is the everyday answer, lemon is the quick one, and a few households use fermented bamboo shoot or a wild sour leaf instead.

Two rules make the difference between a tenga and a fish soup. It is cooked fast, because a long simmer turns the fish woolly and the sourness flat. And it carries no garam masala, no onion paste and no cream, so what you taste is fish, sour fruit and mustard oil, in that order.

Khar, the dish that opens the meal.

The alkaline liquid from the first reading gives its name to a whole family of dishes. A khar is anything cooked with that liquid, and the commonest one is simple: raw papaya or pulses, sometimes a fish head, simmered in water with a spoonful of kolakhar and very little else.

It tastes of almost nothing on its own and it is meant to. The texture is the event: the alkali softens everything in the pot and gives the broth a smooth, faintly soapy body that no acid can produce. Eaten first, with plain rice and a raw chilli, it is a deliberately quiet start to a meal that gets louder afterwards. A household that has run out of kolakhar will use a pinch of baking soda instead, and every Assamese cook will tell you that it is not the same.

The Naga larder: smoke, axone and bamboo.

Nagaland cooks pork more than anything else, and it cooks it out of a larder rather than off a butcher's counter. Pork is cut into slabs, salted lightly and hung over the hearth, where weeks of smoke dry it hard. When a family wants to eat, a piece comes down, goes into water and simmers until it softens again.

What goes in with it decides the dish. Fermented bamboo shoot makes it sour and cuts the fat. Axone makes it deep and savoury, and the combination of smoked pork and axone is as close as Nagaland gets to a national dish. Ao Naga households add anishi, cakes of fermented and dried colocasia leaf, which thicken the pot and taste faintly of the smoke they dried in. Beyond that there is salt, chilli, ginger and water.

It is worth saying plainly that this is not a subtle style and does not try to be. The flavours are large, direct and few, and a Naga cook would think the layered gravies of the north fussy in the same way that a Neapolitan thinks a twelve-topping pizza is fussy.

Eromba, and the Meitei table.

Manipur's valley cooking belongs to the Meitei, and its everyday plate is built on ngari. Eromba is the dish to learn first. Vegetables are boiled soft — potato, beans, whatever the house has — then mashed with roasted dried chilli and a piece of ngari that has been held over a flame until it smells like a grilled anchovy. Herbs go in raw at the end, and it is served at room temperature.

The version people argue about uses yongchak, the tree bean, whose smell is sulphurous and carries across a house. Alongside eromba a Meitei meal usually has chamthong, a clear vegetable stew, and often singju, a raw salad of shredded vegetables dressed with ngari and roasted gram flour. Nothing in that meal is fried and very little of it is hot.

Jadoh, and what Meghalaya eats.

Meghalaya belongs to the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo, and its best-known dish is jadoh: local red rice cooked with pork, ginger, onion and turmeric until the grains take the colour and the fat of the meat. The richest version, ja doh snam, is cooked with pig's blood, which darkens it further and is what you will be served in Shillong at a jadoh stall. A version without blood is ordinary home cooking and is what the recipe in this course describes.

Two other Khasi dishes are worth knowing. Doh khleh is boiled pork, sliced and tossed while still warm with raw onion, ginger, chilli and lemon, which makes it a salad rather than a curry. Tungrymbai is fermented soybean cooked with a paste of black sesame, and the sesame is doing what a nut paste does elsewhere: it thickens, it adds fat and it rounds off the ferment.

Bai, gudok, thukpa.

Three more, quickly, because each stands for a state. Mizoram's bai is a pot of boiled greens and vegetables seasoned with sa-um, fermented pork fat, and sometimes with fermented soya. It is the plainest cooking in this course and Mizo households eat it daily. Tripura's gudok packs vegetables and berma, its fermented fish, into a bamboo tube and steams them in it.

Sikkim looks a different way altogether. Its kitchen is Nepali and Tibetan, so it makes momo, steamed dumplings pleated shut, and thukpa, a broth of hand-pulled or cut noodles with vegetables and meat. Gundruk goes into a thin sour soup, kinema into a stew, and the hard yak's-milk cheese called churpi is chewed for hours like a sweet. None of that would look out of place in Lhasa or Kathmandu, and it is Indian cooking all the same.
