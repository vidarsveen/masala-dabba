Rice, sticky rice and the steamed pot.

Picture the plate this region is built around. Rice sits in the middle, a small bowl of something sour sits beside it, there is a spoonful of mashed potato sharp with mustard oil, and a whole green chilli lies at the edge where a European would expect a garnish. Nothing on that plate has been fried in a spiced gravy, and nothing on it needed a spice tin.

Rice, and three kinds of it.

The Brahmaputra valley is a rice landscape and always has been. The river floods it every year, drops silt across it and moves on, and the cultivation that grew up around that rhythm is wet-rice farming of a kind the region shares with Burma and southern China rather than with the wheat belt to the west.

Assam grows several rices that a shop in Delhi would not stock. Joha is a short aromatic grain, cooked for guests and for festival days, and it perfumes a kitchen as strongly as basmati does without the long grain. Bora saul is glutinous: it is sticky enough to be eaten in the fingers as a ball, and it goes into the rice cakes called pitha. The third is the one worth a paragraph of its own.

Komal saul means soft rice, and it does not need to be cooked. The grain is soaked and parboiled in a particular way at the mill, and after that a bowl of it in warm water for twenty minutes is a meal. A household pours water over it, waits, and eats it with curd, jaggery, a little cream or a spoonful of mashed potato. It is the fastest hot-country breakfast anywhere in India, and it exists because it keeps and because it needs no fuel.

In the hills the rice changes. Meghalaya cooks a local red rice, which is what gives jadoh its colour. Arunachal and the Naga hills grow sticky rice on terraces and in jhum fields, and a packet of it wrapped in a leaf is what a farmer takes to the field. Sikkim, which sits on the Tibetan and Nepali side of the region's cooking, eats rice but also buckwheat, millet and wheat noodles.

Pitika: mashing as a method.

If one technique deserves to travel out of Assam, it is pitika. The word means mashed. A potato is boiled and crushed by hand, then worked with raw mustard oil, finely chopped onion, green chilli and salt, and that is the whole dish. Aubergine is roasted over a flame and mashed the same way. So is fish, so is dried fish, so are boiled greens.

The point of a pitika on the plate is contrast. The rest of the meal is wet and gentle, and the pitika is dry, raw-tasting and sharp, because the mustard oil goes in without being cooked and the onion is never softened. One spoonful pulls a plate of rice and thin broth together, which is exactly the job a pickle does further west.

Steaming, boiling and the pot in the smoke.

Very little is fried here, and in the hills almost nothing is. The three methods that matter are boiling, steaming in a wrapper, and smoking above the fire.

Leaf-wrapped steaming turns up everywhere. Fish, pork, herbs and chilli go into a banana or turmeric leaf, the parcel is tied and set on a griddle or in the embers, and it cooks in its own moisture. Arunachal and Nagaland do the same thing inside a green bamboo tube. Nothing browns, no fat is added, and the wrapper perfumes what is inside it.

Boiling is not the poor relation it is in European cooking. Mizoram's bai is vegetables boiled with a little fermented pork fat and a pinch of alkali, and Manipur's chamthong is seasonal vegetables boiled with a lump of roasted ngari. Both dishes are seasoned entirely by one strong ferment and the water they cook in. A cook who reaches for oil here is missing the point rather than economising.

Almost no dairy.

The north-east is the part of India where milk is least important, and that single fact separates its plate from every other region in this course. There is no paneer in the traditional cooking, no cream in a gravy, no ghee poured over rice and no yoghurt to cool a dish down. Assam is the partial exception, because curd and cream belong to its breakfast, and Sikkim has yak's milk and the hard cheese called churpi.

Take the dairy away and two things follow. Nothing on the plate softens chilli heat, which is why the chilli is served whole on the side where the eater can control it. And fat is not a carrier of flavour here the way ghee is in the north, so flavour has to come from ferment, smoke and the cooking water instead.

The order of an Assamese meal.

Assam eats its courses in a fixed order, and the order is chemistry. The meal opens with khar, the alkaline dish, taken with plain rice. Dal, vegetables, fried things and the meat or fish follow in the middle. It closes with tenga, something sour, and often with a sweet after that.

Alkali first and acid last is a genuinely unusual sequence, and nobody needs a theory to enjoy it. The khar is soft, slightly soapy and very plain, so it settles the appetite rather than sharpening it. The sour bowl at the end does the same work a tamarind chutney does in Bengal: it cuts the fat and it tells you the meal is over.

The hill states do not eat in courses. A Naga or Mizo plate arrives whole, with rice heaped in the middle, a boiled green dish beside it, a piece of smoked meat, a fermented relish and a chilli. Everything is mixed by hand a mouthful at a time, and the relish and the chilli are there so the eater can adjust every mouthful separately.
