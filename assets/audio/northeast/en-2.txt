Rice, sticky rice and the steamed pot.

An Assamese plate may place rice beside a sour broth, a spoonful of mashed potato with mustard oil and a fresh chilli. Elsewhere, sticky or red rice meets boiled greens, smoked meat, fermented relish or noodles. Rice connects many meals without making them identical.

The Brahmaputra valley is a major rice landscape shaped by monsoon water, floodplain soils and irrigation. Flooding can renew fields but also destroy crops and homes. Wet-rice systems connect the valley with eastern and south-eastern Asia, while farmers also grow pulses, oilseeds, vegetables and tea.

Assam grows distinctive aromatic, glutinous and soak-softened rices. Joha is a short aromatic grain used in everyday and festive dishes. Bora saul is glutinous and goes into preparations including pitha. Komal saul needs a different explanation because processing lets it soften by soaking.

Komal saul, or kumol saul, is processed so that soaking softens it without another cooking stage. It may be eaten with curd, cream or jaggery as part of jolpan. Soaking time and water temperature depend on the product, and a packaged rice should be prepared according to its instructions.

Rice types and farming systems change with altitude and community. Red and glutinous rices appear in several states, grown in valleys, terraces and some jhum fields. Sikkim's foodways draw on Nepali, Bhutia, Lepcha and other communities, so rice sits alongside millet, buckwheat and wheat noodles.

Pitika describes mashed preparations. A potato version may combine boiled potato with edible mustard oil, finely chopped onion, green chilli and salt. Aubergine, fish, dried fish and cooked greens can also form the base of other pitika dishes.

A pitika can provide texture and sharpness beside rice and broth. In the course's potato version, the mash remains coarse while uncooked mustard oil and onion supply pungency.

Boiling, steaming in a wrapper and smoking above the hearth are prominent techniques, especially in many hill kitchens. Frying and sautéing also occur, and their importance changes by state, community and dish.

Leaf-wrapped steaming appears in several northeastern traditions. Fish, meat, herbs or chilli may go into a banana or turmeric leaf, where the parcel cooks in its own moisture. Some communities also cook food inside bamboo tubes. These methods can limit added fat and let the wrapper or tube contribute aroma, but ingredients and techniques vary.

Mizoram's bai combines vegetables with ingredients that may include rice water, alkali, fermented soya or pork. Manipur's chamthong is a light stew of seasonal vegetables, often with fermented or dried fish. Formulas vary, but both show how a clear broth can carry herbs and fermented flavours.

Many cuisines in the region use less dairy than much of northern India, but patterns differ. Assamese jolpan may include curd and cream. Sikkim and Arunachal have dairy traditions that include churpi, while many Naga, Mizo and Manipuri dishes build flavour without milk.

Fermented foods, sesame, perilla, pork fat, mustard oil and cooking liquid can each carry flavour. A whole chilli or relish beside the rice lets the diner adjust heat, while other recipes cook chilli directly into the dish.

A formal Assamese meal may open with khar, followed by dal, vegetables, fish or meat, and close with a sour tenga. This is a recognised progression rather than a fixed order in every home.

The contrast between alkaline and sour dishes is culinary as well as chemical. Khar changes texture and has a restrained flavour, while tenga refreshes with acidity. They need not be explained as a rule that governs digestion.

In many hill meals, rice and several accompaniments arrive together. Boiled vegetables, smoked meat, fermented relish and chilli are possible elements, but the composition varies widely among Naga, Mizo and other communities.
