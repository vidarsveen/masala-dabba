The Ahoms, the hills and the pantry that never crossed.

Stand at Nongriat in Meghalaya and look at the bridge. It is made of the living roots of a rubber fig, trained across the stream over fifteen or twenty years by people who would not cross it themselves, and it gets stronger every decade rather than weaker. Nothing about it came from anywhere else. That is a useful thing to hold in mind while reading about a region that India has often described from a distance.

Sukaphaa crosses the Patkai.

In 1228 a prince called Sukaphaa led a few thousand followers over the Patkai hills from Mong Mao, on what is now the Burma-Yunnan border, and came down into the upper Brahmaputra valley. His people were Tai, and they spoke a language related to Thai and Lao rather than to anything in India. They settled, married into the local Bodo-Kachari population, and built a state.

The Ahom kingdom they founded lasted about six hundred years, which makes it one of the longest-lived states in Indian history. It held the valley against repeated Mughal invasions, most famously in 1671 at Saraighat, where Lachit Barphukan's fleet beat a much larger Mughal force on the river itself. The kingdom fell only after Burmese invasions in the 1810s and 1820s, and Assam passed to the British by the Treaty of Yandabo in 1826.

What matters for the kitchen is where the Ahoms came from. They brought wet-rice cultivation, sticky rice, fermented fish, rice beer and a taste for boiled and steamed food, all of it from the south-east. Six hundred years later the Assamese plate still looks east. Sticky rice, an alkali made from ash, a fermented fish paste and a sour broth are ordinary things across upper Burma, Laos and Yunnan, and they are ordinary here, and they are unknown two hundred kilometres west.

The hills were never part of it.

The Ahom kingdom was a valley state. The hills that ring the valley were not in it, and the peoples living there were not governed by it, traded with it only at the edges and cooked differently.

The languages show how separate they are. Naga, Mizo, Meitei and most of Arunachal speak Tibeto-Burman languages, which points north and east. Khasi in Meghalaya is Austroasiatic, in the same family as Khmer and Vietnamese, and it has no relatives anywhere near it. Assamese is Indo-Aryan, the only language in the region that belongs to the same family as Hindi and Bengali. A food map of the north-east follows that language map very closely, and it is worth looking at them together.

Hill agriculture was, and in places still is, jhum: a patch of forest is cleared and burnt, cropped for a year or two, and then left to grow back for a decade. That system supports rice, millet, yam, chilli and a great many leaves and herbs. It does not support the year-round cash cropping that a spice trade needs, and it never produced a surplus to sell west.

Why the pantry never crossed.

Three things kept the Indian spice pantry out. The first is geography: the Brahmaputra is a very large river with a narrow approach from Bengal, and the hills beyond it are steep, wet and roadless. The second is direction. Trade here ran east and south-east into Burma and Yunnan, along the same routes the Ahoms had come down, and the goods that moved were silk, salt and metal rather than cardamom and clove.

The third is that nothing in the local cooking needed them. A kitchen that already had a souring fruit, an alkali, a fermented fish, a fermented bean and a chilli hotter than anything in Kerala had no gap for a spice merchant to fill. Where the pantry did arrive, it arrived late and shallow: Assam uses mustard oil, turmeric and fenugreek, which came up the river from Bengal, and stops there.

Tea, and the people who were brought to pick it.

One plant did travel in the other direction, and it changed the region more than anything in its kitchen. In 1823 Robert Bruce was told about a tea plant growing wild among the Singpho of upper Assam, and by the late 1830s the British had established that it was a true tea, Camellia sinensis var. assamica, and that it would crop on the valley floor. The Assam Company was formed in 1839, and the plantations spread fast.

Local people would not work the estates on the terms offered, so labour was recruited in large numbers from what is now Jharkhand, Odisha and Chhattisgarh, and moved a thousand kilometres to Assam under indenture. Their descendants are a substantial part of Assam's population today and are usually called the tea tribes. They brought their own food with them, and the result is a layer of the Assamese plate — a different set of leaves and greens, and dishes of rice and pulses that came from central India rather than from the valley.

Assam now produces roughly half of India's tea. It is the region's one global export, and it did not come out of the kitchen. It was put into the ground by a company.

1947, and a corridor twenty kilometres wide.

Partition redrew everything. Until 1947 the natural way in and out of the region was down the Brahmaputra and across the plains of eastern Bengal, and that route closed overnight when East Pakistan came into being. What was left was a strip of land between Nepal and Bangladesh, about twenty kilometres across at its narrowest, that still carries every road and railway between these eight states and the rest of India.

The consequences for food are practical rather than romantic. Anything that has to be brought in arrives by a long road, so it costs more, and a local ingredient is almost always the cheaper one. The region's own produce goes out slowly for the same reason, which is part of why axone and ngari are barely known in Mumbai. The modern political history of the north-east is difficult and contested, and this course is not going to adjudicate any of it. It is enough to note that a kitchen at the end of a long road stays local, and this one has.

Why any of this is in a cookery course.

Because it explains a plate that otherwise looks like an oversight. There is no garam masala here because the trade that carried it went the other way round the hills. There is sticky rice and an ash alkali and a pot of fermented fish because a Tai kingdom came over the Patkai and stayed for six hundred years. There is smoked pork because the hills are wet and the market is far. And there is tea, which is the one thing on the list that somebody else decided.
