The Ahoms, tea and changing connections.

At Nongriat in Meghalaya, living aerial roots of rubber fig trees are guided across streams and maintained across generations. These bridges are working routes to farms, markets and neighbouring settlements. They show how movement through difficult terrain depends on local knowledge and collective care.

In 1228 Sukaphaa crossed the Patkai hills and established the first Ahom capital in upper Assam. The Ahoms were Tai speakers, and their state expanded through alliances, conflict and incorporation of neighbouring peoples. Over time its language, religion and institutions also changed.

Ahom rule lasted until the upheavals of the early nineteenth century. At Saraighat in 1671, forces led by Lachit Barphukan defeated a Mughal campaign on the Brahmaputra. Burmese invasions later weakened the kingdom, and the Treaty of Yandabo in 1826 transferred Assam to East India Company rule.

Tai-Ahom food traditions form one strand of Assamese cooking, including rice, fermented foods and ritual dishes. Similar techniques occur across eastern and south-eastern Asia, but resemblance alone cannot prove that one migration introduced every ingredient. The valley also exchanged crops and techniques with neighbouring hill communities and Bengal.

Ahom power centred on the Brahmaputra valley, while relationships with surrounding polities shifted between tribute, trade, alliance and conflict. Present state boundaries should not be projected backwards as sealed cultural borders. People, salt, metals, livestock, forest products and crops moved across them.

The region includes Indo-Aryan, Tibeto-Burman, Tai and Austroasiatic languages, among others. Khasi belongs to the Austroasiatic family and has close relatives including Pnar within Meghalaya. Language history can illuminate movement, but it does not map neatly onto a single cuisine.

Jhum is a varied rotational farming system in which a plot is cropped and then left fallow. Crops, cultivation periods and fallows differ by place. Long fallows can allow recovery, while population pressure and shorter cycles can increase erosion and reduce soil fertility.

The Brahmaputra valley connected Bengal with passes toward present-day Myanmar, Bhutan and Tibet. Hill routes also carried salt, metals, textiles, livestock and forest produce. Difficult terrain shaped the cost and direction of trade without isolating the region from it.

Mustard oil, turmeric and fenugreek are established in Assamese cooking, while herbs, perilla, sesame, bamboo, fermented foods and chillies carry different roles elsewhere. These patterns reflect ecology, community preference and repeated exchange. They cannot be reduced to one spice trade that arrived or failed to arrive.

Singpho and other communities knew and used local tea before British commercial planting. In 1823 Robert Bruce learned of the plant through Singpho contacts. Colonial experiments followed in the 1830s, and the Assam Company was formed in 1839 to expand plantation production.

Plantations recruited large numbers of workers from central and eastern India under coercive colonial labour systems. Their descendants form diverse Adivasi and Tea Tribe communities in Assam. They maintained and adapted food traditions while shaping the industry that made Assam one of India's major tea-producing states.

Tea therefore carries two histories at once: Indigenous knowledge of the plant and a plantation economy built under colonial rule. Neither disappears into the familiar cup.

Partition in 1947 turned routes through eastern Bengal into international border crossings. The Siliguri Corridor, roughly twenty kilometres wide at its narrowest, became the main land connection between the Northeast and the rest of India. Roads, railways, air links and cross-border trade have continued to change since then.

Transport affects price and availability, but it does not freeze a cuisine in place. Packaged axone, fermented bamboo shoot and regional chillies now travel farther, while restaurants and migration carry dishes between cities. Local ingredients remain important for flavour, livelihood and identity.

Rice varieties, fermented foods, mustard oil, tea and smoked meat each carry different histories. Migration and trade mattered, but ecology and community choice mattered too. The result is a group of connected cuisines rather than a remote pantry left outside Indian history.
