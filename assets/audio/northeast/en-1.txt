Fermentation, alkali, smoke and spice.

A Naga meal may place a whole chilli or chilli relish beside the rice, while another dish cooks chilli with meat, bamboo shoot or herbs. Heat can be adjusted at the table or built into the pot. Fermentation and smoke add other kinds of depth.

The course map groups Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura and Sikkim. They contain many communities and do not share one cuisine. Fermented foods, rice, bamboo shoots, fresh herbs and chillies recur in different forms, alongside mustard oil and spices in some areas.

Many hill cuisines use less dairy and fewer ground-spice gravies than kitchens farther west, but absence is not their whole story. Fresh ginger, garlic, chillies, sesame, perilla, herbs and local aromatic plants join fermented ingredients, smoked foods and bamboo shoots. Assam also uses mustard oil, turmeric, fenugreek and other spices.

Fermentation, alkaline cooking and hearth smoking are three important techniques, each with local names and methods. They preserve harvests or change texture as well as flavour. None defines all eight states, and modern homes also fry, pressure-cook and use ingredients carried through trade and migration.

Bhut jolokia is the famous one. Guinness recognised it in 2007 as the hottest chilli in the world, at around a million Scoville heat units, after testing at New Mexico State University. It lost the title in 2011 to the Trinidad Scorpion Butch T, and it has been beaten several times since by the Carolina Reaper and by others bred for the record. So it no longer holds it, and none of that matters much here, because the chilli was doing its job in Assam and Nagaland for a long time before anybody measured it.

Bhut jolokia is a naturally occurring interspecific hybrid associated with Capsicum chinense and C. frutescens. Names vary, including bhut jolokia and Naga or king chilli. It may be eaten fresh, smoked, made into chutney or cooked into a dish. Because its heat varies and can be extreme, the course uses a small measured amount and advises gloves.

Khar is an alkaline ingredient and a family of Assamese dishes. To make kolakhar, dried parts of a suitable banana plant are burned and water is filtered through the ash. The resulting liquid is strongly alkaline and rich in potassium compounds, so a cook uses a small amount rather than treating it like ordinary stock.

Higher pH can soften plant tissue and gives a broth a characteristic smooth feel. Khar may cook with raw papaya, pulses, vegetables or fish head. In a formal Assamese sequence it can appear near the beginning and a sour tenga later, though household meals vary. Acid would reduce the alkalinity, but the customary order is culinary practice rather than a universal chemical rule.

Fermented foods occur across the region, but substrates, microbes and handling differ. A strong aroma can be normal for products such as axone or ngari. It is not by itself proof of safety: cooks should buy established products, follow storage instructions and discard anything showing unexpected mould, damaged packaging or spoilage.

Axone, also spelled akhuni, is Nagaland's. Soybeans are boiled soft, wrapped in leaves and left somewhere warm for several days, then either pounded into a paste or pressed into cakes and hung over the fire to dry. The smell as it ferments is sharply ammoniacal. Cooked into pork it turns savoury and deep, more like a stock than a seasoning. Sikkim makes kinema from the same bean, and the threads it pulls when you lift it will be familiar to anybody who has eaten natto. Meghalaya's version is tungrymbai, cooked with a paste of black sesame and a little pork fat.

Fish ferments include Manipur's ngari and products called shidal or berma in parts of Assam and Tripura. Their fish, preparation vessels and maturation methods are not identical. Cooks use pieces in relishes, stews or mashed dishes. Sikkim's gundruk follows another path: leafy greens ferment and are then dried for later use.

Fresh bamboo shoots contain cyanogenic compounds and require adequate processing before eating. Peeling, slicing, boiling in plenty of water and draining reduce them; commercially prepared or fermented products should be used according to their label. Fermented shoots such as khorisa and soibum can contribute both sourness and savoury aroma to meat, fish and relishes.

Bamboo is also a pot. A green tube is packed with fish, herbs and rice, plugged with a leaf and laid in the embers, and the sap inside steams the contents while the outside chars. Arunachal and the Naga hills both cook this way, and it needs no oil, no pan and no fat at all.

In some Naga and Mizo kitchens, racks above a hearth expose pork, fish, chillies or bamboo shoot to smoke and gentle heat. The result and keeping quality depend on the food, salting, time, temperature and later storage.

Smoking can dry food and extend keeping time while creating a valued flavour. Methods, salting and storage differ, and smoking alone does not guarantee that meat is safe at room temperature. The course therefore treats purchased smoked pork according to its label and keeps fresh or opened meat refrigerated.

A few specialist ingredients open useful starting points: prepared fermented bamboo shoot, an established fermented soybean product, and a chilli whose heat you understand. They do not represent all eight states, but they let the course teach fermentation, smoke, bitterness and heat without pretending that one pantry stands for the whole region.
