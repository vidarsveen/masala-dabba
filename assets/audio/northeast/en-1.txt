Fermentation instead of spice.

In a Naga house the chilli does not go into the pot. It lies on the rim of the plate, whole and raw, and you bite a corner off it between mouthfuls of rice. That one habit tells you a great deal about this corner of India. Heat is a condiment here rather than a foundation, and the depth of a dish comes from somewhere else entirely.

This module covers eight states: Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura and Sikkim. They do not share a kitchen, and the course will keep saying so. What they share is a pantry a cook in Delhi would not recognise, because almost none of the Indian spice trade ever reached up here. Reading four explains why; this one is about what stands in its place.

The pantry that is not there.

Start with the absences, because they are the whole story. There is no tandoor in this region and never was. There is very little ghee, almost no cream and no paneer. Onion and garlic turn up, but a long-simmered masala gravy does not, and the jars of ground coriander, cumin and garam masala that anchor a Punjabi kitchen are marginal here or missing altogether.

Four techniques do the work instead. Cooks ferment, they use an alkali, they smoke things over the hearth, and they put fresh heat on the side of the plate. Once you can see those four, the food of eight very different peoples starts to make sense as a set.

The chilli that held the record.

Bhut jolokia is the famous one. Guinness recognised it in 2007 as the hottest chilli in the world, at around a million Scoville heat units, after testing at New Mexico State University. It lost the title in 2011 to the Trinidad Scorpion Butch T, and it has been beaten several times since by the Carolina Reaper and by others bred for the record. So it no longer holds it, and none of that matters much here, because the chilli was doing its job in Assam and Nagaland for a long time before anybody measured it.

Botanically it is mostly Capsicum chinense with some C. frutescens in its ancestry. Assam calls it bhut jolokia, Nagaland and Manipur call it raja mircha or king chilli, and the fruit ripens from green to a wrinkled scarlet. The interesting part is how it is used. It is eaten raw beside the food, or dried in the smoke above the fire, or pounded with salt into a chutney the size of a thumbnail. It is rarely simmered into a dish, and the reason is practical: cook a chilli that hot into a gravy and the heat goes everywhere, with no coconut milk, no cream and no yoghurt anywhere in the region to catch it.

Khar, which is not a spice.

Now the ingredient with no equivalent anywhere else in India. Khar is an alkali. A household burns the sun-dried peel of a banana, or a length of dried banana trunk, down to a pale ash. The ash goes into a cloth or into a cone of bamboo, water is poured slowly through it, and what drips out is a clear, faintly yellow liquid. Assam calls that liquid kolakhar, from kola, banana. It is mostly potassium carbonate, and it is strongly alkaline.

An alkali behaves in a pot the way an acid does, only in the other direction. It softens tough pulses and fibrous vegetables, it breaks down fat, and it turns the texture of a dal soft and faintly slippery. Assam uses it to open a meal: the first dish served is often the one called khar, made with raw papaya or pulses or fish head, and it is eaten with plain rice before anything else. Sour dishes come at the end. The two are chemical opposites, which is why they never share a bowl — put an acid in with the alkali and both of them stop working.

Ferments, and why the smell is the point.

Every state here ferments something, and each one does it differently. A European reader should be told plainly at the start that the smell is not a fault. These are ripe-cheese smells, and they belong to the ingredient in the same way that a washed-rind cheese smells of the cellar it came from.

Axone, also spelled akhuni, is Nagaland's. Soybeans are boiled soft, wrapped in leaves and left somewhere warm for several days, then either pounded into a paste or pressed into cakes and hung over the fire to dry. The smell as it ferments is sharply ammoniacal. Cooked into pork it turns savoury and deep, more like a stock than a seasoning. Sikkim makes kinema from the same bean, and the threads it pulls when you lift it will be familiar to anybody who has eaten natto. Meghalaya's version is tungrymbai, cooked with a paste of black sesame and a little pork fat.

Fish follows the same logic. Manipur's ngari starts as small carp, sun-dried until hard, then packed tight into an earthen pot that is sealed and left for months. Assam and Tripura make shidal and berma the same way, in pots smeared inside with oil. None of these is a fish sauce, because there is no liquid to pour: the fish stays solid, and a piece the size of a walnut is roasted over a flame and crumbled into a dish. Sikkim's gundruk ferments no protein at all, only leafy greens, packed down without salt until the lactic bacteria take over, and then dried in the sun.

Bamboo, doing two jobs.

Bamboo shoot is eaten fresh, boiled in several changes of water to take the bitterness out, and it tastes mild and faintly sweet. Ferment the same shoot and it becomes something else. Assam calls it khorisa, Manipur soibum, Nagaland bastenga, and in that form it is a souring agent rather than a vegetable. It is sharp, a little cheesy, and it goes into pork, into fish and into a chutney with raw chilli.

Bamboo is also a pot. A green tube is packed with fish, herbs and rice, plugged with a leaf and laid in the embers, and the sap inside steams the contents while the outside chars. Arunachal and the Naga hills both cook this way, and it needs no oil, no pan and no fat at all.

In the pantry: axone.

 Aroma: Ammonia and old cheese when raw, which frightens people and should not.

 Flavour: Deeply savoury once cooked, closer to a meat stock than to a spice.

 What it does: Gives a dish its backbone of depth, which is the job garam masala does elsewhere.

 When to add: Early, fried in a little fat with chilli, so it loses its rawness and melts into the pot.

 Instead of it: Nothing is honest. Miso or fermented black bean gets you a savoury dish, not this one.

Smoke, the fourth technique.

Walk into an old house in the Naga or Mizo hills and look up. There is a rack above the hearth, and on it sit slabs of pork, fish, chillies and bamboo shoot, drying in the smoke of a fire that is never quite out. Meat can sit up there for weeks and comes down dark, hard and dry.

That began as storage rather than flavour. These are wet hills with a long monsoon, no refrigeration until recently and no easy road to a market, so anything killed had to keep. The flavour was a by-product that the cooking then organised itself around, which is why smoked pork is not a special-occasion dish here but the ordinary contents of a larder.

What this means for a cook.

You cannot substitute your way into this food from a supermarket spice rack. Three purchases get you most of the way: fermented bamboo shoot, some form of fermented soybean, and a chilli hot enough to use a sliver at a time. The rest is technique, and technique travels for nothing.
