Poha, bafla and the Bhopali korma.

Indore is closely associated with street breakfasts of poha topped with sev, onion, coriander, spice and lime. Jalebi is a familiar sweet accompaniment. Vendors and customers vary the toppings, balance and portion, so the pairing is a city habit rather than a fixed plate.

Poha is parboiled, flattened and dried rice. The flakes are moistened according to thickness, then warmed with seasoning. Many Indore versions are soft and pale yellow with turmeric, but cooking methods vary rather than forming a clean border with Maharashtra or Gujarat.

Sev stays crisp when added at the plate, lime supplies fresh acidity, and jiravan adds a local finishing blend. Jiravan commonly includes cumin, black salt and sour or aromatic ingredients, with no single formula shared by every stall.

Jalebi beside poha puts syrupy sweetness against a savoury, sour breakfast. The combination appears at morning stalls and in the wider street-food culture for which Indore is known, including Sarafa after dark.

Sev is gram-flour dough pressed through a perforated plate into hot oil. It tops poha and chaat and appears in snack mixtures. Ratlami sev is a protected regional name associated with a more assertively spiced style, though the exact blend varies by producer.

For bhutte ka kees, fresh corn is grated into a wet mash. In one common method it cooks in ghee with mustard seed, green chilli, ginger and turmeric before milk is added. Stirring turns it into a soft savoury mash.

Lemon, coriander and grated coconut can finish the dish. Less-sweet mature corn gives a more savoury result, while readily available sweetcorn produces a sweeter variation.

Bafla is closely related to Rajasthan's bati. Its defining step is to simmer the wheat dough balls before baking or roasting them. They are then broken open and served with dal and ghee.

Boiling sets the outside and helps the centre remain softer before dry heat browns the surface. Dal, the amount of ghee and the souring vary. Bafla is prepared both at home and in larger batches for gatherings.

Bhopal's food includes courtly traditions alongside everyday and migrant cooking. The eighteenth-century state was founded by Dost Mohammad Khan, an Afghan soldier who had served the Mughals. Meat, yoghurt, browned onion and aromatic whole spices recur in dishes associated with the old court, but they do not define every kitchen in the city.

The course recipe is goat or lamb cooked with yoghurt and browned onion, thickened with poppy seed and chironji, and finished with an aromatic blend. Tomato and cream are absent from this version, while other Bhopali kormas differ. Gentle heat helps keep the yoghurt together and tenderises the meat.

Bhopal is also known for kebabs, paya and other meat dishes sold in the old city's food lanes. Shops and opening patterns change, so the course keeps the focus on the foods rather than promising a particular vendor.

Bhopal also cooks a rogan josh, and it is not Kashmir's. The name is often explained through Persian words for oil and boiling, though other etymologies exist. Kashmir's red version uses colour-rich chilli and sometimes ratan jot without requiring fierce heat. Bhopal applies the same established dish name to a court recipe made from the pantry at hand.

Some Bhopali versions begin with onion browned in fat and use a local garam masala, unlike many Kashmiri Pandit methods. The shared name covers distinct recipes rather than proving a single line of descent.

Chironji can enrich milk sweets such as barfi. Dried mahua also appears in laddus with ingredients such as ghee, grain flour or nuts. Both show how gathered tree products can move into celebratory food as well as everyday trade.
