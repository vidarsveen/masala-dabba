Poha, bafla and the Bhopali korma.

Indore eats breakfast in the street, and it eats the same breakfast every day. A man scoops flattened rice out of a wide steaming pan into a paper plate, throws over a fistful of crisp gram-flour noodles, adds raw onion, coriander, a pinch of a spice powder he mixed himself, and half a lime squeezed over the lot. Beside him another stall is frying jalebi. Most people take one of each, which sounds wrong and is the whole point.

Indori poha.

Poha is rice that has been parboiled, rolled flat and dried, so it needs moistening rather than cooking. The thick grade is rinsed in a sieve until it softens, drained, and then steamed rather than fried, which is what makes the Indore version different from the poha of Maharashtra or Gujarat. It comes out loose, pale yellow with turmeric, and light enough to eat a large plate of without noticing.

Three things finish it. Sev goes over the top and must go on at the last second, or it softens. Lime is squeezed at the plate rather than stirred through in the pan. And most stalls dust it with jiravan, a local powder built on cumin with dried mango, black salt and fennel in it. Every shop's jiravan is different, nobody publishes theirs, and asking about it is a reasonable way to start a conversation in Indore.

The jalebi is not a garnish. A hot, sticky, syrup-soaked coil of fried batter eaten alongside a savoury, sour, lightly spiced plate of rice is a genuinely good combination, and the city has been doing it long enough that nobody there finds it worth remarking on. Indore's night market at Sarafa, where jewellers' shops become food stalls after dark, has made the habit famous well beyond Madhya Pradesh.

Sev, and why the city is covered in it.

Sev is gram-flour dough pushed through a perforated plate straight into hot oil, so it fries in seconds into fine crisp strands. It goes on poha, on chaat, into a dry snack mix, and by the handful into a child's palm. Indore eats an extraordinary amount of it, and the most distinctive kind comes from Ratlam, a few hours west, where the dough is spiked with ajwain and clove until the sev is hot enough to eat on its own.

Bhutte ka kees.

The other Indore dish worth learning is bhutte ka kees, and it is unusual enough to be worth the trouble. Fresh corn is grated raw off the cob, which takes patience and produces a wet mash rather than kernels. That mash goes into ghee with mustard seed, green chilli, ginger and turmeric, and is stirred until it stops smelling raw. Then milk goes in, and the pan is stirred more or less continuously until the starch in the corn thickens the milk into something between a porridge and a savoury mash.

It is finished with lemon, coriander and grated coconut, and it is eaten hot with a spoon. The mistake outsiders make is using sweetcorn, which is bred for sugar and turns the dish into a pudding. Field corn, or the least sweet corn you can find, is what it wants.

Dal bafla.

Rajasthan's reading explains bati, the hard wheat ball baked in embers and drowned in ghee at the table. Bafla is its neighbour and its improvement. The dough is the same coarse wheat and ghee, but the balls are dropped into boiling water first and simmered until they float, then lifted out and baked or roasted until the outside cracks and browns.

The boiling is the whole difference. It gelatinises the starch before the dry heat gets to it, so a bafla stays soft and slightly moist inside where a bati is dense and dry. It still gets cracked open and drowned in ghee, and it still sits in a plain toor dal soured with a little tamarind. Malwa treats it as feast food and cooks it in quantity, often outdoors, and a hundred baflas at a time is not unusual at a wedding.

The Bhopali korma.

Bhopal cooks differently from the rest of the region, because its kitchen came down from a court rather than up from a village. The founder of the state was an Afghan soldier in Mughal service, and Persian and Mughal habits arrived with him and stayed. Meat is central, the whole spices are aromatic rather than hot, and everything is cooked slowly with the lid weighted down.

A korma here is goat cooked in yoghurt with a great deal of browned onion ground to a paste, thickened with poppy seed and sometimes chironji, and scented with the local garam masala. It has no tomato in it, very little chilli, and no cream. The technique is a sequence rather than a set of ingredients: brown the onion properly, cook the yoghurt in slowly so it does not split, add the poppy-seed paste once the fat has separated, then let the pot sit on the lowest possible heat until the meat gives.

The city's other reputation is for meat cooked over fire. The lane around Ibrahimpura, known to everyone as Chatori Gali, sells seekh kebab, shami kebab and paya through the evening, and it is the single easiest place in central India to eat well for very little money.

Rogan josh, arriving from somewhere else.

Bhopal also cooks a rogan josh, and it is not Kashmir's. The Kashmir reading takes the name apart: rogan is Persian for fat, josh is Persian for vigour, and the deep red of the valley version comes from a mild colouring chilli and from ratan jot rather than from heat. All of that stands. What Bhopal has is the same Persian name arriving through a different court, applied to a dish cooked with the pantry that was actually to hand.

So a Bhopali rogan josh starts with onion browned in fat, which a Kashmiri Pandit cook would never do, and it is seasoned with the local garam masala. It is a cousin rather than a copy, and neither city is cooking the other's dish wrongly. Names travel faster than recipes, which is worth remembering every time two regions claim the same one.

Sweets, and the forest in them.

Chironji ki barfi is the sweet that could only come from here: milk cooked down with sugar until it sets, with chironji through it and more scattered over the top. Mahua laddu is plainer and older, made of dried mahua flowers pounded with ghee and often with chironji or wheat flour, and it belongs to the villages rather than to the sweet shops. Both are the forest arriving on a plate, which is where this region always ends up.
