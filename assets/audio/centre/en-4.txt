Ashoka, the Begums and the Bastar kitchens.

On a low hill above the Betwa valley, forty kilometres from Bhopal, stands a solid dome of stone about sixteen metres high with a stone railing round it and four carved gateways at the compass points. It has been there, in one form or another, for well over two thousand years. It contains no room. You cannot go inside it, and there was never anything to go inside for, because a stupa is a mound raised over relics rather than a building.

Sanchi.

The core of the Great Stupa was raised under the emperor Ashoka in the third century BC, and it was smaller then and built of brick. A century or two later it was roughly doubled in size and cased in the stone you see now, under the Shungas. The four gateways, the toranas, came later still, around the first century BC, and they are the reason people travel to Sanchi.

Sanchi is often described as the oldest stone structure in India, and that claim is worth handling carefully. What is solid is that the site began under Ashoka, that the stonework here is among the earliest surviving Buddhist architecture anywhere, and that the gateway carving is some of the finest. The place was then forgotten for centuries, overgrown and partly plundered, until a British officer reported it in 1818. A proper restoration followed under John Marshall between 1912 and 1919, and it became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1989.

Why it stands here at all is a food-adjacent accident of trade. Sanchi overlooks the ancient route between the Gangetic plain and the western ports, and Vidisha, a rich merchant town, sits a few kilometres away. The inscriptions on the railings are largely records of small donations by ordinary people, including guilds of ivory carvers. The monument was paid for by traders, and the traders were there because everything moving between north India and the sea came past.

Ashoka writes down what he stopped eating.

Ashoka matters to a cookery course for one unusual reason. He had his edicts cut into rock across the subcontinent, and the first of the Major Rock Edicts, after forbidding animal sacrifice, turns to the royal kitchen. It records that hundreds of thousands of animals had been killed every day for the palace table, that the number was now down to two peacocks and a deer, and that even those three would not be killed in future.

Historians argue about how much this changed anyone's diet outside the court, and it plainly did not make India vegetarian by decree. What is remarkable is that a ruler in the third century BC thought the contents of his kitchen were a matter of public policy worth carving into stone. Reading it next to the vegetarian traditions that grew in this part of India over the following centuries makes both harder to dismiss as accident.

A hundred and seven years of Begums.

Bhopal was founded early in the seventeen-hundreds by Dost Mohammad Khan, a Pashtun soldier who had come south with the Mughal armies and stayed. That origin explains the meat, the yoghurt and the slow pots in its kitchen. What happened next has no parallel anywhere else in princely India.

In 1819 the ruler was killed in an accident, and his widow Qudsia Begum took power in the name of her infant daughter. She held it. Her daughter Sikandar Begum ruled after her, first as regent and then in her own right, and in 1864 she made the pilgrimage to Mecca and wrote an account of it, which makes her the first Indian ruler to have done either. Shah Jahan Begum followed and spent heavily on building, beginning the enormous Taj-ul-Masajid. Sultan Jahan Begum, the fourth, ruled until 1926, wrote the life of her great-grandmother Qudsia, and became the first woman chancellor of Aligarh Muslim University. Then she abdicated in favour of her son.

A century of women running a Muslim princely state produced, among much else, a court kitchen with a distinct character. The food that came out of it is aromatic rather than fiery, heavy on goat and on yoghurt, thickened with nuts and poppy seed, and cooked slowly under a weighted lid. That is the Bhopali korma of reading three, and it is why Bhopal tastes closer to Lucknow than to the villages twenty kilometres outside it.

Bastar, and the food of a forest.

Six hundred kilometres south-east the story is entirely different, and it is much older than any court. The Bastar plateau is sal forest, and the people who live in it are Adivasi: Gond, Muria, Maria, Halba, Bhatra, Dhurwa and others, each with their own language and their own calendar. Their food is not a regional variation on Indian cooking. It is a separate answer to the question of what to eat, written by the forest rather than by a kitchen tradition.

The list is short and it changes with the month. Rice and millet form the base. Bamboo shoot, wild greens, tubers and forest mushrooms fill it out. Mahua provides sugar, flour and drink. Tamarind and chironji provide sourness, fat and cash. And in the hot months before the rains, the red weaver ants nesting in the mango and sal trees are taken down, nest and all, and ground with salt, garlic and chilli into chapda chutney.

Chapda is sharply sour, because the ants carry formic acid, and it is eaten in small quantities with rice in the way a pickle is. It is also taken as a remedy for fever. There is nothing exotic about it from the inside: it is protein and acid, available free, at the season when the fields have least to offer.

Bastar keeps its own festival too. Bastar Dussehra runs for something like seventy-five days, far longer than the festival anywhere else, and its centre is the goddess Danteshwari rather than the story of Rama. It is one of the clearest signs that this is a culture that has absorbed the mainstream on its own terms rather than the other way round.

Who owns the forest.

None of this can be described honestly without saying that access to the forest is contested. Mahua, tendu, chironji and tamarind are not hobbies but income, and who may collect them, who may sell them and at what price has been argued over since colonial excise and forest law first restricted them. The Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognised community rights over such produce, and the argument about what that means in practice has continued ever since. That is a subject for somewhere other than a cookery course. It belongs here only because the pantry in reading one depends on the answer.

Why the middle gets left out.

This region is rarely written about, and the reason is not that the food is dull. It is that the food has no single story. There is no coastline to hang it on, no empire whose capital was here for long, no diaspora carrying the dishes abroad and no restaurant category called central Indian. What is here instead is three kitchens that barely speak to one another: a wheat plateau that eats rice for breakfast, a rice plain with its own bread, and a forest that gathers half of what it eats. That is a harder thing to summarise and a more interesting thing to learn.
