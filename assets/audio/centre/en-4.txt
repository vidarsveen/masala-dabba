Ashoka, the Begums and the Bastar kitchens.

On a hill near Vidisha stands Sanchi's Great Stupa, a solid domed monument surrounded by a railing and four carved gateways. Its earliest brick core dates to the Mauryan period. Later dynasties enlarged and ornamented it, so the structure visible today records several centuries rather than one building campaign.

The Great Stupa began as a smaller brick monument associated with Ashoka in the third century BCE. It was enlarged and faced with stone during the Shunga period. The decorated gateways were added later, around the first century BCE and early first century CE.

UNESCO describes Sanchi as one of the oldest extant Buddhist sanctuaries and traces work at the site from the third century BCE into the medieval period. It became a World Heritage site in 1989. This is more precise than calling one rebuilt monument India's oldest stone structure.

Sanchi stood near Vidisha and important routes through central India. Inscriptions record donations from many patrons, including artisans. Trade helped sustain the wider religious centre, but it is too simple to assign the whole monument to merchants or to one route.

The first Major Rock Edict turns from animal sacrifice to the royal kitchen. In a standard translation, Ashoka says that many animals had once been killed there daily. At the time of the inscription, the number was two peacocks and a deer, with even those intended to stop.

The statement concerns the court and does not prove a dietary change across the empire. Its value here is narrower: a third-century BCE ruler made killing for his own kitchen part of a public moral declaration.

The present city of Bhopal was established in the early eighteenth century by Dost Mohammad Khan, an Afghan soldier who had served the Mughals. Courtly food later developed through many cooks, households and political eras; the founder's origin alone cannot explain every ingredient or technique.

After Nawab Nazar Mohammad Khan died in 1819, his widow Qudsia Begum assumed authority. Her daughter Sikandar Begum, granddaughter Shah Jahan Begum and great-granddaughter Sultan Jahan Begum each later ruled. Their combined era included regencies and interruptions, so “1819 to 1926” names a dynastic span rather than unbroken personal succession. Sultan Jahan became the first woman chancellor of Aligarh Muslim University and abdicated Bhopal in 1926.

Dishes associated with Bhopal's court often feature meat, yoghurt, browned onion, nuts or seeds and aromatic spices. The evidence supports a courtly strand in the city's cooking, but not a single menu created by the Begums or a clean divide from nearby villages.

Bastar includes many Adivasi communities, among them Gond, Muria, Maria, Halba, Bhatra and Dhurwa peoples. Languages, livelihoods and food practices differ among them. Farming, forest gathering, markets and purchased goods all contribute, so “forest food” describes a relationship rather than a sealed cuisine.

Rice and millets may form a base, joined by bamboo shoot, greens, tubers, mushrooms and seasonal tree crops. Some communities prepare chapda, a condiment made with red weaver ants and their eggs, salt, chilli and other seasonings. Recipes and occasions vary.

Formic acid contributes to chapda's sharp taste. It is served in small quantities as a condiment. The course treats it as a specific food practice and makes no medical claim for it.

Bastar Dussehra lasts about seventy-five days. Government descriptions centre Maa Danteshwari and local deity traditions rather than the burning of Ravana. The festival brings communities and ritual roles from across Bastar into Jagdalpur.

Access to minor forest produce affects both food and income. The Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognises rights that include access to and disposal of such produce, while implementation and market terms continue to shape what collectors receive. A pantry built partly on gathering cannot be separated from those rights.

Central India resists one culinary label. Malwa's wheat and Indore's poha, Chhattisgarh's rice foods, Bhopal's courtly strand and Bastar's varied Adivasi foodways overlap without collapsing into one cuisine. The region is best understood through these connections and differences rather than a single representative plate.
