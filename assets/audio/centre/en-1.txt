Poppy seed, chironji and the forest pantry.

Drive out of Bhopal in the first week of April and the ground under certain trees has turned pale yellow. The mahua drops its flowers in the night, a few thousand at a time, and by six in the morning whole families are out with baskets sweeping them into heaps. Nobody planted these trees. Nobody prunes them or waters them. For six weeks they feed a household, and for the rest of the year they are simply trees.

That is the first thing to understand about the middle of India. Half of this pantry is bought in a shop, and the other half is picked up off the forest floor, which ties a cook here to a season and a place in a way a bag of cumin never does.

Poppy seed, and what Malwa does with it.

The Malwa plateau runs west from Bhopal towards Indore, and its gravies are thickened with white poppy seed, khus khus. The seed is soaked, ground with a little water into a pale paste, and stirred into the pot, where it does the job cream does in Europe. It gives a sauce body and a faint nuttiness, and it leaves the flavour of the meat and the spices alone.

Bengal grinds this seed until the paste becomes the dish itself, and reading one of that region explains why. Malwa never lets it take the lead. Here it is structure rather than flavour, which is why a Bhopali korma can be rich without a spoonful of cream anywhere near it.

The seed is cheap here for a reason that is worth stating plainly. Malwa was one of India's great opium districts in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, growing outside the East India Company's Bengal monopoly and shipping through Portuguese ports on the west coast. Licensed cultivation for the pharmaceutical trade continues in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh under the Narcotics Commissioner, and the government factory at Neemuch sits in the middle of it. Opium comes from the unripe pod. The seed is the legal leftover, and in Malwa it has always been the cheap part.

In the pantry: poppy seed (khus khus).

 Aroma: Almost nothing raw; a warm marzipan smell once ground and cooked.

 Flavour: Mild and milky, faintly nutty, with no edge and no heat.

 What it does: Thickens a gravy and rounds it, so meat and whole spice stay in front.

 When to add: Soaked and ground to a paste, stirred in halfway; never thrown in whole.

 Instead of it: Cashew or melon-seed paste gives the same body and a little more sweetness.

Chironji, which nobody farms.

Chironji is the kernel of Buchanania lanzan, a tree of the dry forest that is also called charoli. The fruit is a small stone fruit, the stone is cracked, and inside sits a flat seed about the size of a lentil. It tastes somewhere between an almond and a pistachio, and it costs more by weight than either, because every one of them was gathered by hand from a wild tree.

A Malwa kitchen uses chironji in two ways. Ground with poppy seed it enriches a meat gravy, and whole it studs the sweets, above all chironji ki barfi. It carries a great deal of oil, so it goes rancid faster than most nuts and should be kept cold. Adivasi households across Bastar and the Satpura forests collect it in April and May and sell it on through the weekly markets, and a good year for chironji is a good year for cash.

Mahua, food and drink at once.

Mahua is the flower of Madhuca longifolia, and it is the single most important gathered food in this region. The tree sheds its corollas overnight in March and April, they are collected at first light before the sun spoils them, and they are dried in the sun until they go from cream to brown and leathery. Dried mahua is roughly half sugar, which is the whole point of it.

Food comes first. The dried flowers are cooked with flour into a heavy sweet bread, boiled into a thick porridge, or bound with chironji and ghee into mahua laddu. In a bad year they are simply eaten, and they have carried households through failed harvests for as long as anyone has written about the subject.

Then there is the liquor. Mahua ferments readily because of that sugar, and the fermented wash is distilled in the village into a clear spirit that smells of the flower. It is drunk at weddings and at festivals, it is offered to the household gods, and it is also drunk the way cheap spirits are drunk anywhere. Colonial excise law treated the flower as a raw material for illicit distilling rather than as a food, and restricting its collection did real damage to the people who ate it. Madhya Pradesh has since moved the other way and licensed village-made mahua spirit as a heritage product. Both the harm and the correction belong in the story, and neither one makes mahua exotic.

Black cardamom, and the Bhopal blend.

Green cardamom perfumes sweets and tea. Black cardamom, badi elaichi, does something else entirely, and the two are not interchangeable. The pods are large, ribbed and dark, and they are dried over a wood fire, so they come out smelling of smoke, camphor and resin. One pod will mark a whole pot of meat.

Bhopal puts black cardamom at the centre of its garam masala. There is no single recipe, and every household and every shop grinds its own, but the Bhopali version leans on black and green cardamom, mace, nutmeg, cloves, cinnamon and black pepper, and it keeps chilli out. That matters more than it sounds. A blend with no chilli in it can be added late and generously, because the cook controls heat separately with whatever chilli the dish needs.

Tamarind, and the sour end of the forest.

Tamarind grows wild all over this region, and in Bastar it is gathered rather than farmed, which puts it in the same basket as chironji and mahua. The pods are collected, the pulp is pressed into dark blocks, and a household buys it by the lump rather than by the spoon.

Its work here is not the work it does in the south. Tamil Nadu and Andhra sour almost everything with it, and their readings say so. In Malwa the block is reached for in smaller quantities: a sour edge in a dal, a sweet-sour chutney made with jaggery to go beside anything fried, and a good deal of it in the chaat that Indore lives on. In Bastar it sours aamat, the thin stew of vegetables and bamboo shoot that is the everyday dish of the forest villages. One ingredient, three quite different jobs, which is the ordinary condition of Indian cooking.

What makes this pantry unusual.

Every other region in this course buys its pantry. A Kerala cook buys pepper, a Punjabi cook buys wheat, and the price moves with markets and harvests. Here a meaningful part of the larder still arrives by being found, and that changes the arithmetic: the season is fixed by the tree rather than by the sowing, the supply depends on who has the right to collect, and a family's cash income can turn on six weeks in spring. Reading two moves from the forest to the fields, where the story is wheat on one side of the state and rice on the other.
