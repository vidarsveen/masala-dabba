Wheat in Malwa, rice in Chhattisgarh.

Chhattisgarh became a separate state on 1 November 2000. The political border also helps reveal a broad change in staple crops: wheat is prominent around Malwa in western Madhya Pradesh, while rice is central across much of Chhattisgarh. Neither side is uniform, and both eat more than one grain.

Malwa's heavy black soils developed over Deccan basalt and retain monsoon moisture. Wheat and gram can therefore be sown after the rains as winter crops. Rainfall, irrigation and local soils still vary, so stored moisture is part of the system rather than its only source of water.

Madhya Pradesh is a major wheat producer. Sharbati wheat from districts around Sehore and Vidisha is valued for chapati, while soybean has also become an important crop and source of cooking oil. These commercial crops sit beside older household preferences for ghee and other fats.

A Malwa meal may combine wheat roti, dal, a dry vegetable, curd and pickle. Gram flour appears in pakora, kadhi and chila, a savoury pancake whose batter and additions vary.

Sweet and savoury foods often share the table. Dal bafla is closely related to Rajasthan's dal bati, but the direction and agents of that exchange are harder to prove than the similarity of the dishes.

Poha shows why a staple map is never absolute. Flattened rice softens quickly and has become a celebrated breakfast in wheat-growing Indore, usually finished with sev, lime and a local spice blend.

Rice is central to Chhattisgarh's agriculture and public food system. The rice scientist R. H. Richharia assembled a collection reported in the thousands, often cited at about nineteen thousand accessions from the region. That historical collection should not be confused with the number still cultivated today. Aromatic landraces such as Jeeraphool remain part of conservation work.

Rice also becomes breads and dumplings. Rice flour can be shaped into farra, fried as chousela or patted into angakar roti. Bafauri, made from gram, is steamed rather than fried. The names and methods vary across districts and communities.

Basi is cooked rice soaked in water and eaten cool, often with onion, chilli or pickle. Related preparations include panta bhat in Bengal and pakhala in Odisha. Traditional overnight fermentation is culturally important, but cooked rice can support harmful bacterial growth; a modern home cook should cool it promptly and ferment only with a controlled, food-safe method.

South of the rice plain, Bastar includes plateau, forest and cultivated land. Many communities combine farming with seasonal gathering, and weekly haats connect forest produce with goods brought from elsewhere.

Seasonal foods include bamboo shoots, mushrooms, mahua, tamarind, chironji and tendu leaves. Some go directly into meals; others are sold. Safe preparation matters for bamboo shoots because varieties and processing needs differ, so this course does not treat one boiling method as universal.

Aamat is one name for a vegetable stew that may include bamboo shoot and tamarind. Rice and millets, cultivated vegetables, gathered foods and occasional meat appear in different combinations. No single menu represents all of Bastar's communities.

Soil and rainfall help explain the broad crop pattern, but trade, migration and taste keep it from becoming a clean divide. Chila may be made from gram, rice or pulses; poha travels widely; and sweet-shop repertoires cross state borders.
