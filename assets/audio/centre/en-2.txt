Wheat in Malwa, rice in Chhattisgarh.

Chhattisgarh separated from Madhya Pradesh in November 2000, and the new border did something that state borders in India rarely do. It fell almost exactly along a line that was already there in the kitchen. West of it people eat wheat and call a meal roti and dal. East of it people eat rice three times a day and call themselves the rice bowl of India. The two sides had been one administrative unit for more than forty years and had never eaten alike.

Black soil, and what grows in it.

The Malwa plateau sits at about five hundred metres, which makes it cooler and drier than the plains on either side. Its soil is black and heavy, weathered out of the same basalt lava flows that built the Deccan, and it holds water the way a sponge does. That single property decides the farming year. The monsoon fills the soil between June and September, and the wheat and the gram are then sown in October into moisture that is already in the ground rather than falling from the sky.

Madhya Pradesh is one of India's largest wheat producers, and the wheat it is proudest of is sharbati, grown around Sehore and Vidisha. The grain is golden, hard and high in protein, and it is sold at a premium to millers who want it for chapati flour. The state also grows more soybean than any other, most of it crushed for oil, which has quietly become the frying fat of a great many Malwa kitchens.

The Malwa table.

A plain meal in Indore or Ujjain is wheat roti, a dal, a dry vegetable, curd and a pickle, and ghee goes over most of it. Gram flour does a lot of work: it is fried into pakora, cooked into a kadhi with sour curd, and spread thin on a griddle as chila, the gram-flour pancake eaten for breakfast or handed to a child after school.

The sweet and the savoury sit on the same plate rather than in sequence, as they do in Rajasthan next door, and the border between the two states is porous in both directions. Dal bafla, which reading three takes apart, is a close cousin of Rajasthan's dal bati, and it travelled east with soldiers and merchants rather than being invented here.

The oddity of the Malwa table is breakfast. Wheat country wakes up and eats rice, because poha is flattened rice, steamed soft in minutes and eaten by the plateful from half past seven. It is cheap, it keeps dry for months, and it cooks faster than anything made of wheat. Nobody in Indore thinks of it as a rice dish at all.

Chhattisgarh, the rice bowl.

Cross east and the fields change colour. Chhattisgarh is a wet, low plain of rice, and it grew so many kinds of it that the count became famous. The rice scientist R. H. Richharia, working from Raipur in the nineteen-seventies, collected and catalogued close to twenty thousand named varieties from this region, and a fair number of them are still sown in villages today. The best known outside the state is jeeraphool, a small aromatic grain named for its likeness to a cumin seed.

What a kitchen with that much rice does is make bread from it. Rice flour is steamed into farra, small crescents like a plain dumpling, fried into chousela, or patted flat and baked into angakar roti between leaves. Gram flour reappears as bafauri, a steamed dumpling that is the local answer to a fritter. There is far less wheat on the table and far less ghee, and the chilli is used more freely than in Malwa.

Then there is basi, which is the everyday dish nobody outside the state has heard of. Cooked rice is covered with water and left overnight, it ferments very slightly, and it is eaten cool in the morning with salt, raw onion, green chilli and a pickle. It began as a way of keeping rice edible through a hot night without a fridge, and it has become something people want for its own sake. Bengal and Odisha do the same thing and call it panta bhat and pakhala. Chhattisgarh has taken to marking its own version publicly on the first of May, the labour holiday, which tells you exactly whose food it is understood to be.

Bastar eats a third way.

South of the rice plain the land rises into the Bastar plateau, and the forest closes in. Sal trees dominate it, the rainfall is heavy, and the villages are largely Adivasi. Here the field is only part of the food supply, and the forest is the rest.

What the forest gives changes every few weeks. Bamboo shoots are cut young, boiled to take out the bitterness and then cooked or pickled. Boda mushrooms push up out of the ground after the first heavy rain and are sold along the roads for a fortnight, at prices that make them a small windfall. Mahua arrives in spring, tamarind and chironji after it, tendu leaves in between. A household that gathers well eats well, and the same list of ingredients turns into the year's cash.

The cooking is plainer than Malwa's and much less spiced. Aamat is a thin sour stew of whatever vegetables and bamboo shoot are to hand, soured with tamarind and seasoned with garlic and chilli. Rice is the constant, millet turns up in the drier stretches, and meat is eaten when there is meat. Reading four comes back to Bastar, because the kitchen there is inseparable from who lives in the forest and on what terms.

Why the two halves never merged.

More than forty years in the same state changed remarkably little. Wheat and rice were not a preference here but a consequence of soil and rainfall, and administrative boundaries do not move either. What did travel between the two halves is the middle ground: chila is made in both, from different flours; poha is sold in both; and the sweet shops look much the same in Indore and in Raipur. Reading three takes the Malwa half of the table dish by dish, and ends in the one kitchen in this region that was built for a court.
