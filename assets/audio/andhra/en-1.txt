Guntur heat and gongura sour.

Drive out of Guntur in February and the ground beside the road is red. Chillies lie drying on bare rock and on flat roofs, raked into long ridges and turned over by hand through the day. The workers who turn them keep their faces covered, and anyone walking past starts coughing within a minute. This is where a great deal of India's dried chilli comes from, and it is the first reason the Telugu kitchen tastes the way it does.

The chilli yards.

Guntur sits on the coastal plain between the Krishna and the Godavari, in country that is hot, flat and irrigated. Chilli goes into the ground after the monsoon and comes out from December onwards, and the crop is dried in the open before it is graded and sold. The market yard at Guntur handles an enormous volume of it, enough that traders across Asia quote Guntur prices, and cold stores ring the town so that the harvest can be released through the year rather than dumped in one month.

Two facts are worth holding on to, and both are safer stated plainly than dressed up. India grows and exports more dried chilli than any other country. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana together grow more of it than any other part of India. You will also read that Guntur has the largest chilli market in Asia, which may well be true, but the figures behind that claim move about, so this course says only that it is one of the largest and leaves it there.

What the Guntur chilli is.

The variety that carries the district's name is Guntur Sannam, and sannam means thin. The pod is slim, six to eight centimetres long, deep brick red when dried, with a fairly thick skin and a lot of seed. It is genuinely hot. Published Scoville figures for it sit in the tens of thousands, which puts it many times above the Kashmiri and Byadgi chillies that other regions grow mainly for colour. Those numbers vary by season, by grade and by whoever measured them, so treat the range as the honest answer rather than picking one figure out of it.

An Andhra cook uses chilli in two forms and they do different jobs. Whole dried pods go into hot oil at the start of a dish, where they scorch slightly and give a smoky background. Ground chilli, karam, goes in later with the other powders and carries the actual burn. Households buy the pods and grind their own, because a powder ground last month tastes of more than a powder bought last year.

Heat here is not a flourish at the end. It is the seasoning, and the cooking is built to carry it, which is why the next two ingredients matter as much as the chilli does.

Gongura, which is sour in the leaf.

Gongura is the leaf of Hibiscus sabdariffa, the roselle, a tall stemmed plant grown across the Telugu districts and picked young. What makes it unusual is where the sourness lives. Most cooking gets its acidity from something added to the pot: tamarind, lime, yoghurt, vinegar. Gongura arrives sour on its own. Bite a raw leaf and you get a sharp green acidity with a faint metallic edge behind it, closer to rhubarb stalk than to lemon.

Indian English usually calls it sorrel, and that is worth correcting once. True sorrel is Rumex acetosa, a completely different plant, and its acidity comes mostly from oxalic acid. Roselle is a hibiscus, it is grown across the tropics for its leaves and its calyces, and the same species gives the red hibiscus tea sold in Europe. The two taste similar enough for the nickname to have stuck, and they are not the same thing.

There are two kinds in the market. The green-stemmed leaf is milder and the red-stemmed one is sharper, and cooks who care will ask for the red. Cooked down, gongura collapses into a dark khaki mass in a few minutes, and from there it becomes either a pachadi, ground with fried chillies and tempered, or the base of a mutton dish. Reading three takes both further.

This is the part that makes the region a region. A chilli level tells you very little, because plenty of Indian kitchens are hot. A sour leaf that grows in one stretch of country and is eaten nowhere else in the same way tells you a great deal.

Tamarind, which does the everyday work.

Gongura is seasonal and a little grand. Tamarind, chintapandu in Telugu, is the sour that goes into food every day. It is sold as a pressed block with the seeds still in it, and it is soaked in hot water and squeezed by hand until the water turns brown and thick. That extract sours pappu, it makes pulihora what it is, and it goes into the thin peppery broths that close a meal.

Tamarind and gongura are not interchangeable, and a cook here would not swap one for the other. Tamarind is round, dark and slightly sweet, and it sits underneath a dish. Gongura is green, sharp and immediate, and it sits on top of one. Kerala keeps two sours apart for fish and for vegetables; this kitchen keeps two apart for every day and for the dish you serve a guest.

The tempering, and the powders beside it.

Almost every savoury dish starts or ends with a tempering, which Telugu calls popu. Groundnut or sesame oil goes into a pan, black mustard seed goes in and pops, and then a pinch of split black gram and split chickpea, two or three fenugreek seeds, a broken dried chilli, a fistful of curry leaf and a lentil-sized lump of asafoetida. It takes forty seconds and it is the taste of the region more reliably than any single spice.

Fenugreek, menthulu, needs a word of warning. It is bitter, and in this kitchen it is used in numbers you could count: three seeds in a tempering, a quarter of a spoonful roasted and ground into pulihora. Roasting it darkens the flavour and softens the bitterness, and a heavy hand with it ruins a dish in a way nothing later can repair.

Beside the tempering sits a row of dry powders called podi. Kandi podi is roasted lentils and chilli, ground coarse and mixed with hot rice and a spoonful of ghee. Karam podi is chilli-led and fierce. A podi is not a curry powder and is not cooked into anything: it is eaten dry, on rice or with a dosa, and it is how a Telugu household makes a plain meal taste of something on a bad day.

In the pantry: Guntur chilli.

 Aroma: Sun-dried fruit and faint smoke, sharper the moment it hits hot oil.

 Flavour: Clean, direct heat with a fruity edge; less sweet than Kashmiri, far hotter.

 What it does: Carries the burn and the red colour at once, and stands up to sour leaves and tamarind.

 When to add: Whole pods into the oil at the start; ground chilli later, off the heat, because powder scorches fast.

 Instead of it: Any hot dried red chilli plus a little Kashmiri for colour. Cayenne powder is close enough in heat.
