Guntur heat and gongura sour.

Drive out of Guntur in February and the ground beside the road is red. Chillies lie drying on bare rock and on flat roofs, raked into long ridges and turned over by hand through the day. The workers who turn them keep their faces covered, and anyone walking past starts coughing within a minute. This is where a great deal of India's dried chilli comes from, and it is the first reason the Telugu kitchen tastes the way it does.

Guntur sits on the coastal plain between the Krishna and the Godavari, in country that is hot, flat and irrigated. Chilli goes into the ground after the monsoon and comes out from December onwards, and the crop is dried in the open before it is graded and sold. The market yard at Guntur handles an enormous volume, and traders beyond India follow its prices. Cold stores around the town let growers and merchants release the harvest through the year.

Guntur is one of India's major chilli-growing and trading centres. Production totals and market rankings change from harvest to harvest, so the durable point is the district's long association with dried chilli and the protected Guntur Sannam name.

The variety that carries the district's name is Guntur Sannam, and sannam means thin. The pod is slim, six to eight centimetres long, deep brick red when dried, with a fairly thick skin and a lot of seed. It is genuinely hot. Published Scoville figures for it sit in the tens of thousands, which puts it many times above the Kashmiri and Byadgi chillies that other regions grow mainly for colour. Those numbers vary by season, by grade and by whoever measured them, so treat the range as the honest answer rather than picking one figure out of it.

An Andhra cook uses chilli in two forms and they do different jobs. Whole dried pods go into hot oil at the start of a dish, where they scorch slightly and give a smoky background. Ground chilli, karam, goes in later with the other powders and carries the actual burn. Households buy the pods and grind their own, because a powder ground last month tastes of more than a powder bought last year.

Heat here is not a flourish at the end. It is the seasoning, and the cooking is built to carry it, which is why the next two ingredients matter as much as the chilli does.

Gongura is the leaf of Hibiscus sabdariffa, the roselle, a tall stemmed plant grown across the Telugu districts and picked young. What makes it unusual is where the sourness lives. Most cooking gets its acidity from something added to the pot: tamarind, lime, yoghurt, vinegar. Gongura arrives sour on its own. Bite a raw leaf and you get a sharp green acidity with a faint metallic edge behind it, closer to rhubarb stalk than to lemon.

Indian English usually calls it sorrel, and that is worth correcting once. True sorrel is Rumex acetosa, a completely different plant, and its acidity comes mostly from oxalic acid. Roselle is a hibiscus, it is grown across the tropics for its leaves and its calyces, and the same species gives the red hibiscus tea sold in Europe. The two taste similar enough for the nickname to have stuck, and they are not the same thing.

There are two kinds in the market. The green-stemmed leaf is milder and the red-stemmed one is sharper, and cooks who care will ask for the red. Cooked down, gongura collapses into a dark khaki mass in a few minutes, and from there it becomes either a pachadi, ground with fried chillies and tempered, or the base of a mutton dish. Reading three takes both further.

Heat alone does not identify a regional kitchen. Gongura is more revealing: Telugu cooks use the sour leaf in dal, relish and meat dishes, giving related dishes a flavour that chilli by itself cannot supply.

Tamarind, chintapandu in Telugu, supplies another kind of sourness. It is sold as a pressed block, soaked in warm water and squeezed into an extract. That extract can sour pappu, forms the seasoning paste for pulihora and appears in thin peppery broths.

Tamarind and gongura give different results. Tamarind is dark, rounded and slightly sweet; gongura is greener and more immediate. Recipes choose between them for flavour and texture rather than treating either as a universal substitute.

Many savoury dishes start or end with a tempering, often called popu in Telugu. One common version uses mustard seed, split pulses, dried chilli, curry leaf and asafoetida in hot oil, but the combination changes with the dish and household.

Fenugreek, menthulu, needs a word of warning. It is bitter, and in this kitchen it is used in numbers you could count: three seeds in a tempering, a quarter of a spoonful roasted and ground into pulihora. Roasting it darkens the flavour and softens the bitterness, and a heavy hand with it ruins a dish in a way nothing later can repair.

Dry powders called podi provide another layer of seasoning. Kandi podi combines roasted pulses and chilli and can be mixed with hot rice and ghee. Karam podi is chilli-led. Some podis are served dry with rice or a crepe, while others are used during cooking, so the word describes a powder rather than one fixed recipe.
