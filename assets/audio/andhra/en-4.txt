Qutb Shahis, Nizams and a Deccan capital.

Charminar stands at a crossroads in Hyderabad's old city, four minarets over four arches. Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah commissioned it in 1591 as the new city took shape on the plain beyond Golconda. Sources give several explanations for the monument, so its date and central place in the city are firmer than any single origin legend.

Golconda is a granite hill with a fort wrapped around it, and it was the seat of the Qutb Shahi sultans from the early sixteenth century. The founder, Sultan Quli, came from Hamadan in Persia, arrived in the Deccan as a soldier in the service of the Bahmani sultanate, and broke away as its authority collapsed. His descendants ruled from the hill for most of a century.

Golconda became famous as a centre for diamonds mined in the wider Deccan and traded through the kingdom. Famous stones are often assigned to particular mines in later accounts, but individual provenances are not always secure. The durable connection is between the diamond trade, the Qutb Shahi state and Golconda's international reputation for wealth.

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founded Hyderabad on the plain in 1591 and placed Charminar at the centre of its planned urban core. Accounts differ over the precise reasons for the move from Golconda.

Aurangzeb took Golconda in 1687 after a long siege, and the sultanate ended. Hyderabad became a Mughal province, and thirty-seven years later its governor stopped taking orders. Mir Qamar-ud-din Khan, titled Nizam-ul-Mulk and remembered as Asaf Jah I, established his own authority in 1724 as Mughal power drained away from the centre. His line, the Asaf Jahi Nizams, ruled from Hyderabad until 1948.

Hyderabad State was the largest of the princely states under British paramountcy and among the wealthiest. It covered much of the Deccan and issued its own currency. Reports called the seventh Nizam, Mir Osman Ali Khan, the world's richest man. Such wealth supported large court kitchens and long supply lines for saffron, dried fruit and nuts.

When the British left in 1947 the Nizam did not accede to either India or Pakistan and tried to remain independent. In September 1948 the Indian army entered the state in an operation code-named Polo, resistance collapsed within a week, and Hyderabad became part of India.

What happened around that week is contested and it was violent. Large-scale communal violence followed the operation, most of it against Muslims, and an official committee under Pandit Sunderlal investigated and reported to the government of India. That report was not published at the time and only became publicly available decades later. Estimates of the dead vary by an enormous margin between sources, and the episode is argued over to this day by historians and by the communities involved. This course names it and does not adjudicate it, because the honest answer is that the figures and the responsibility are still disputed.

Two later dates reshape the map. Andhra State was created in 1953; in 1956 it joined Telangana districts from the former Hyderabad State to form Andhra Pradesh, with Hyderabad as capital. Telangana became a separate state in 2014. Hyderabad is its capital, while this course groups the neighbouring states for study without claiming that their kitchens are identical.

Listen to the vocabulary of the food and you can hear the whole history without being told it. Pappu, pulihora, pachadi, avakaya, gongura, mamsam and podi are Telugu, a Dravidian language spoken across both states by the great majority of people. Biryani, haleem, salan, baghar, khubani and dum are not Telugu at all. They come through Dakhni.

Dakhni developed in the Deccan through contact among Indo-Aryan speech, Persian and Arabic literary cultures, and regional languages including Marathi, Kannada and Telugu. It became a language of poetry and courtly life as well as everyday speech. Food words such as salan, baghar and khubani reflect that multilingual history, although no vocabulary list divides neatly into court and countryside.

Hyderabadi cooking combines Deccan produce with ingredients carried through inland and maritime trade. Saffron, rose water, nuts and dried fruit travelled along those routes; cardamom and other spices also connected the city to growing regions elsewhere in southern India. Courtly demand helped sustain these supply lines.

The result is not a clean division between local food and foreign food. Rice, pulses, chilli, tamarind, sesame, groundnut and gongura meet traded aromatics, dried fruit and nuts in a city shaped by Telugu, Dakhni and other Deccan cultures.
