Qutb Shahis and Nizams: Persia in the Deccan.

Charminar stands at a crossroads in the old city of Hyderabad, four minarets over four arches, and the market has grown so tightly around it that you can buy bangles from a stall leaning against its plinth. It went up in 1591. The court had just moved eleven kilometres down from the fortress at Golconda onto open ground by the Musi river, and this was the first thing built in the new city. Everything in this reading runs through that move.

Golconda, and a dynasty from Hamadan.

Golconda is a granite hill with a fort wrapped around it, and it was the seat of the Qutb Shahi sultans from the early sixteenth century. The founder, Sultan Quli, came from Hamadan in Persia, arrived in the Deccan as a soldier in the service of the Bahmani sultanate, and broke away as its authority collapsed. His descendants ruled from the hill for most of a century.

What made Golconda rich was under the ground about a hundred and fifty kilometres away. The mines of the Krishna valley, Kollur above all, produced the finest diamonds anyone in the world had seen, and Golconda was where they were cut and sold. The Koh-i-Noor and the Hope diamond both came out of that trade. For two centuries the word Golconda meant a diamond market rather than a fort, and European merchants used it as a synonym for wealth.

By 1590 the hill had a water problem and no room to grow, so Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah laid out a planned city on the plain with Charminar at its centre. He is also the reason this reading ends up talking about language, but that comes later.

From Mughal province to Nizam's state.

Aurangzeb took Golconda in 1687 after a long siege, and the sultanate ended. Hyderabad became a Mughal province, and thirty-seven years later its governor stopped taking orders. Mir Qamar-ud-din Khan, titled Nizam-ul-Mulk and remembered as Asaf Jah I, established his own authority in 1724 as Mughal power drained away from the centre. His line, the Asaf Jahi Nizams, ruled from Hyderabad until 1948.

Hyderabad State was the largest of the princely states under British paramountcy and among the wealthiest. It covered a great deal of the Deccan, it issued its own currency, and the seventh Nizam, Mir Osman Ali Khan, was reported in the 1930s as the richest man in the world. Whether or not that headline stood up, the spending was real, and a court of that size supported a professional kitchen with cooks, suppliers and a standing order for saffron, dried fruit and nuts from the north.

1948.

When the British left in 1947 the Nizam did not accede to either India or Pakistan and tried to remain independent. In September 1948 the Indian army entered the state in an operation code-named Polo, resistance collapsed within a week, and Hyderabad became part of India.

What happened around that week is contested and it was violent. Large-scale communal violence followed the operation, most of it against Muslims, and an official committee under Pandit Sunderlal investigated and reported to the government of India. That report was not published at the time and only became publicly available decades later. Estimates of the dead vary by an enormous margin between sources, and the episode is argued over to this day by historians and by the communities involved. This course names it and does not adjudicate it, because the honest answer is that the figures and the responsibility are still disputed.

Two later dates finish the map. In 1956 the Telugu-speaking districts were reorganised into the state of Andhra Pradesh, with Hyderabad as its capital. In 2014 Telangana separated from Andhra Pradesh and became a state of its own, and Hyderabad is now its capital. The kitchen did not divide, which is why this course treats the two as one region.

Two languages on one plate.

Listen to the vocabulary of the food and you can hear the whole history without being told it. Pappu, pulihora, pachadi, avakaya, gongura, mamsam and podi are Telugu, a Dravidian language spoken across both states by the great majority of people. Biryani, haleem, salan, baghar, khubani and dum are not Telugu at all. They come through Dakhni.

Dakhni is the southern form of the language that became Urdu, and it grew in the Deccan from the fourteenth century onwards as northern soldiers, administrators and Sufis settled among Marathi, Kannada and Telugu speakers. It took Persian and Arabic vocabulary from above and local grammar and words from around it, and it developed a literature well before Urdu had one in the north. Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah, who built Charminar, is generally credited as the first poet in the language to leave a complete collected divan. A city that produced court poetry in Dakhni in the 1590s was always going to produce a court kitchen that named its dishes in it.

What Persia actually sent.

The aromatics of the Hyderabadi kitchen are the clearest import of all. Saffron, rose water, green cardamom, mace, almonds, pistachios and dried apricots are not Deccan crops, with cardamom the partial exception, and they arrived through a trade that ran from Persia and Central Asia into the Deccan by land and by sea. A court founded by a soldier from Hamadan went on cooking with what it had cooked with at home, and it could afford to import all of it.

So the two kitchens of this region are not two moods. They are two supply lines. The Telugu kitchen cooks what the coastal plain and the plateau grow: rice, pigeon peas, chilli, tamarind, sesame, groundnut, gongura. The Hyderabadi kitchen cooks the same goat and the same rice with things carried a very long way, and the result is perfumed rather than hot. A sweet made of dried apricots at the end of a meal in a city on the Deccan is a receipt, and reading three ended with it on purpose.
