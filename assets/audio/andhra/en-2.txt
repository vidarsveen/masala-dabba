Rice, pappu and mango pickle.

Rice, ghee and pappu form a familiar opening in many Telugu meals. Other dishes may be mixed into the rice in turn, with pickles and relishes used in small amounts. Meal order varies by household and occasion, but the combination shows how a mild staple can carry sharper flavours.

Pappu is a Telugu word for cooked pulses and dal dishes. Toor dal is common, cooked until soft and often paired with a vegetable or souring ingredient. Tomato gives tomato pappu, yellow cucumber gives dosakaya pappu, raw mango gives mamidikaya pappu, and gongura gives gongura pappu.

Methods vary. The dal and vegetable may cook together or separately before they are combined, and a tempering often finishes the dish. The consistency can range from loose enough to mix through rice to considerably thicker.

Pappu is also why the meal opens gently. Everything that follows it is hot, sour or both, and a plate of rice needs one thing on it that is neither.

Pulihora is a family of sour seasoned rice dishes; this version uses tamarind. Cooked rice is spread in a clean tray so steam can escape and the grains separate. Tamarind extract is cooked with oil, turmeric, chilli and tempering until concentrated, then folded through the rice. Serve promptly, or refrigerate within two hours and chill quickly.

Letting steam escape makes the rice easier to mix without crushing it. Peanuts and split chickpeas add texture, while a little roasted fenugreek and sesame deepens the tamarind seasoning. These are features of the course recipe rather than rules for every pulihora.

Pulihora is made at home and also distributed as prasadam at temples, including Tirupati. Acidity does not make cooked rice safe to hold indefinitely: for a packed meal, cool it quickly, keep it chilled and follow ordinary cooked-rice storage rules.

Pesarattu resembles a dosa on the plate and cooks on the same kind of griddle, but its batter is usually prepared for same-day use rather than deliberately fermented overnight.

The batter is whole green gram, pesalu, soaked for a few hours and ground with green chilli, ginger and cumin, sometimes with a handful of rice for crispness. It is used at once. There is no overnight rise, no sourness and no urad dal, and the crepe comes off the griddle green, a little chewy, and tasting distinctly of the pulse rather than of fermentation.

The standard partner is allam pachadi, a dark ginger and tamarind chutney, which is sharp enough to cut through the pulse. A pesarattu with a layer of upma folded inside it is called MLA pesarattu, and the story is that it was named for the legislators eating it near the assembly in Hyderabad. Take that as a good story rather than a documented fact.

A pachadi is a fresh relish, ground and eaten within a couple of days. Gongura pachadi is the one to know. The leaves are wilted in oil until they collapse, then ground coarsely with fried dried chillies, garlic and salt, and a tempering of mustard seed, fenugreek, urad dal and curry leaf goes over the top. The result is dark, thick and very sharp, and a teaspoon of it flavours a whole plate of rice.

Almost anything becomes a pachadi here: tomato, aubergine, ridge gourd, coriander, ginger. What they share is method rather than ingredients. Something is cooked briefly in oil, ground with chilli and salt, and finished with a tempering, and it is then eaten as a condiment rather than as a dish.

Avakaya pairs firm unripe mango with mustard, chilli, salt and oil. Traditional households make large seasonal batches by established methods, but salt and an oil layer alone do not establish a safe shelf life for an untested home recipe.

The course therefore teaches a refrigerated adaptation. Cut the mango flesh safely away from the stone, mix a small batch with the seasonings, place it in a clean jar and refrigerate immediately. Let the flavours develop in the refrigerator and use a clean utensil each time.

Oil carries flavour and limits contact with air, but it is not a complete safety process. This recipe makes no claim to a tested room-temperature or year-long shelf life. Discard the pickle if it develops mould, fizzing, a bulging lid, slime or an off smell.
