Rice, pappu and the pickle that lasts a year.

The first mouthful of a Telugu meal is almost always the same. Rice goes down in the middle of the plate, a spoonful of ghee goes into a well in the top of it, and a ladle of pappu goes over that. The eater mixes the three with the fingers of the right hand and eats it plain, before anything sour or hot arrives. It is a deliberately quiet opening to a meal that is about to be very loud, and nobody here would start the other way round.

Pappu, the dal that anchors everything.

Pappu is the Telugu word for dal, and the everyday version is toor dal, the split pigeon pea, cooked until it falls apart. What makes it a regional dish rather than a generic one is that a vegetable is almost always cooked into it, and the vegetable names it. Tomato gives tomato pappu. A yellow cucumber gives dosakaya pappu. Raw mango gives mamidikaya pappu in the spring, and gongura gives gongura pappu, which is the sour leaf doing the souring instead of tamarind.

The method hardly changes. The dal and the vegetable are pressure-cooked together with turmeric, mashed a little, salted, and soured if the vegetable has not already done that. A tempering of mustard seed, cumin, dried chilli, garlic and curry leaf goes over at the end. The dal is loose enough to run into rice and thick enough to coat it, and it is never a soup eaten on its own.

Pappu is also why the meal opens gently. Everything that follows it is hot, sour or both, and a plate of rice needs one thing on it that is neither.

Pulihora, which is rice cooked twice.

Pulihora is tamarind rice, and the name says so: puli is sour. Rice is boiled, spread out to cool and loosened so the grains stay separate. Meanwhile tamarind extract is cooked down in sesame oil with turmeric, chilli and a great deal of tempering, until it darkens and thickens into a paste that holds together on the spoon. That paste is folded through the cool rice, and the whole thing is left to stand for an hour before anybody eats it.

Three things in it are non-negotiable. The rice must be cold, because warm rice turns to paste when you fold anything through it. The tempering carries peanuts and split chickpeas, which stay crunchy and give every mouthful something to bite. And a small quantity of roasted fenugreek and sesame is ground in at the end, which is what separates a good pulihora from a sour one.

It is temple food as much as home food. Pulihora is distributed as prasadam at Tirupati and at temples across the Telugu country, and it keeps for a day without a fridge, which is exactly why it travels to the temple and to the train in the first place.

Pesarattu is not a dosa.

Tamil Nadu's module explains the fermented batter at length: rice and urad dal, ground apart, left overnight, raised by wild bacteria that live on the lentil. Pesarattu looks like a dosa on the plate and is made on the same griddle, and it is built on the opposite principle. Nothing ferments.

The batter is whole green gram, pesalu, soaked for a few hours and ground with green chilli, ginger and cumin, sometimes with a handful of rice for crispness. It is used at once. There is no overnight rise, no sourness and no urad dal, and the crepe comes off the griddle green, a little chewy, and tasting distinctly of the pulse rather than of fermentation.

The standard partner is allam pachadi, a dark ginger and tamarind chutney, which is sharp enough to cut through the pulse. A pesarattu with a layer of upma folded inside it is called MLA pesarattu, and the story is that it was named for the legislators eating it near the assembly in Hyderabad. Take that as a good story rather than a documented fact.

Pachadi, and the difference between a chutney and a pickle.

A pachadi is a fresh relish, ground and eaten within a couple of days. Gongura pachadi is the one to know. The leaves are wilted in oil until they collapse, then ground coarsely with fried dried chillies, garlic and salt, and a tempering of mustard seed, fenugreek, urad dal and curry leaf goes over the top. The result is dark, thick and very sharp, and a teaspoon of it flavours a whole plate of rice.

Almost anything becomes a pachadi here: tomato, aubergine, ridge gourd, coriander, ginger. What they share is method rather than ingredients. Something is cooked briefly in oil, ground with chilli and salt, and finished with a tempering, and it is then eaten as a condiment rather than as a dish.

Avakaya, the pickle that has to last a year.

Avakaya is the most serious thing in this reading and the easiest to get wrong. The name is literal: ava is mustard, kaya is an unripe fruit. It is made once a year, in May, when the hard sour mangoes are in, and the jar has to feed the household until the following May.

The mango is cut with a heavy blade into large pieces, and each piece keeps a fragment of the hard inner shell attached to it. That shell is not laziness. It holds the piece together through a year in salt and stops it turning to mush. The wet seed inside is scraped out and thrown away, and the cut pieces are wiped dry, because a drop of water is what starts mould.

Then the proportions, which are not timid. Mustard is dried and ground to a coarse powder, chilli powder is measured out in the same order of quantity, and salt goes in generously. Those three are mixed with the mango and with a great deal of sesame or groundnut oil, and the jar is filled and left. Over a few days the salt pulls juice out of the mango, the mustard swells, and the whole thing settles under a standing layer of oil.

That oil layer is the preservation, and everything about handling the jar follows from it. The pickle must stay under oil, so more is topped up if the level drops. The jar is stirred with a dry ladle for the first week and then left alone. And no wet spoon ever goes into it, because water carries the organisms that salt and oil are there to keep out. A single wet spoon can lose a household a year of pickle, which is why in many houses one person owns the jar and nobody else opens it.
