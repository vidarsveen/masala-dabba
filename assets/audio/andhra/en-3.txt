Biryani, haleem and the gongura mutton.

A meal in coastal Andhra might place rice and pappu beside gongura pachadi and goat cooked with sour leaves. In Hyderabad, a cook may break the dough seal on a biryani scented with mint and fried onion. This course groups the neighbouring states in one module while keeping these traditions distinct.

One celebrated form of Hyderabadi biryani uses the kacchi method, and kacchi means raw. Marinated raw meat goes into the bottom of a heavy pot, partly cooked rice goes on top, and the lid is sealed with dough. Gentle heat then cooks the meat and finishes the rice together.

The timing is demanding. A pakki method cooks the meat before layering it with partly cooked rice. With kacchi biryani, the cut, marinade, rice texture and heat must be judged so the meat becomes tender without overcooking the rice. Recipes use different tenderising ingredients and cooking arrangements to reach that balance.

The pot matters as much as the recipe does. A heavy vessel holds an even, gentle heat, and the dough seal means nothing evaporates, so the rice steams in the meat's own juices. The seal is broken at the table, and the smell that comes out of it is half the dish.

This is one of many Indian biryani traditions rather than an uncontested original. Lucknow, Kolkata, Thalassery and other cities have their own forms. The kacchi method is strongly associated with Hyderabad, and its rice cooks in the juices released by the marinated meat below.

Mirchi ka salan, long green chillies in a peanut-and-sesame gravy, and a yoghurt accompaniment are common partners. Their acidity and looser texture balance the rich rice.

Haleem is especially visible in Hyderabad during Ramadan. Wheat, meat, lentils and spices cook for hours and are beaten or mixed into a cohesive, spoonable dish, commonly topped with fried onion, herbs and lemon.

Hyderabadi haleem is related to harees traditions from West Asia but developed its own local seasoning, texture and commercial culture. Its geographical indication was registered in 2010 and defines the protected Hyderabad name and production area.

Long cooking hydrates the grain and softens the meat; beating distributes the fibres through the thickened grain and pulse mixture. The cohesive texture is one of the dish's defining features.

Now back to the Telugu side, and to the dish that puts this region on a plate more precisely than the biryani does. Gongura mamsam is goat cooked with the sour leaf from reading one. The meat is browned with onion, ginger, garlic and a lot of chilli and cooked until it is nearly tender. A separate pan of gongura is wilted down to a dark mass, ground coarsely, and folded in for the last stretch of cooking.

In this version the goat cooks nearly tender before the gongura is added. That order keeps the leaf's flavour vivid and lets the cook adjust its acidity near the end. Other versions use different sequences and may include additional thickeners or seasonings.

No free photograph of gongura mamsam exists for this course to use, so the earlier picture of the leaves is as close as we can get. The dish looks like a dark, loose meat curry with no obvious green in it at all, because the leaves have gone almost black by the time it is finished.

Bagara baingan is a well-known Hyderabadi vegetable dish. Small round aubergines are slit without cutting them through, then fried until their skins blister. They finish in a gravy of roasted peanut, sesame and dry coconut ground with tamarind, onion and chilli.

The word bagara is related to baghar, a term for tempering. Bagara khana is a mildly spiced rice, and ground peanut, sesame and coconut may be sold as bagara masala. Mirchi ka salan uses a related gravy with chillies instead of aubergines.

Qubani ka meetha is a Hyderabadi sweet of dried apricots soaked and stewed until glossy, then served warm or cold, often with cream, thickened milk or custard.

Dried apricots reached the Deccan through trade from cooler growing regions to the north and northwest. Their place in a Hyderabad dessert shows how courtly demand and long-distance supply joined local ingredients at the same table.
