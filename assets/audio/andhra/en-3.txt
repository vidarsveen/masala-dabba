Biryani, haleem and the gongura mutton.

At two in the afternoon in a small town in coastal Andhra, lunch is rice, pappu, a fierce gongura pachadi and a bowl of goat cooked with sour leaves. Ninety kilometres inland and a world away, a Hyderabadi kitchen is breaking the dough seal off a pot that has been sitting on low coals, and what comes out smells of saffron, mint and fried onion. Both are the same region. Neither is a version of the other, and this reading is mostly about keeping them apart.

The kacchi method.

Hyderabadi biryani is made by the kacchi method, and kacchi means raw. Marinated raw meat goes into the bottom of a heavy pot, rice that has been boiled for only a few minutes goes on top of it, and the lid is sealed to the pot with a rope of dough. The pot then cooks slowly, usually with coals on the lid as well as heat underneath, and the meat and the rice finish at the same moment.

That is harder than it sounds, and it is worth understanding why. In the commoner pakki method the meat is cooked first as a curry and the rice is cooked separately, and the two are layered together and warmed through. Nothing can go badly wrong. In the kacchi method the cook is making a single bet: the rice will be done in about the same time as the goat. If the meat needs longer than the rice, the top of the pot turns to porridge. If the rice needs longer, the meat is tough. Everything in the recipe exists to close that gap. The meat is cut small, it is marinated for hours in yoghurt and raw papaya to tenderise it, and the rice is pulled off the boil while it still has a hard core.

The pot matters as much as the recipe does. A heavy vessel holds an even, gentle heat, and the dough seal means nothing evaporates, so the rice steams in the meat's own juices. The seal is broken at the table, and the smell that comes out of it is half the dish.

Two things are worth saying plainly. This is not the only biryani in India and not the original one, whatever a restaurant menu claims. Lucknow, Kolkata, Thalassery and half a dozen other cities have their own, and Awadh has its own module in this course. What is fair to say is that the kacchi method is the Hyderabadi signature, and that a biryani made this way tastes of raw meat juices going up into the rice rather than of a curry sitting under it.

It is never served alone. Mirchi ka salan, long green chillies in a thin peanut and sesame gravy, sits beside it, and so does a bowl of thinned yoghurt with onion and mint. The salan is doing a real job: the biryani is rich and dry, and the salan is sour, thin and hot.

Haleem, and eight hours of pounding.

Haleem arrives once a year, in Ramadan, and for that month it is sold from vats outside restaurants all over Hyderabad. Wheat is soaked, goat or mutton is cooked down with lentils and whole spices, and then the whole mass is pounded for hours with a heavy wooden paddle until nothing in it is recognisable. What you eat is a thick, smooth, pale brown paste, eaten with a spoon, topped with fried onion, mint, lemon and slivers of fried cashew.

The dish came from the Arab harees and travelled with soldiers and traders, and Hyderabad turned it into something of its own by adding far more spice and by making it a public, commercial, once-a-year event. In 2010 Hyderabadi haleem was granted a geographical indication, which was the first time an Indian prepared meat dish had one. The GI covers haleem made in and around Hyderabad to a specified method, and it is checkable, which is more than can be said for most origin stories in this course.

Why pound it at all? Because the point of the dish is that the wheat starch and the meat protein end up in the same suspension. Nothing separates, nothing is chewy, and a fasting person can eat a very large quantity of concentrated food quickly. The texture is the recipe.

Gongura mamsam.

Now back to the Telugu side, and to the dish that puts this region on a plate more precisely than the biryani does. Gongura mamsam is goat cooked with the sour leaf from reading one. The meat is browned with onion, ginger, garlic and a lot of chilli and cooked until it is nearly tender. A separate pan of gongura is wilted down to a dark mass, ground coarsely, and folded in for the last stretch of cooking.

The order matters. Gongura is acidic, and acid slows meat down, so a cook who puts the leaves in at the start is still waiting an hour later. Added late, the acidity cuts the fat of the goat directly and the dish tastes sharp rather than heavy. There is no yoghurt in it, no cream, no coconut, and nothing thickens the gravy except the leaves and the onion.

No free photograph of gongura mamsam exists for this course to use, so the earlier picture of the leaves is as close as we can get. The dish looks like a dark, loose meat curry with no obvious green in it at all, because the leaves have gone almost black by the time it is finished.

Bagara baingan.

Bagara baingan is the Hyderabadi vegetable dish that everybody remembers. Small round aubergines are slit into quarters without cutting them through, fried whole until the skins blister, and then simmered in a gravy of roasted peanut, sesame and dry coconut ground to a paste, with tamarind, onion and chilli. The aubergines stay whole on the plate and the gravy is thick, dark and slightly grainy.

The word bagara comes from baghar, the tempering, and it turns up across this kitchen: bagara khana is a mildly spiced rice, and the ground mixture of peanut, sesame and coconut is often sold as bagara masala. Mirchi ka salan uses very nearly the same gravy with chillies instead of aubergines, which tells you that the paste is the dish and the vegetable is a passenger.

Qubani ka meetha.

A Hyderabadi meal ends with dried apricots. Qubani ka meetha is khubani, dried apricot, soaked overnight, stewed with sugar until it breaks down into a dark, glossy compote, and served warm or cold with cream, thick set milk or custard. The blanched kernel from inside the stone is often put back on top, which tastes faintly of almond.

Stop and look at that ingredient for a moment. Apricots do not grow on the Deccan plateau. They are dried in the far north and in Central Asia and they arrive here as trade goods, and they have been arriving since the court that reading four is about. A sweet made of imported dried fruit, at the end of a meal in a city five hundred metres above a tropical coast, is the clearest single sign that two different food cultures are sitting at the same table.
