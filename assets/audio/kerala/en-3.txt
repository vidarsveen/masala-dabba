Meen curry, appam and the sadya.

Kerala has a reputation for heat, and the reputation is half deserved. Some of its food is fierce, and some of it is deliberately, almost startlingly mild. Working out which is which is the quickest way into the kitchen, because the two styles come from different communities and have been sitting side by side for a very long time.

Meen curry: the everyday dish.

Fish curry is what a coastal household eats most days, and the Kerala version is thin, red and sharply sour. Chilli, turmeric, fenugreek, shallot, ginger and a fistful of kudampuli go into a clay pot with the fish, and the pot goes on a low flame until the gravy tightens. There is no coconut milk in the strictest version, which is why it looks so lean next to the curries most people expect from the region.

Two things about it are worth copying. It is cooked in unglazed clay, which holds heat gently and, once seasoned, carries the flavour of every curry made in it before. And it is better the next day, because the kudampuli goes on working overnight. A Kerala cook will make it in the evening for lunch tomorrow, which is the opposite of how a European treats fish.

The luxury version uses karimeen, the pearl spot, a small flat backwater fish that is the state fish of Kerala. It is coated in masala, wrapped in a banana leaf and cooked in a dry pan, so the leaf scorches and perfumes the fish steaming inside. Any firm-fleshed whole fish works the same way, and in Norway a small farmed trout or a plaice does the job well.

Stew, and the dish with no chilli in it.

Now the other style. Ishtu, the stew that comes with appam, is the cooking of the Syrian Christian houses of central Travancore, and it contains no chilli powder at all. Chicken or mutton or potato simmers in thin coconut milk with whole spices, green chilli, curry leaf and a great deal of sliced shallot, and thick coconut milk goes in at the end off the heat. The result is pale, sweet and gentle, and it tastes of cardamom, clove and pepper rather than of burn.

That mildness is not a compromise. These communities have had the expensive whole spices on their doorstep for centuries, and a dish built to show them off has no reason to bury them under chilli.

Avial, and the coconut vegetables.

Avial is the dish to understand if you want to understand the vegetarian side. Long batons of whatever vegetables are in the house go into a pot with turmeric and green chilli and very little water, and when they are just cooked a paste of grated coconut, cumin and more green chilli goes in, followed by sour curd and finally a spoonful of raw coconut oil and a handful of curry leaves. Nothing is fried, nothing browns, and the finished dish is thick, pale and fresh.

The raw coconut oil at the end is the part outsiders leave out and should not. It is doing what good olive oil does over a Tuscan soup, and without it the dish tastes flat.

Beef fry and parotta.

The pairing that has travelled furthest out of Kerala is parotta and beef fry. The bread is wheat dough rested, oiled, coiled into a spiral and slapped out flat, so it cooks into a stack of loose layers that pull apart in threads. The beef is cubed, pressure-cooked with pepper, coriander and garam masala until it is soft, then fried down hard in coconut oil with curry leaves and slivers of fresh coconut until the pieces are dark and almost dry.

It is cheap food, eaten standing up at a roadside shop late in the evening, and it is the single strongest argument against the idea that Indian food means a bowl of sauce.

The sadya.

Once or twice a year the whole system arrives at once. A sadya is served on a banana leaf with the narrow end to the eater's left, and it carries between twenty and twenty-six items, all vegetarian, each in a fixed position. Pickles and salt go at the top left, dry vegetables and thorans along the top, then the wetter dishes, with rice heaped in the centre of the lower half.

The order in which servers come round is the recipe. Parippu, a plain dal, goes over the rice first with a spoon of ghee. Sambar follows, then rasam, then buttermilk, and each is mixed and eaten before the next arrives. It ends with payasam, a sweet milk or jaggery pudding, which many people eat with a pappadam crumbled into it. Folding the leaf towards you at the end means you enjoyed it, and folding it away means you did not, so it is worth knowing which way to fold.
