Meen curry, appam and the sadya.

Kerala cooking includes chilli-hot dishes, peppery dishes and deliberately mild ones. These contrasts cross regional, religious and household traditions rather than dividing the state into two fixed community styles.

One important Kerala fish-curry style is thin, red and sharply sour. Chilli, turmeric, fenugreek, shallot, ginger and kudampuli simmer with the fish. Some versions omit coconut milk; others use it, and coastal formulas differ across districts and communities.

An unglazed clay pot can hold gentle heat and is traditional for many versions. For next-day flavour, cool the curry promptly in a shallow container, refrigerate it within two hours and reheat it thoroughly. Kudampuli adds acidity and aroma but does not preserve cooked fish at room temperature.

The luxury version uses karimeen, the pearl spot, a small flat backwater fish that is the state fish of Kerala. It is coated in masala, wrapped in a banana leaf and cooked in a dry pan, so the leaf scorches and perfumes the fish steaming inside. Any firm-fleshed whole fish works the same way, and in Norway a small farmed trout or a plaice does the job well.

Ishtu, often served with appam, is strongly associated with Syrian Christian cooking in central Kerala but is now shared more widely. Chicken, goat, potato or vegetables may simmer in coconut milk with whole spices, green chilli, curry leaf and shallot. This course version omits chilli powder and adds richer coconut milk near the end.

Its pale colour and gentle heat leave cardamom, clove and pepper easy to recognise. Other household versions change the meat, vegetables and level of chilli.

Avial brings mixed vegetables together with coconut and cumin. Batons of firm vegetables cook with turmeric, green chilli and little water. A coconut paste follows, then sour curd in this version. Coconut oil and curry leaves finish the dish, which stays thick and pale without browning.

The raw coconut oil at the end is the part outsiders leave out and should not. It is doing what good olive oil does over a Tuscan soup, and without it the dish tastes flat.

Parotta and beef fry has travelled widely beyond Kerala. Rested, oiled wheat dough is stretched, coiled and flattened so it cooks into loose layers. The beef is cooked tender with spices, then fried in coconut oil with curry leaves and coconut until the pieces are dark and nearly dry.

Parotta and beef fry is a prominent restaurant and roadside pairing in Kerala. Its dry, layered textures challenge the narrow idea that Indian food must arrive as a bowl of sauce.

A sadya is a vegetarian feast served on a banana leaf. Many hosts place pickles and small accompaniments along the upper part, rice below, and serve liquid dishes in a recognisable progression. The exact number, position and order vary by region, family and occasion.

Parippu with ghee, sambar, rasam and buttermilk may arrive in sequence for mixing with rice. Payasam, made with milk, coconut milk or jaggery depending on the version, supplies the sweet course. Leaf-folding etiquette is not uniform enough to use as a reliable verdict on the meal.
