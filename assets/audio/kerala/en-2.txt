Rice in four shapes, coconut in four more.

In most of India the staple question has one answer per region: wheat in Punjab, millet in Rajasthan, rice in Bengal. Kerala answers rice as well, but the interesting part is not the grain. It is that a cook here will turn the same rice into a steamed cylinder at breakfast, a bowl of chewy red grains at lunch and a lacy fermented pancake at supper, and will think of those as three unrelated foods.

Matta rice, and why it is parboiled.

The rice on a Kerala plate is short, fat, reddish-brown and noticeably chewy. It is matta, and it has been parboiled: soaked, steamed in the husk and dried again before milling. That sounds like extra work for nothing, and it is not. Steaming in the husk drives vitamins from the bran into the grain, so parboiled rice keeps nutrition that polishing would otherwise throw away. It also hardens the starch, which matters in a climate where a sack of rice has to survive a monsoon, and it stops the grains from collapsing into paste when they are cooked in a lot of water.

The texture is the point. Matta rice is coarse enough to stand up to a fish curry sharp with kudampuli, and it is what you want under something rich. Basmati would disappear underneath the same sauce.

The batters that ferment.

Three of Kerala's four best-known breakfasts are made from ground rice, and two of them ferment overnight. Soaked rice is ground with grated coconut, and the batter is left out in a warm kitchen until it rises and smells faintly sour. In a house that still makes toddy, a cupful of the fresh palm sap does the raising, because it is already fermenting; most kitchens now use a pinch of yeast instead.

Appam is that batter cooked in a small round-bottomed pan, swirled so the edges run thin. The rim crisps into lace and the middle stays thick and spongy, which gives one bread two textures and a bowl in the centre for stew. Idiyappam, string hoppers, uses a stiff dough of roasted rice flour pressed through a sieve into nests and steamed. Puttu is rice flour and grated coconut packed in layers into a bamboo or steel cylinder and steamed until it slides out as a striped log.

Puttu is the one to try first, because it shows the method plainly. There is no fat in it and no leavening, so it lives or dies on the moisture of the flour, and every Kerala cook has an opinion about that. The test is whether the crumb holds together when you press it and falls apart when you do not.

Coconut, in four states.

Coconut is not one ingredient here. It arrives in four forms, and a recipe that says "coconut" without saying which one is useless.

Grated fresh goes into thoran, where vegetables are cut small and tossed with coconut, chilli and mustard seed until barely cooked. Thin milk, the second and third pressings, is the cooking liquid: it can simmer for as long as you like. Thick milk, the first pressing, is the finish, and it goes in with the heat turned off, because boiling splits it into oil and curds. Coconut oil is the frying fat and, in some houses, a spoonful poured raw over the finished dish.

That thin-then-thick order is the single most useful rule in this reading. Get it wrong and a coconut curry turns oily and grainy; get it right and it stays glossy and smooth.

How a meal is built.

The plate is not a sequence of courses. Rice goes in the middle, and everything else arrives around it in small quantities: a wet curry, a dry vegetable, a pickle, a pappadam, buttermilk. The eater mixes a mouthful at a time with the fingers of the right hand, deciding the ratio themselves, and no two mouthfuls have to be the same. A Kerala meal is therefore less like a plate of food and more like a small set of ingredients handed over with the assembly left to you.

The formal version of this is the sadya, and it is the subject of the next reading. Everything above still applies, only with twenty-odd dishes instead of four and a banana leaf instead of a plate.
