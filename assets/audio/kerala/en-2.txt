Rice in four shapes, coconut in four more.

Rice is central to many Kerala meals, but it does not appear in one fixed form. It can become chewy matta grains, a steamed cylinder of puttu, nests of idiyappam or a lacy fermented appam. Wheat, tapioca and other staples also matter in particular places and communities.

Kerala matta is a reddish, relatively plump rice sold in parboiled form. During parboiling, paddy is soaked, heated and dried before milling. The process changes the starch and can move some water-soluble nutrients inward, while the retained bran colour and chosen variety also shape the grain's firm, chewy texture.

The texture is the point. Matta rice is coarse enough to stand up to a fish curry sharp with kudampuli, and it is what you want under something rich. Basmati would disappear underneath the same sauce.

Several well-known Kerala breakfasts are made from ground rice, and some batters ferment overnight. Soaked rice is ground with grated coconut, then kept at controlled warmth until the batter rises and smells faintly sour. Some traditions use fermenting toddy from a regulated food-grade source; many modern recipes use a small amount of yeast. Once risen, the batter should be refrigerated until cooking.

Appam uses fermented batter swirled in a small round-bottomed pan so the edges run thin. The rim crisps while the middle stays thick and spongy. Idiyappam uses a stiff rice-flour dough pressed into nests and steamed. Puttu layers moistened rice flour and coconut in a cylinder for steaming.

Puttu is the one to try first, because it shows the method plainly. There is no fat in it and no leavening, so it lives or dies on the moisture of the flour, and every Kerala cook has an opinion about that. The test is whether the crumb holds together when you press it and falls apart when you do not.

Coconut changes its function according to form. A useful recipe needs to say whether it means grated flesh, coconut milk, coconut cream or oil.

Grated coconut appears in dishes such as thoran. Thinner coconut milk can serve as cooking liquid, while richer milk or cream is often added later to reduce the chance of separation. Coconut oil is used for frying and, in some households, as an aromatic finish.

The thin-then-rich sequence is useful for many coconut-milk curries, although canned products differ from fresh pressings. Gentle heat and stirring matter as much as timing when the aim is a smooth emulsion.

One common meal pattern places rice with several small accompaniments: perhaps a wet curry, a dry vegetable, pickle, pappadam and buttermilk. Diners may mix each mouthful by hand. The exact combination, placement and sequence depend on household, region and occasion.

A sadya expands this logic into a vegetarian feast on a banana leaf. Its dishes and serving conventions vary, even though recognisable orders and placements are widely taught.
