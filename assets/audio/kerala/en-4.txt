Muziris to Vasco da Gama: the trade that made the kitchen.

In about 60 CE a Greek-speaking merchant wrote a handbook of the Indian Ocean routes, and it names a port on this coast called Muziris. Ships left Egypt in July, caught the south-west monsoon across the open sea, loaded pepper and were home within the year. The Roman empire paid for it in gold, and Pliny the Elder complained in writing about how much of it was leaving.

Muziris and the Roman pepper run.

The site has most likely been found, at Pattanam near Kodungallur, where excavations have turned up Mediterranean amphorae, Roman glass and beads from as far away as the Red Sea. A second-century contract written on papyrus and now in Vienna covers a single cargo from Muziris, and the numbers on it are extraordinary: the shipment is valued at several million sesterces, enough to buy a substantial estate in Italy.

Two things follow from that trade. Kerala has been selling to foreigners for longer than most countries have existed, and the foreigners kept arriving and staying. The kitchen is the record of who stayed.

Arabs, Jews and Syrian Christians.

Arab merchants worked this coast for centuries before Islam and continued afterwards, and the community that grew from them, the Mappilas of the Malabar coast, still cooks a distinct food: Thalassery biryani, pathiri, the meat-and-egg pastry called unnakkaya, and a great deal more wheat than the rest of Kerala eats. By tradition the mosque at Kodungallur was founded in 629 CE, which would make it the oldest in India, and whether or not the date holds, the trading link it stands for certainly does.

Jewish merchants were settled here early too. Copper plates granting them privileges survive from around the tenth century, and the Paradesi synagogue in Mattancherry was built in 1568 and is still standing. The Saint Thomas Christians, who by their own tradition date from the first century and certainly had Syrian liturgical links by the fourth, became the community whose kitchen gave Kerala appam and stew.

The Chinese nets.

The cantilevered nets at Fort Kochi are called cheena vala, and the name credits China. Chinese fleets did come: Zheng He's ships reached Calicut several times in the early fifteenth century, and Calicut was important enough that he is recorded as having died on the way back from it. The nets themselves may have arrived with those voyages or later with Portuguese contact from Macau, and historians disagree. What is not in doubt is that a village on the Kerala coast has been using a Chinese fishing technique for centuries and calling it by its foreign name, which is the whole story of this region in one object.

1498.

On 20 May 1498 Vasco da Gama anchored off Kappad, a few kilometres north of Calicut, having sailed round Africa. Asked what he wanted, his men are said to have answered: Christians and spices. He got an unimpressed reception from the Zamorin of Calicut, who ruled a port that had been trading with the world for a thousand years and was not obviously in need of Portugal, but the route was open and everything followed from it.

What Portugal took was pepper, and it took it violently and for a long time. What Portugal gave, without meaning to, changed Indian cooking more than any conquest did.

What the ships brought back.

Portuguese ships were also running to Brazil, and they carried American plants east. The chilli reached India in the sixteenth century, spread faster than any crop before it, and within a few generations had displaced pepper as the everyday heat of the subcontinent. Cashew, pineapple, papaya, guava and the tomato came the same way. Tapioca arrived later and mattered enormously here: the Travancore court pushed it as a famine crop in the 1880s, and kappa with fish curry is now about as Kerala as food gets, despite being South American.

The Dutch, who took Kochi from the Portuguese in 1663, brought nutmeg and clove cultivation over from the Moluccas, which is why both now grow in Thrissur and Ernakulam. So the "ancient spice coast" sells several spices that have only been here for three hundred years, and its most characteristic heat is five hundred years old at most.

Why any of this is in a cookery course.

Because it explains the plate. The mild white stew and the fierce red fish curry come from different communities on the same coast. The word for the flaky wheat parotta and the word for the rice pathiri come from different arrivals. Beef is eaten here and not in Rajasthan for reasons that are eleven centuries old. And when a European reaches for "curry powder", the thing being flattened is not one cuisine but a couple of thousand years of people landing on this beach and staying.
