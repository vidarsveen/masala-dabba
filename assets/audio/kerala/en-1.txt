Pepper, cardamom and the spice coast.

Walk into a backyard in Wayanad and you will not see a spice field. You will see a jackfruit tree with a rope of dark green leaves wound up its trunk, and hanging off it, strings of small green berries. That is black pepper. It is a vine, it needs something to climb, and for two thousand years it has been grown up the side of whatever tree was already standing there.

The Western Ghats rise along Kerala's eastern edge. Moist south-west monsoon winds meet the mountains and bring heavy rain to the windward slopes; the north-east monsoon adds rain later in the year. Warmth, moisture, shade and elevation create suitable zones for pepper and cardamom, although crops and rainfall vary across the state.

These valuable spices drew merchants into older Indian Ocean networks. Mediterranean, Arab, Chinese and later European trade connected the coast to distant markets, but each relationship had its own period and purpose.

Black, white and green pepper are the same fruit handled three ways. Green berries dried in the sun blacken and shrivel into black peppercorns. For white pepper, ripe berries are soaked and the softened outer layer is removed, leaving the pale seed with a different aroma. Green berries preserved in brine stay soft and mild.

The heat comes mainly from piperine and differs from chilli's capsaicin. Pepper can feel sharp and aromatic without the same lingering burn. Before American chillies spread in India, cooks also used long pepper, ginger, mustard and other pungent ingredients; black pepper was important, but never the only source of heat.

Cardamom grows higher up, between about 600 and 1500 metres, under shade on the Idukki slopes. The plant is a tall herb, but the pods form on stalks near the base rather than in the canopy. Pickers crouch and return to the same plant repeatedly because the pods ripen in batches. Timing matters because overripe pods can split and lose seeds or aromatic quality.

Repeated hand-picking helps explain cardamom's cost. Guatemala and India are both major producers, and Idukki remains central to India's small-cardamom crop. In Kerala the green pods appear in sweets, biryani, stew, tea and selected spice blends.

Pepper and cardamom made the coast famous, but daily cooking draws on a wider pantry. Coconut oil, mustard seed, dried chilli, curry leaf, turmeric, shallot, ginger and garlic recur in many dishes. The choice and sequence vary by region, community and recipe.

Curry leaf is unrelated to curry powder. Fresh or frozen leaves release a citrus-and-toasted-nut aroma in hot fat, either early in cooking or in a finishing tempering. Dried leaves are much quieter, so they are a weak substitute.

Kerala cooks use several souring agents. Tamarind often gives vegetable and pulse dishes rounded acidity. Kudampuli, the dried rind of Garcinia gummi-gutta, is strongly associated with fish curries and adds a sharp, sometimes smoky sourness. Neither acid makes cooked fish safe at room temperature; leftovers need prompt refrigeration.

Kudampuli and tamarind are not interchangeable in flavour, but fish-curry formulas vary within Kerala. The useful distinction is to recognise what each souring ingredient contributes rather than turn one household pattern into a statewide ban.

British-style curry powder compressed many practices into one commercial blend. Kerala cooks may use single spices, fresh pastes and mixtures made for particular dishes, alongside packaged powders in modern kitchens. A fish-curry blend, biryani masala and roasted coconut paste do different jobs and cannot be treated as one universal seasoning.
