Pepper, cardamom and the spice coast.

Walk into a backyard in Wayanad and you will not see a spice field. You will see a jackfruit tree with a rope of dark green leaves wound up its trunk, and hanging off it, strings of small green berries. That is black pepper. It is a vine, it needs something to climb, and for two thousand years it has been grown up the side of whatever tree was already standing there.

Why the spices grow here.

The Western Ghats run down Kerala's eastern edge like a wall, and the wall does the work. When the south-west monsoon comes off the Arabian Sea in June, the mountains stop it and wring it out, so the slopes take two and a half to five metres of rain a year. A second monsoon arrives from the north-east in October. The result is a long wet season, a short dry one and a temperature that barely moves, which is exactly what a pepper vine wants and what almost nowhere else in the world could offer before the twentieth century.

That accident of weather is the reason Romans sailed here, the reason Arabs settled here, and the reason a Portuguese captain turned up in 1498 asking for Christians and spices. Reading four is about what they all left behind. This one is about what they came for.

Pepper: one berry, three spices.

Black, white and green pepper are the same fruit handled three ways. Pick the berries while they are still green and dry them in the sun, and the skin blackens and shrivels into the black peppercorn everyone knows. Let them ripen to red, soak them until the skin rots off, and what is left is the pale inner seed, which is white pepper: cleaner, sharper, and without the fruity top notes the skin carries. Pick them green and preserve them in brine, and they stay soft and mild.

The heat comes from a compound called piperine, and it behaves differently from chilli heat. Piperine hits the front of the mouth and fades quickly, which is why pepper can be used generously in a dish that is not meant to burn. Before chilli reached India in the 1500s, pepper was the only real heat any Indian kitchen had.

Cardamom, and the hills named after it.

Cardamom grows higher up, between about 600 and 1500 metres, in the shade of the evergreen forest on the Idukki slopes. The plant is a tall herb, and the pods do not hang in the canopy where you would look for them: they sit on a stalk running along the ground at the base of the plant. Pickers therefore crouch, and they come back to the same plant every few weeks through the season, because the pods ripen a handful at a time and a pod picked ripe splits open and loses its oil.

All that stooping is why cardamom costs what it does. Guatemala now grows more of it than India, because a Bavarian planter took seed there in 1914 and the crop turned out to like Central America. India still grows the small green pod that Kerala cooks use, and it goes into far more than sweets: a pod or two goes into biryani, into stew, into the tea that arrives at the end.

What is actually in the kitchen.

Here is the part that surprises people. Pepper and cardamom made Kerala famous, but an everyday Kerala kitchen does not lean on them. The daily pantry is cheaper and closer to hand, and it looks like this: coconut oil, black mustard seed, dried red chilli, curry leaf, turmeric, shallot, ginger and garlic. Almost every savoury dish in the region starts or ends with those, heated in oil until the mustard seeds pop.

Curry leaf deserves a note, because it is the ingredient most often got wrong outside India. It is not related to anything in curry powder, it tastes of nothing much when dried, and it is not a herb you stir in at the end like basil. You drop it into hot oil for a few seconds, it crackles, and it gives up a smell somewhere between citrus peel and toasted nuts. Buy it fresh or frozen, and if neither is possible, leave it out rather than use the grey dried version.

Two sours, for two jobs.

The most regional thing in the whole pantry is the souring agent, because Kerala uses two and keeps them apart. Tamarind goes into vegetable and pulse dishes, where it adds a rounded, slightly sweet acidity. Kudampuli, the smoked and dried rind of Garcinia gummi-gutta, goes into fish. It is sharper, faintly smoky, and it does something tamarind cannot: it firms the flesh of the fish and keeps a curry edible for two or three days without a fridge.

A cook in Kerala would no more put tamarind in a fish curry than a cook in Bologna would put cream in a ragù. That distinction, repeated across fourteen regions with different ingredients each time, is what this course is about.

In the pantry: black pepper.

 Aroma: Pine, warm wood, a little citrus in fresh berries.

 Flavour: Sharp, quick heat from piperine, fading fast; the black skin adds fruit.

 What it does: Carries heat without the lingering burn of chilli, and lifts fat and coconut.

 When to add: Cracked at the end for aroma; whole early for a rounder background heat.

 Instead of it: Nothing really substitutes. Use less white pepper, or long pepper for a sweeter heat.

The curry-powder problem.

No Kerala kitchen has a jar of curry powder. The British assembled one in the eighteenth century so that a single tin could stand in for a pantry, and it has been sold back to the rest of the world ever since. What a Kerala cook keeps instead is a small set of single spices and a handful of blends made for one job each: a fish-curry mix heavy on chilli and coriander, a garam masala for biryani, a roasted coconut paste for a Malabar meat dish. The blend follows the dish. It never runs the other way.
