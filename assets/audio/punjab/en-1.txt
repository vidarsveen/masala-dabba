Garam masala and the tandoor.

Stand beside a tandoor at a roadside dhaba and the heat reaches you a metre away. The cook wets one hand, presses a disc of dough onto a cloth pad and drives his arm down into the mouth of the oven. The bread sticks to the clay wall. Forty seconds later he hooks it off with an iron rod, blistered and smoking, and starts the next one.

Warm rather than hot.

Garam masala means warm spices, and the warmth is not chilli. The name comes from an older way of sorting food by what it does to the body, and these are the spices that were held to heat you from the inside. So a Punjabi kitchen grinds cinnamon, cloves, black and green cardamom, mace, nutmeg, bay and black pepper together, in proportions that change from one house to the next.

Two things follow from that. There is no turmeric and no chilli in a real garam masala, which is what separates it from the curry powder sold abroad. And because the aroma sits in volatile oils that boil away, the blend goes in near the end of cooking rather than at the start. A cook who fries garam masala in hot fat for ten minutes has thrown most of it away.

Cumin and coriander do the daily work.

The showy spices are not the ones a Punjabi cook uses most. That honour belongs to cumin and coriander seed, which work as a pair. Cumin goes whole into hot ghee at the very beginning, and it has to be watched, because it darkens in about twenty seconds and turns bitter a few seconds later. That smell is the smell of north Indian cooking starting.

Coriander seed does something less obvious. It is used ground and by the spoonful, and it gives a gravy body as well as flavour, because the ground seed thickens as it cooks. Its own taste is mild and faintly of orange peel, so it can go in at quantities that would ruin a dish made with anything stronger. When a north Indian recipe calls for far more coriander than seems reasonable, that is why.

Ajwain, which smells of thyme.

Ajwain is the strangest thing in the box, and a European nose recognises it at once. The seed carries thymol, the compound that makes thyme smell of thyme, so a pinch in the palm smells like a herb garden that has caught fire. It is fierce, and a heavy hand makes food taste medicinal.

It has two jobs here. It goes into fried things, above all into the gram-flour batter on Amritsari fish, where it cuts the fat and stops the coating tasting flat. And it goes into food that is thought to sit heavily, which is why it turns up in parathas and with pulses. That reputation as a digestive is old, widely believed and hard to test, so treat it as a reason cooks give rather than as a fact.

Kasuri methi, crushed between the palms.

Fenugreek grows as a leaf and as a seed, and Punjab uses both, but the dried leaf is what marks the region. It is called kasuri methi after Kasur, a town that has been on the Pakistani side of the border since 1947, and it smells of maple syrup with a bitter edge underneath.

The method matters more than the amount. You take a pinch, crush it between your palms over the pot so the leaf breaks up and warms as it falls, and you do it in the last minute of cooking. Dal makhani, butter chicken and half the creamy dishes of Delhi depend on it. A cook who leaves it out gets a dish that tastes sweet and slightly empty, and cannot say why.

Amchur, and sourness without a lemon.

Every Indian kitchen needs acid, and each region picks its own. The south uses tamarind and kudampuli, the west uses kokum, and the northern plains reach for unripe mango. Green mangoes are sliced, sun-dried until they are pale and leathery, and ground to a fawn-coloured powder called amchur.

Amchur has one advantage over a lemon, and it is dryness. It sours a dish without loosening it, which makes it right for a stuffed paratha, for a dry potato dish and for chickpeas, where added liquid would be a nuisance. Pomegranate seed, anardana, does a similar job with a darker, fruitier taste.

In the pantry: garam masala.

 Aroma: Sweet bark and clove over something resinous from the black cardamom.

 Flavour: Warm and slightly numbing, with no chilli heat at all.

 What it does: Lifts a finished dish and ties the other spices together rather than adding one flavour.

 When to add: In the last minute, off the boil; long cooking drives the aroma off.

 Instead of it: Nothing sold as curry powder. Grind cinnamon, clove, cardamom and pepper yourself instead.

The oven in the ground.

The tandoor is a clay jar about a metre deep, narrowing at the neck, sunk into the earth or set inside an insulated drum. Charcoal burns at the bottom, the clay soaks up the heat and holds it, and the oven then works in two ways at once. Bread cooks by contact against the wall, and meat cooks on vertical skewers, which is why its fat drips onto the coals and comes back as smoke.

Ovens of this shape are very old. Similar ones have been dug up at Harappan sites in the Indus valley, and the word itself reached Punjabi through Persian from an Akkadian root. What is new is the restaurant tandoor, and reading four is about the eighty years in which that happened.

In the villages the oven was shared. One tandoor served a cluster of houses, the fire was lit once, and women brought their own dough to it and carried the bread home, so the oven was also where the news went round. It was called the sanjha chulha, the shared hearth. Much of what a restaurant now sells as tandoori cooking began as a way of not lighting a fire in every house.
