Wheat, dairy and the Punjabi plate.

A Punjabi meal is built around bread, and the bread is made one piece at a time while people are already eating. Someone stands at the stove and rolls a disc, cooks it on a flat iron pan, then presses it onto the open flame until it balloons. It is smeared with ghee and passed to the table, and the next one goes on. The plate is not really a plate. The bread is.

Five rivers and a flat, deep soil.

The name says what the place is. Punjab comes from the Persian for five waters, and the five are the Jhelum, the Chenab, the Ravi, the Beas and the Sutlej, all running southwest off the Himalaya into the Indus. Partition put most of that water on the Pakistani side, and the Indian state keeps the three eastern rivers. Both halves share the same silt, hundreds of metres deep, and winters cold enough for wheat.

That last point decides the food. Wheat is a winter crop. It is sown in October once the monsoon has gone, it grows through a cool dry season, and it is cut in April. Rice needs standing water and heat, which is why rice belongs to the east and the south, and why the north eats bread.

The harvest that changed the country.

Punjab is a small state, about one and a half per cent of India's land, and it feeds a share of the country out of all proportion to that. The reason has a date. In the mid-1960s India was importing grain to avoid famine, and a breeding programme brought in short-stemmed wheats from Mexico. Short stems matter, because a tall wheat given plenty of fertiliser grows heavy ears and falls over. The new varieties stood up, so they could be fed and watered hard.

Punjab had the canals, the flat fields and the farmers to take that up, and by the end of the decade its harvests had roughly doubled. India stopped importing wheat. Punjab and Haryana still supply much of the grain the government buys for the national food system, and a Punjabi farmer's confidence about food comes from those years.

It came at a price that is still being paid. The same fields now grow summer rice for the rest of the country, which they are not suited to, and the water for it is pumped from underground. The water table in central Punjab falls by something close to half a metre a year. The rice stubble is burned in October to clear the ground for wheat, and a good part of Delhi's winter smog blows in from those fires.

One flour, many breads.

The everyday flour is atta, wholemeal wheat ground between stones so the bran and the germ stay in it. From that one flour comes a whole family of breads, and the differences between them are worth learning, because they are differences of method rather than of ingredient.

Roti, also called phulka, is unleavened, rolled thin and cooked dry on a flat pan, then puffed for a few seconds directly on the flame. Paratha is the same dough folded around ghee and rolled again so it cooks in layers, or stuffed with spiced potato, cauliflower or grated radish. Naan is a different thing altogether: white flour, leavened with yoghurt or yeast, and baked on the wall of a tandoor, which is why no home kitchen in Punjab makes it. Kulcha, the Amritsari speciality, is a stuffed leavened bread baked the same way and finished with an alarming quantity of butter. Bhatura is leavened dough deep-fried until it inflates like a balloon.

Then there is the winter exception. Makki di roti is made from maize flour, which has no gluten, so the dough will not stretch and cannot be rolled. It is patted out between wet palms or pressed flat under a sheet of plastic, and it must be cooked at once, because a maize dough left standing cracks. It is coarser, sweeter and heavier than wheat bread, and it belongs to the greens of the next reading.

Milk, and everything made from it.

No part of India drinks and cooks with as much milk as Punjab does, and most of it comes from buffaloes rather than cows. That matters in the kitchen. Buffalo milk carries about seven per cent fat against a cow's four, so it sets thicker, churns richer and reduces faster. A household with one animal has more milk than it can drink, and the surplus becomes everything else.

Set the milk with a little of yesterday's curd and it becomes dahi, which is eaten at almost every meal. Churn the dahi with water and two things separate at once: the butter rises, and what is left underneath is lassi. That is the real Punjabi lassi, thin, faintly sour and salted, drunk by the tumbler in the fields, and it has little to do with the thick sweet version served in shops. The butter that comes off the churn is makhan, white, unsalted and slightly sour, and it goes on saag and on hot bread while it is still soft.

Simmer the butter until the water boils off and the milk solids brown, and you have ghee, which keeps for months in a hot climate and smells nutty rather than creamy. Curdle hot milk with lemon or whey and press the curds, and you have paneer. Paneer is set with acid rather than rennet, and that is why it behaves as it does. It never melts, so it can be cubed, fried, skewered and simmered in a gravy without disappearing.

How the meal is put together.

A plate at home carries a dal or a bean dish, one dry vegetable, a bread or two, curd, a raw onion and a pickle. Nothing is a course. The bread does the work a fork does, and the eater tears a piece, folds it round a mouthful and keeps going until a new one arrives.

Rice has its own occasions, and they are specific. It turns up as rajma with rice on a Sunday, as kadhi with rice, and as a pulao at a wedding. The everyday answer is bread, and that one fact separates this region from almost every other in the course.
