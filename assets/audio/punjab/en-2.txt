Wheat, dairy and the Punjabi plate.

A Punjabi meal is built around bread, and the bread is made one piece at a time while people are already eating. Someone stands at the stove and rolls a disc, cooks it on a flat iron pan, then presses it onto the open flame until it balloons. It is smeared with ghee and passed to the table, and the next one goes on. The plate is not really a plate. The bread is.

The name says what the historical region is. Punjab comes from the Persian for five waters: the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej, which run from the Himalaya into the Indus system. Partition divided that river country between India and Pakistan. The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty allocated use of the three eastern rivers to India and the three western rivers to Pakistan, although western rivers also cross Indian territory. Both Punjabs have alluvial plains and winters cool enough for wheat.

That season helps explain the food. Wheat is sown in October and November after the monsoon, grows through the cool dry months and is harvested in April. Irrigated summer rice is now a major crop too, but bread remains central to many Punjabi meals.

Punjab is a small state, about one and a half per cent of India's land, and it feeds a share of the country out of all proportion to that. The reason has a date. In the mid-1960s India was importing grain to avoid famine, and a breeding programme brought in short-stemmed wheats from Mexico. Short stems matter, because a tall wheat given plenty of fertiliser grows heavy ears and falls over. The newly introduced varieties stayed upright even when farmers applied more fertiliser and irrigation water.

Punjab had canals, flat fields and farmers able to take that up. Wheat production rose rapidly, and India sharply reduced its dependence on imported grain. Punjab and Haryana remain major suppliers to the government grain system.

It came at a price that is still being paid. The same fields now grow water-intensive summer rice, much of it with groundwater. Punjab Agricultural University calculated an average annual water-table fall of 0.53 metres across the state from 1998 to 2018. Farmers also have little time between the rice and wheat crops, and burning rice residue contributes to severe seasonal air pollution across northern India.

The everyday flour is atta, wholemeal wheat ground between stones so the bran and the germ stay in it. From that one flour comes a whole family of breads, and the differences between them are worth learning, because they are differences of method rather than of ingredient.

For a roti, the cook rolls the unleavened dough thin and cooks it dry on a flat pan. Puffed over the flame, it is also called phulka. Fold the same dough around ghee and roll it again, and a paratha cooks in layers. It can also hold a filling of spiced potato, cauliflower or grated radish.

The dough changes for naan, which is usually made with white flour and leavened. A tandoor gives it a blistered surface, but home cooks also make it on a hot pan. Amritsar's stuffed kulcha comes from the tandoor too, with butter melting over it. For bhatura, leavened dough goes into hot oil and inflates like a balloon.

Then there is the winter exception. Makki di roti is made from maize flour, which has no gluten, so the dough will not stretch and cannot be rolled. It is patted out between wet palms or pressed flat under a sheet of plastic, and it must be cooked at once, because a maize dough left standing cracks. Its coarse, mildly sweet crumb is made for scooping mustard greens.

Milk is unusually abundant in Punjab, and buffaloes have supplied a large share of it. That matters in the kitchen. Buffalo milk commonly contains more fat than cow's milk, so it sets into rich curd and yields more butter. Surplus milk can become several other foods.

Set the milk with a little of yesterday's curd and it becomes dahi. Churn dahi with water and the butter rises; the liquid left below is one traditional form of lassi, thin, faintly sour and often salted. Punjab also has thick sweet lassi, especially associated with shops and special treats. The butter from the churn is makhan, white, unsalted and slightly sour, and it goes on saag and hot bread.

Simmer the butter until the water boils off and the milk solids brown, and you have ghee, which keeps far longer than fresh butter and smells nutty. Curdle hot milk with lemon or whey and press the curds, and you have paneer. Its protein structure keeps it from melting like many rennet-set cheeses, so it can be cubed, fried, skewered and simmered in gravy while holding its shape.

A plate at home carries a dal or a bean dish, one dry vegetable, a bread or two, curd, a raw onion and a pickle. Nothing is a course. The bread does the work a fork does, and the eater tears a piece, folds it round a mouthful and keeps going until a new one arrives.

Rice has its own occasions, and they are specific. It turns up as rajma with rice on a Sunday, as kadhi with rice, and as a pulao at a wedding. The everyday answer is bread, and that one fact separates this region from almost every other in the course.
