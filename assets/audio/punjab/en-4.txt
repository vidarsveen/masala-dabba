1947: the refugee kitchens that invented butter chicken.

In the summer of 1947 a British lawyer named Cyril Radcliffe was given about five weeks to draw a line through Punjab and Bengal. He had never been to India before. The map he produced was published on 17 August, two days after both new countries had already become independent. By then people were moving in both directions without knowing which side they would end up on.

The line through Punjab.

Punjab was the worst place for such a line, because its Muslim, Hindu and Sikh populations were not in separate districts but in the same villages. Lahore, Multan and Rawalpindi went to Pakistan. Amritsar, Jalandhar and Ludhiana stayed in India. Kasur, which gave kasuri methi its name, ended up on the other side of a border fifty kilometres from Amritsar.

Somewhere between ten and twelve million people crossed in Punjab, most of them walking, in the space of a few months. The number who died is genuinely disputed: serious estimates run from a couple of hundred thousand to more than a million, and no count was ever made. What is not disputed is that a whole population arrived at the other end with nothing, and that a very large share of it arrived in Delhi.

Delhi doubles.

Delhi in 1941 held about seven hundred thousand people. By 1951 it held nearly one and a half million, and roughly half a million of the newcomers were refugees from West Punjab. Whole districts were built for them, and their names are still on the map: Lajpat Nagar, Rajinder Nagar, Patel Nagar, Punjabi Bagh.

Before 1947 Delhi's own food was Mughal and Urdu-speaking, and it belonged to the old walled city. It meant nihari braised overnight and eaten at dawn, korma and kebabs, and the trade that Karim's had been running near the Jama Masjid since 1913. That food did not disappear, and you can still eat it where it was always eaten. But it stopped being what Delhi meant to the rest of the country, because thousands of people arrived who needed work, knew how to cook and had nothing else to sell.

Moti Mahal, and a story worth telling carefully.

A restaurant called Moti Mahal had been running in Peshawar. Its people came across in 1947 and started again in Daryaganj, in Delhi, with a tandoor. The claim attached to that restaurant is large. Tandoori chicken as a restaurant dish, butter chicken and the modern dal makhani are all said to have started there. Nehru is said to have eaten there often and to have had visiting heads of state taken along.

The story usually runs like this. Tandoori chicken dries out if it is not sold, so the kitchen simmered the unsold pieces in tomato, butter and cream. The dish that came out was better than the one it rescued. That is a good story and a plausible mechanism, and it should still be handled with care. The families involved have disagreed in public about who did what, and the dispute reached a Delhi court in 2024, which tells you the question is not settled. Say that butter chicken came out of the refugee kitchens of Delhi in the late 1940s, and you are on firm ground. Say exactly whose hand held the spoon, and you are taking a side.

The mechanism is the part that is certainly true, and it is the part a cook should carry away. Butter chicken is a sauce built for meat that has already been cooked over fire. Made with raw chicken poached in the gravy it is a different and much duller dish, which is why the home version is so often disappointing.

The oldest free kitchen still running.

Not everything about food in Punjab came out of 1947, and the oldest institution here is the one that has changed least. Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru, established the langar in the early sixteenth century. A langar is a free kitchen attached to a place of worship, and everyone who eats there sits on the floor in rows and gets the same food. The sitting is the argument. Caste rules about who may eat beside whom cannot survive a floor where a general and a labourer are handed the same dal.

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar the scale is hard to take in. The kitchen runs all day and all night, and it cooks something like a hundred thousand meals a day, more at festivals. Much of the labour is voluntary. People turn up to roll rotis, wash steel plates and stir vats of dal as an act of service. The food is always vegetarian, and the reason is practical rather than doctrinal. A vegetarian meal is one that a visitor of any religion can eat without having to ask a question.

The temple itself was completed in 1604, when the Sikh scripture was first installed in it. The gold that gives it its English name came much later, applied under Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century. The kitchen is older than the gold.

Why the world thinks Indian food tastes like this.

By the 1960s the Delhi tandoori restaurant had become the template for an Indian restaurant everywhere else. Butter chicken, tandoori chicken, naan, dal makhani and a creamy paneer dish are the menu that spread, and all of them come from this one region and mostly from the last eighty years. The restaurants that carried that menu abroad were often run by families from elsewhere, and in Britain much of the trade came from Sylhet in what is now Bangladesh.

So a European who thinks Indian food means a mild orange gravy and a piece of bread from a clay oven is not wrong, exactly. They have met one region of fourteen, in its post-war restaurant form, and mistaken it for a country. That is the reason this course exists, and it is why the thirteen other regions in it look so unlike this one.
