1947: refugee kitchens and butter chicken.

In the summer of 1947 a British lawyer named Cyril Radcliffe was given about five weeks to draw a line through Punjab and Bengal. He had never been to India before. The boundary award was published on 17 August, after the independence ceremonies in Pakistan and India. By then people were already moving in both directions without knowing where the line would fall.

Punjab was the worst place for such a line, because its Muslim, Hindu and Sikh populations were not in separate districts but in the same villages. Lahore, Multan and Rawalpindi went to Pakistan. Amritsar, Jalandhar and Ludhiana stayed in India. Kasur, which gave kasuri methi its name, ended up on the other side of a border fifty kilometres from Amritsar.

Roughly ten to twelve million people were displaced across Punjab during the turbulent months surrounding the drawing and announcement of the new border. Many travelled in long columns on foot, while others went by train, bus or car. Estimates of the dead vary from hundreds of thousands to more than a million. Delhi became one major destination for Hindu and Sikh refugees from West Punjab and the North-West Frontier Province, while many of the city's Muslims left for Pakistan.

The territory of Delhi grew from 917,939 people in the 1941 census to 1,744,072 in 1951. A scholarly census comparison counts 495,391 incoming refugees, alongside the departure of more than 200,000 Muslims. New neighbourhoods included Lajpat Nagar, Rajinder Nagar and Patel Nagar.

The Urdu-speaking walled city already had an influential Mughal-derived cuisine: nihari cooked overnight and eaten at dawn, korma, kebabs, and the food Karim's had sold near Jama Masjid since 1913. That cooking remained. Refugees added another restaurant culture, built around the tandoor and the foods they knew how to sell.

The founders associated with Moti Mahal came from Peshawar and opened their Delhi restaurant in Daryaganj after Partition. Later accounts credit the restaurant with popularising tandoori chicken, butter chicken and modern restaurant-style dal makhani. Descendants and rival restaurant businesses disagree over who may claim the inventions.

The familiar story says that cooked tandoori chicken was simmered in tomato, butter and cream so it would stay moist. The method is plausible, but the named inventor is not settled history. In 2024, competing claims by the Moti Mahal and Daryaganj restaurant businesses became part of a Delhi High Court case over the right to advertise the invention. The filing proves the modern dispute, not which family account is correct.

The cooking method still explains the dish. Chicken is roasted first, so its browned surface and tandoor seasoning enter the tomato-and-butter sauce. Poaching raw chicken directly in the gravy produces a different result.

Not everything about food in Punjab came out of 1947. The Sikh institution of langar developed from the teachings and community established by Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru. A langar is a free communal kitchen at a gurdwara. Diners traditionally sit together in rows on the floor and receive the same food, with chairs or other accommodation available for people who need them. The arrangement makes equality visible across caste, wealth and status.

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar the kitchen runs continuously. It serves more than fifty thousand people on an ordinary day and can pass one hundred thousand during festivals. Much of the work is seva, voluntary service: people roll rotis, wash steel plates and stir vats of dal. The vegetarian menu allows people from different religious and dietary backgrounds to share the meal.

The Sikh scripture was installed at Harmandir Sahib in 1604. The gold that gives the shrine its English name came much later, under Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century. Langar belongs to the institution's older history of equality and service.

By the later twentieth century, Delhi's tandoori restaurant repertoire had become one influential model for Indian restaurants in many countries. Butter chicken, tandoori chicken, naan, dal makhani and creamy paneer dishes travelled together on those menus. The restaurants that carried them abroad were often run by families from other regions; in Britain, many early curry-house owners came from Sylhet in present-day Bangladesh.

A European who thinks Indian food means a mild orange gravy and a piece of bread from a clay oven has met one region of fourteen, in its post-war restaurant form. They have mistaken it for a country.
