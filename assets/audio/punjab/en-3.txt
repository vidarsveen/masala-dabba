Saag, dal makhani and the tandoori table.

In winter, mustard flowers turn the fields between Ludhiana and Amritsar yellow. In the kitchen, mustard leaves go into sarson da saag, cooked until tender and served with maize bread. The greens have a sharpness that survives the long cooking, even under a spoonful of butter.

Mustard greens have a sharp, sometimes bitter flavour. For sarson da saag, cooks often combine them with spinach and bathua, a leafy plant also gathered from cultivated fields. An hour or two of cooking softens the leaves and mellows their bite.

A wooden masher breaks up the cooked greens while leaving some texture. A blender can do the job too, provided it stops before the mixture becomes completely smooth. Maize flour thickens the saag and contributes its own grain flavour. At the end, a tempering of ghee, ginger, garlic, onion and green chilli goes over the greens. White butter melts into them at the table.

Makki di roti brings the same maize to the table as bread. Its coarse texture and mild sweetness sit alongside the sharp greens. Tear off a piece and use it to gather a mouthful of saag and melting butter.

Whole black urad, also called black gram, forms the base of dal makhani. It is a small bean with a dark skin, often sold as a black lentil. Cooks soak it overnight with a smaller quantity of kidney beans, then boil or pressure-cook both until completely tender.

The softened pulses are then simmered with tomato and butter. As some break down, they thicken the cooking liquid; stirring and gentle mashing help bring the pot together. Cream adds richness, but pouring it over firm beans will not produce the same texture.

Whole urad also makes a lighter everyday dal, often called ma di dal. Onion, tomato, ginger and garlic can flavour the pot without the butter and cream that enrich dal makhani. The name does not prescribe a tomato-free recipe: household versions differ.

At a tandoori restaurant, the oven serves several dishes, each prepared differently. For tandoori chicken, the cook removes the skin and makes cuts in the flesh to expose more surface to the seasoning. The pieces sit for several hours in yoghurt with ginger, garlic, chilli, lemon and garam masala. The acid helps tenderise the surface, while the thick yoghurt holds the spices against the meat.

The marinated chicken goes onto skewers and into the hot oven. The aim is a browned, lightly charred surface with cooked, juicy meat inside; timing depends on the size of the pieces and the heat. Kashmiri chilli supplies a strong red colour with relatively mild heat. Some cooks use food colouring too, so brightness alone cannot tell you how the chicken was seasoned.

For seekh kebab, the cook presses seasoned minced mutton around a skewer. Cubes of paneer take a different preparation, coated in marinade for paneer tikka. The cheese holds its shape as it cooks. Meanwhile, tandoori roti, naan and Amritsari kulcha bake against the wall. The same fire cooks meat, cheese and bread, but it does not make their recipes identical.

A plate of rajma-chawal makes rice central to the meal. Tender kidney beans simmer with onion, tomato, ginger and garlic, and a few are mashed to thicken the sauce. Spoon them over plain rice and the grains absorb some of the gravy. In many Punjabi and Delhi households, this is a familiar Sunday lunch.

Breakfast can be just as substantial. With chole bhature, spiced chickpeas arrive beside a large puffed bread, fried until golden. Some versions of the chickpeas are darkened with black tea. Dried amla, or Indian gooseberry, can add both colour and sourness; dried pomegranate seed gives a fruity tang. Tea used for colour does not replace those souring ingredients. The bhatura collapses as it is torn, leaving soft folds to scoop up the chickpeas.

Punjab has no coast, and its rivers and ponds supply freshwater fish. In Amritsari fish, firm pieces are seasoned with ginger, garlic, chilli and ajwain, coated with gram flour and fried until crisp. Lemon and chaat masala finish the hot fish. The coating holds the seasoning close to the flesh while the surface turns golden.

Ajwain gives that crust a thyme-like aroma alongside the chilli. In Norway, saithe, haddock or thick pieces of cod make practical substitutes for the freshwater fish. Choose pieces firm enough to turn in the batter without breaking. The spice remains recognisable with Norwegian fish.
