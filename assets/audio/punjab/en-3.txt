Saag, dal makhani and the tandoori table.

In the middle of January the fields between Ludhiana and Amritsar turn yellow. Mustard is in flower, and the leaves under those flowers are what the whole state wants to eat. Sarson da saag is a seasonal dish in a way that very little Indian food still is, and asking for it in June marks you out as someone who does not know.

Sarson da saag: two hours and a wooden masher.

Mustard greens on their own are too pungent and slightly bitter to be pleasant, so the dish is built to tame them. They are boiled with spinach and, where it can be found, bathua, a wild leaf that grows in the wheat fields. The pot goes on for an hour or two, which breaks the leaves down and drives off most of the bite.

Then two things happen that are easy to skip and should not be. The greens are mashed with a wooden masher rather than blended, so the texture stays slightly rough and the dish does not turn into soup. And a handful of maize flour goes in, which thickens the greens and adds a faint sweetness that answers the mustard. The tempering of ghee, ginger, garlic, onion and green chilli is poured over at the end, and a lump of white butter melts on top at the table.

It is served with makki di roti, and that pairing is not decoration. The bread is made from the same maize that thickens the greens, it is sweet and coarse, and it stands up to a dish that would tear a thin wheat roti apart.

Dal makhani: the dal that cooks all night.

Whole black urad, a small dark lentil with a firm skin, is the base of the most famous dal in India. Soaked overnight and then simmered for hours with a few kidney beans, it breaks down into something thick, sticky and faintly smoky, and a spoon stands in it.

In the villages this was ma di dal, cooked without tomato or cream in a pot left in the cooling tandoor overnight. The restaurant version, dal makhani, adds tomato, a great deal of butter and a swirl of cream, and it is the one that travelled. Both depend on the same thing, which is time. The creaminess is not the cream. It is starch released by a lentil held below a simmer for six hours or more, and no amount of dairy added at the end will fake it.

The tandoori table.

A tandoori restaurant menu looks varied and is in fact one technique repeated. Chicken is skinned, because skin keeps the marinade off the flesh, and slashed to the bone so the seasoning gets in. It then sits for several hours in yoghurt with ginger, garlic, chilli, lemon and garam masala. The yoghurt does two jobs at once. Its acid loosens the muscle, and the thick curd clings to the meat where a thin marinade would run off.

Then it goes onto a skewer into an oven running far hotter than any domestic one. Cooking takes minutes rather than an hour, and the outside chars while the inside is still juicy. The red colour that people associate with the dish should come from Kashmiri chilli, which is mild and stains strongly. A great deal of restaurant tandoori chicken is coloured with dye instead, and once you have seen the pillar-box red version you can tell them apart.

Seekh kebab is minced mutton pressed onto a flat skewer and cooked the same way. Paneer tikka is the vegetarian answer, and it works because acid-set cheese holds together over a fire. Tandoori roti, naan and Amritsari kulcha come off the wall of the same oven. One fire, one marinade and one wall cover the whole menu.

Beans, chickpeas and the two big lunches.

Rajma-chawal is what a Punjabi or Delhi household eats on a Sunday. Kidney beans are simmered until several of them collapse and thicken the gravy, with onion, tomato, ginger and garlic behind them, and the whole thing is eaten over plain rice. It is one of the few occasions in this region when rice is the point rather than the alternative.

Chole bhature is the other, and it is breakfast. The chickpeas are dark brown rather than golden, and the colour has a reason. They are cooked with black tea, or with dried amla, or with pomegranate seed, and each of those darkens and sours them at the same time. Beside them arrives a bhatura the size of a dinner plate, fried so fast that it inflates. Eaten in Delhi at eight in the morning, it is a serious commitment for the rest of the day.

Amritsari fish, and the freshwater habit.

Punjab is landlocked, so its fish comes out of rivers and ponds rather than the sea. A landlocked place seasons fish firmly and fries it, and that is exactly what happens here. Thick pieces are marinated with ginger, garlic, chilli and a good deal of ajwain, coated in a gram-flour batter and deep-fried until the crust is rough and golden. A squeeze of lemon and a pinch of chaat masala go over it as it comes out.

The ajwain is the signature, and it is doing something specific: a thyme-like aromatic cuts through the fat of a deep-fried crust in a way that chilli alone cannot. Anyone cooking this in Norway can use pollack, haddock or a thick piece of cod, and the result is closer to the original than a delicate fillet would be.

What is not here.

Two absences are worth naming. There is almost no coconut in this kitchen and almost no tamarind, and both of those are the defaults further south. Sourness comes from tomato, yoghurt, amchur and pomegranate seed; richness comes from dairy and from the onion cooked down at the start. That is the deep difference between north and south Indian cooking, and it is easier to hear stated plainly than to work out from a menu.
