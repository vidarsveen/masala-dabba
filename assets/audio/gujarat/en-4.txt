Ports, Jains and the Parsi landing at Sanjan.

About sixty kilometres south-west of Ahmedabad, on flat ground near the Sabarmati, there is a rectangular basin lined with fired brick. It is roughly two hundred and twenty metres long. It was built by people of the Indus civilisation, and the settlement around it dates from about 2400 BC. Gujarat has been loading ships for four thousand years, and this is where the argument starts.

Lothal, and what is actually established.

Lothal was excavated between 1955 and 1962, and what the digs found is not in doubt. There was a planned town with a citadel and a lower town, a drainage system, a bead factory, weights matching the Indus standard, and seals of the kind used to mark goods. Beads from Lothal have turned up in Mesopotamia, and that trade is well attested from both ends.

The basin is the disputed part. The excavator read it as a tidal dock connected to the river by a channel, and that reading gave Lothal its fame as the oldest known dockyard in the world. Other archaeologists have argued since the 1960s that it is a water tank, built to store what a dry region could not count on. Neither side has produced a knockout, so the honest sentence is the careful one: Lothal was a working port town, and whether that particular basin held boats or water is still open.

Bharuch, Khambhat, Surat.

The trade never stopped, and the port simply moved as the rivers silted up. Bharuch on the Narmada is the Barygaza of the Greek handbook of the Indian Ocean written in the first century, which describes pilots rowing out to guide ships through the shoals. Khambhat, which Europeans called Cambay, was the great port of medieval Gujarat and gave its name to the whole gulf, until its own harbour silted and the ships went elsewhere.

They went to Surat, which by the seventeenth century was the busiest port in Mughal India and the point of departure for pilgrims to Mecca. The English East India Company opened its first Indian factory there in 1613. Surat was sacked twice by Shivaji, recovered both times, and later lost its position to Bombay, which had a deeper harbour and a British government.

Four thousand years of this produced a merchant culture, and that shows on the plate. A kitchen that expects the household to travel keeps a bread that survives a week. A kitchen that expects guests serves a meal you cannot exhaust. And a community with money and no interest in meat spends its money on technique instead, which is where a dish like khandvi comes from.

The Jains, and the rules that shaped the plate.

Jains are a small minority in Gujarat, barely one person in a hundred, and their influence on the food is out of all proportion to that number. Jainism holds that harm to any living being is to be avoided as far as a person can manage, and the dietary rules follow from that one principle rather than from a list.

No meat, fish or eggs, which is where most people stop reading. But the rule that shaped Gujarati cooking is the next one. Root vegetables are avoided, because pulling up a root kills the whole plant rather than taking a fruit from it, and because the soil around a root is full of small lives that come up with it. So a strict Jain kitchen cooks without potato, onion, garlic, carrot, radish or ginger, and it is precisely that kitchen which needs asafoetida in the way described in reading one. The strictest households will not use hing either, since the resin is tapped from a root, and that tells you how seriously the rule is taken.

A third rule matters as much and is less often mentioned. Jains do not eat after dark, because a lamp draws insects and an evening meal kills them. That pushes the main meal into the middle of the day, which is one reason the full thali is a lunchtime thing in Gujarat and not an evening one.

Very few Gujaratis are Jains. But when a large, respected, commercially powerful community eats this way, restaurants learn to cook without onion and garlic, and neighbours learn the recipes. About half of Gujarat is vegetarian, a far higher share than India as a whole, and the Jain and Vaishnav traditions together are most of the reason.

The Parsis at Sanjan.

Somewhere on this coast, at a place called Sanjan in southern Gujarat, a group of Zoroastrians from Persia came ashore and asked for permission to stay. That much is generally accepted. Almost everything else about the landing comes from one text, the Qissa-i Sanjan, which was written down in 1599 by a priest recording what had been passed on orally for centuries, and the date it implies is argued over. Some read it as the eighth century and some as the tenth.

The story it carries is the one every Parsi child learns. The local ruler sent out a vessel of milk filled to the brim, meaning that his country was full and had no room. The leader of the newcomers stirred a spoonful of sugar into it, handed it back without spilling a drop, and said that his people would live among the ruler's people like that: invisible, taking up no room, and sweetening what was already there. They were given land on conditions, and one of them was that they would speak the local language, which is why Parsis have spoken Gujarati ever since.

It is a story, and it should be read as one. What is solid is the outcome. The sacred fire the refugees are said to have consecrated at Sanjan has been kept burning ever since and now sits at Udvada, a village on the Gujarat coast, and it is the oldest continuously burning fire in the Zoroastrian world. The Parsi kitchen that grew from this community eventually moved to Bombay and became something else entirely, and that is Maharashtra's story rather than Gujarat's.

Rani ki Vav, and why water.

The last thing to understand about Gujarat is how much of it is dry. The most extraordinary building in the state is not a temple or a palace. It is a well.

Rani ki Vav at Patan was cut seven storeys into the ground in the 1060s, and it is a stepwell: a flight of stairs descending to the water table, with the walls of the descent carved into tier after tier of sculpture. It was commissioned by Queen Udayamati in memory of her husband, the Chaulukya king Bhima I. The Saraswati river later flooded it and buried it in silt, which is the reason the carvings survived so well, and the Archaeological Survey dug it out in the 1980s. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014.

The point is not the sculpture. The point is that a kingdom put that much labour and that much art into reaching water, and that the same shortage runs north into Rajasthan and shapes that kitchen too. Gujarat solved it by trading across the sea and eating what keeps. The desert next door solved it differently, and the two answers are next to each other on the map.
