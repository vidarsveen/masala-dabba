Ports, Jains and the Parsi landing at Sanjan.

South-west of Ahmedabad, the Harappan settlement of Lothal contains a large basin lined with fired brick. Excavator S. R. Rao identified it as a tidal dock; other archaeologists proposed a reservoir. Recent landscape research strengthens the dock interpretation, but the structure still rewards careful wording.

Excavations uncovered a planned settlement, drains, bead-working areas, standardised weights, seals and objects showing exchange beyond the region. UNESCO's tentative-list record calls Lothal a Harappan port town on the Bhogava river.

The basin's purpose has nevertheless been debated since the first excavation. A 2024 study reconstructed an ancient connection to the Sabarmati system and added support for a dock, while acknowledging earlier reservoir readings. Calling it a probable dock with a contested history reflects the evidence better than claiming an uncontested world record.

Much later sources reveal other ports. Bharuch on the Narmada is generally identified with Barygaza in the first-century Periplus of the Erythraean Sea. Khambhat, or Cambay, became a major medieval port before silting limited access.

Surat became a leading Mughal port and a departure point for pilgrims to Mecca. A Mughal firman of January 1613 allowed the English East India Company to establish factories at Surat and other places. Surat later lost traffic and political importance to Bombay.

These ports connected Gujarat to long-distance merchant networks. Travel foods such as thepla fit that history, but trade alone did not create the thali or khandvi; region, religion, agriculture and household practice also shaped the cuisine.

Jains are a minority in Gujarat, but Jain food practice is visible in restaurants and homes beyond the community. The principle of ahimsa, or avoiding harm to living beings, informs vegetarianism, fasting and additional restrictions observed at different levels.

Observant Jain diets exclude meat, fish and eggs. Many also avoid roots and tubers because harvesting them destroys the plant and disturbs organisms in the soil. A strict kitchen may omit potato, onion, garlic, carrot, radish and ginger. Some cooks use hing for savoury depth, while others avoid it because the resin comes from a root.

Eating before sunset is another recognised Jain discipline, but contemporary surveys show that observance varies among lay people. These practices helped make food without onion, garlic or root vegetables widely available. Vaishnav traditions also contribute to Gujarat's prominent vegetarian repertoire, alongside many Gujarati communities that eat meat, fish or eggs.

The Qissa-i Sanjan, composed by a Parsi priest in 1599 or 1600, is the principal narrative of Zoroastrian settlement at Sanjan. It drew on oral tradition centuries after the events, and proposed dates for the migration range from the eighth to the tenth century.

The famous bowl of milk filled to the brim, sweetened by the newcomers without overflowing, is a later oral legend. It does not appear in the surviving Qissa. The poem does describe asylum under a local ruler, conditions of settlement and the consecration of the Iranshah fire.

Parsi tradition connects the fire now kept at Udvada with the one consecrated at Sanjan. Historical sources trace its later movement through several towns before it reached Udvada in the eighteenth century. Parsi food developed further in Bombay, where it belongs to Maharashtra's story as well as Gujarat's.

The last thing to understand about Gujarat is how much of it is dry. The most extraordinary building in the state is not a temple or a palace. It is a well.

Rani ki Vav at Patan was built in the eleventh century as a memorial to a king. UNESCO describes it as a water-management system and an inverted temple arranged across seven levels, with more than five hundred principal sculptures. Flooding and silting by the Saraswati helped preserve much of the structure before excavation and conservation.

The stepwell joins engineering, ritual and art around access to water. It is a strong landmark for Gujarat's varied dry landscapes, without reducing the state's food to a single environmental cause.
