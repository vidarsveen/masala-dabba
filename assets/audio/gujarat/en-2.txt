The thali as a system.

A waiter puts a steel tray in front of you, and within a minute seven small bowls have landed on it and something has been spooned into each one. Nothing has been ordered. Nothing will be, and nothing will be billed by the item. The thali is not a plate of assorted food. It is a meal with a structure, and the structure is doing a job.

The tray itself is a thal, and its small bowls are katori. In a restaurant they often sit along the far rim, leaving room for breads, rice, salad, pickle, papad and chutney. The exact number and arrangement vary.

Many diners eat with the fingers of the right hand, tearing bread to gather a vegetable or sauce. Rice can then be mixed with dal or kadhi. The layout keeps wet and dry foods distinct while letting the diner combine them mouthful by mouthful.

A sweet may be served with the meal rather than saved for the end. Gujarat Tourism describes mishthaan as an integral part of the thali, so shrikhand can sit beside savoury dishes from the start.

Restaurant thalis often arrive in stages. Farsan may come early while it is hot, with vegetables, dal, kadhi and fresh breads following. Rice is commonly paired with dal or kadhi, whether it is served later or placed on the tray at the outset.

Unlimited thali restaurants offer repeated servings, while home meals and fixed thalis work differently. A diner can simply tell the server when enough has been served; there is no statewide rule that an empty bowl must be left partly full.

Gujarati kadhi is one familiar thali dish, though no item appears on every tray. Yoghurt or buttermilk is thickened lightly with gram flour, often sweetened with jaggery and sharpened with green chilli and ginger. A hot tempering may include mustard seed, cumin, curry leaf and cloves.

Compared with many Punjabi versions, Gujarati kadhi is usually thinner and less likely to contain pakora. Its sweet-sour liquid suits rice and contrasts with fried farsan and hotter vegetables.

Yoghurt can separate under strong heat. Whisking it with gram flour while cold, heating it gradually and stirring as it comes to a simmer helps keep the kadhi smooth.

The everyday bread is rotli, a thin, soft wheat flatbread. Thepla is sturdier and often includes gram flour, spices and fenugreek leaves, though recipes also use bottle gourd or radish. A little oil helps it stay pliable.

Thepla is strongly associated with journeys because it packs easily and remains pleasant after cooling. Let it cool before closing the container, and refrigerate it for storage beyond the day of cooking, especially when the dough contains yoghurt or fresh vegetables.

Gujarat regulates alcohol under the Gujarat Prohibition Act of 1949, with permits and statutory exceptions. A common non-alcoholic companion to food is chaas, diluted yoghurt or buttermilk seasoned with salt, cumin and sometimes coriander. Tea, sugarcane juice and kokum drinks also have their own settings and seasons.
