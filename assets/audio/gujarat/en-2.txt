The thali as a system.

A waiter puts a steel tray in front of you, and within a minute seven small bowls have landed on it and something has been spooned into each one. Nothing has been ordered. Nothing will be, and nothing will be billed by the item. The thali is not a plate of assorted food. It is a meal with a structure, and the structure is doing a job.

The geometry of the tray.

The tray itself is called a thal, and the small bowls standing on it are katori. They are arranged in an arc along the far rim, which keeps the wet food away from your sleeve and leaves the near half of the tray clear. That near half is where the dry things go: the breads, the rice when it comes, a heap of salad, a pickle, a papad and a smear of chutney at the edge.

There is a reason for every one of those placements. You eat with the fingers of the right hand, tearing a piece of bread and pushing it into a bowl or wrapping it round something dry. The bowls are within reach of one hand and the breads are directly under it. A European reading the layout as decoration will put the tray down and start with the sweet, and the layout was never decoration.

Two more things sit on the tray that Europeans do not expect. Salt is served as a small pile rather than shaken over, so you season each mouthful yourself. And a sweet is usually there from the beginning rather than at the end, which is why a Gujarati meal can open with a spoon of shrikhand and nobody thinks it strange.

The order of service, which is the recipe.

Food arrives in a sequence, and the sequence carries as much information as the dishes. It usually opens with the farsan, the fried or steamed savoury item, brought hot and eaten first while it is still crisp. The vegetables follow: two of them, one dry and one in gravy, and they change with the market rather than with the menu. Dal and kadhi come next in their own katoris, and the breads arrive one at a time, straight off the griddle, because a stack of rotli goes leathery within minutes.

Rice comes near the end, and this is the part visitors get wrong. By the time the rice lands you should still be hungry, because the last act of the meal is rice with dal or kadhi poured over it and eaten together. Anyone who fills up on bread in the first ten minutes has skipped the course the whole thing was building towards.

The other thing visitors get wrong is refusal. A thali is refilled, not portioned, and the server will keep coming round until told to stop. An empty katori reads as a request. So you leave a little in a bowl you have finished with, or you cover it with a hand and say the word for enough, and until you do that the food keeps arriving.

Kadhi, and why it is thin.

Kadhi is on every thali in Gujarat, and it is not the same dish as the kadhi of Punjab or Rajasthan. It is buttermilk thickened with a little gram flour and almost nothing else, sweetened with jaggery, sharpened with green chilli and ginger, and tempered with mustard seed, cumin, curry leaf and a clove or two. It is pale yellow, pourable and served warm.

The thinness is the point. Punjabi kadhi is thick and carries fried gram-flour dumplings, and it is a dish you eat on its own terms. Gujarati kadhi is a liquid for the rice, and it also does the work that a European would give to a glass of wine: it cuts the fried farsan, it cools the chilli, and it resets the mouth between one vegetable and the next.

One technical note carries beyond this dish. Yoghurt splits the moment it meets a hot pan, so the buttermilk is whisked with the gram flour while it is cold, brought up slowly and stirred in one direction until it simmers. Stop stirring too early and you get a pan of curds and whey that nothing will rescue.

The bread that travels.

The everyday bread is rotli, a soft wheat flatbread rolled thinner than a north Indian chapati and cooked in seconds. The one worth knowing about is thepla, which is the same dough enriched with gram flour, yoghurt, turmeric, chilli and a large handful of chopped fenugreek leaves, rolled out and cooked with a little oil on both sides.

Thepla keeps. Cooked properly it is good for three or four days at room temperature and longer in cool weather, and that single property has made it the food a Gujarati family carries on a journey. A tin of thepla goes on the train, on the plane and into the first week of a new country, and Gujaratis have been leaving in numbers for a century and a half. You can learn more about a culture from a bread designed to survive a journey than from a paragraph describing it.

What is drunk.

Gujarat was dry before it was a state. The Bombay Prohibition Act of 1949 applied here while this was part of Bombay State, and the new state of Gujarat kept the ban when it was created in 1960. It is one of the very few Indian states that still bans the sale of alcohol. What arrives with the meal instead is chaas, thin buttermilk salted and seasoned with cumin and sometimes coriander, served cold and in quantity. In summer the kokum drink from the last reading appears beside it, and after the meal comes tea, or in a Surti house a glass of sugarcane juice.
