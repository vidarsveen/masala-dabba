Dhokla, undhiyu and the steamed kitchen.

Gujarat's savoury snacks do not rely on one cooking method. Some are steamed, some baked or griddled, and others fried. A spongy cake, a rolled sheet of gram flour and a sesame-crusted loaf begin with different batters, each controlled by heat, acidity or fermentation.

Traditional dhokla can begin with rice and split chickpea, chana dal, soaked, ground and fermented into a faintly sour batter. The batter is steamed in a shallow tray, cut and finished with a hot tempering of mustard seed and curry leaf.

The yellow gram-flour snack widely sold under the same broad name is often khaman. Many quick versions use fruit salt or bicarbonate for their lift, while recipes and naming overlap in everyday trade. The useful distinction is between grain-and-pulse batters that ferment and gram-flour batters that can be raised quickly.

The tempering is not a garnish in either version. Hot oil with popped mustard seed, sesame and curry leaf is poured over the cut squares, and often a little sugared water goes over as well, which keeps the crumb moist for hours.

Khandvi begins with gram flour whisked into soured yoghurt or buttermilk with turmeric, salt and ginger. The mixture is cooked while stirring until a test smear sets thinly and peels from a cool surface. It must then be spread promptly before it firms.

In that window the paste is poured onto an oiled steel surface or the back of a tray and spread as thin as a sheet of paper with the edge of a palette knife. It sets within a minute or two as it cools. Then it is cut into strips and each strip is rolled up, and the rolls are tempered with mustard seed, sesame, curry leaf and sometimes grated coconut and coriander.

Two things go wrong, and both of them are honest failures rather than bad luck. Undercooked paste never sets and slides off the tray, and there is nothing to do but scrape it back into the pan and keep cooking. Overcooked paste sets before you have spread it and tears into flakes instead of peeling away in a sheet. Every Gujarati cook has a test: a smear on a cold plate should peel off cleanly after a minute, and if it does not, the pan goes back on the flame.

Handvo turns a fermented rice-and-dal batter into a substantial savoury cake. Grated bottle gourd, ginger, chilli and spices enrich the batter. It is topped with sesame and cooked until a crisp crust surrounds a moist, coarse crumb.

The contrast matters: a soft, slightly coarse middle against a dark, nutty crust. Handvo is eaten warm with chutney as a snack or a more substantial meal.

Undhiyu is associated with winter produce in southern Gujarat, especially around Surat. Its name is linked to undhu, meaning upside down. Related village preparations called umbhadiyu cook vegetables in an inverted earthen pot over or under a fire; modern undhiyu is commonly made upright in a covered pot.

A home version layers seasonal vegetables with green masala in a covered pot and stirs as little as possible. Purple yam, sweet potato, potatoes, small aubergines and green banana may join surti papdi, the flat bean closely associated with the dish. Recipes change with produce and household.

Two other things go into the pot. The green masala is coriander, coconut, green chilli, ginger, sesame and a little sugar, ground coarse and stuffed into slits cut in the aubergines and the bananas. And muthiya, small dumplings of gram flour and fenugreek leaf squeezed into shape in the fist, are fried and dropped in near the end, where they soak up the liquid and hold their shape.

Not everything is steamed. Farsan is the whole category of savoury snacks that sit alongside a meal, and a good part of it comes out of oil. The best-known is fafda, strips of gram-flour dough pressed out by hand and deep-fried until they snap, eaten with fried green chillies and a shredded raw papaya relish called sambharo.

Fafda is often paired with jalebi, coils of batter fried and soaked in sugar syrup. The combination is especially associated with Dussehra and is also eaten for breakfast or as a snack. Its salt-sweet contrast echoes the balancing of tastes elsewhere in Gujarati cooking.
