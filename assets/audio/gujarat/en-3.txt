Dhokla, undhiyu and the steamed kitchen.

Almost everywhere else in India the savoury snack comes out of hot oil. Gujarat steams instead, and that one choice runs through the whole of its famous food: a spongy yellow cake, a rolled sheet of gram flour, a savoury loaf with a sesame crust. The results are lighter than fried food, and every one of them is harder to make than it looks.

Dhokla, and the version that is not dhokla.

Proper dhokla starts with rice and split chickpea, chana dal, soaked separately, ground together into a thick batter and left overnight in a warm kitchen. Wild yeasts and the bacteria already on the grain do the rest. By morning the batter has risen and smells faintly sour, and it is poured into a shallow tray and steamed for about a quarter of an hour. It comes out pale, spongy and tangy, and it is cut into squares and tempered with mustard seed, sesame and curry leaf in hot oil.

The yellow, sweeter, softer thing sold everywhere as dhokla is usually khaman, and it is a different dish. Khaman is made from gram flour alone, nothing ferments, and the rise comes from a spoonful of eno or bicarbonate stirred in at the last second. It takes twenty minutes instead of twelve hours, which is why it has largely taken over. It is good, but it tastes of chemistry and sugar rather than of fermentation, and the two should be told apart on a menu.

The tempering is not a garnish in either version. Hot oil with popped mustard seed, sesame and curry leaf is poured over the cut squares, and often a little sugared water goes over as well, which keeps the crumb moist for hours.

Khandvi, the hardest thing here.

Khandvi is the dish that separates a confident Gujarati cook from a hopeful one. Gram flour is whisked into buttermilk with turmeric, salt and ginger until there is not a single lump, and the mixture goes into a pan over a medium flame and is stirred without stopping. For several minutes nothing appears to happen. Then it thickens all at once, and from that moment you have perhaps ninety seconds.

In that window the paste is poured onto an oiled steel surface or the back of a tray and spread as thin as a sheet of paper with the edge of a palette knife. It sets within a minute or two as it cools. Then it is cut into strips and each strip is rolled up, and the rolls are tempered with mustard seed, sesame, curry leaf and sometimes grated coconut and coriander.

Two things go wrong, and both of them are honest failures rather than bad luck. Undercooked paste never sets and slides off the tray, and there is nothing to do but scrape it back into the pan and keep cooking. Overcooked paste sets before you have spread it and tears into flakes instead of peeling away in a sheet. Every Gujarati cook has a test: a smear on a cold plate should peel off cleanly after a minute, and if it does not, the pan goes back on the flame.

Handvo, the savoury cake.

Handvo takes the dhokla batter idea and turns it into a loaf. Rice and a mixture of dals are soaked, coarsely ground and left to ferment, and then grated bottle gourd goes in along with ginger, green chilli, yoghurt and dhana-jeera. The batter is poured into a heavy pan, covered with sesame seed and cooked slowly, either in an oven or on a low flame with a lid, until the bottom and the top have gone dark and crisp.

What you are after is the contrast. The inside stays soft and a little coarse, while the crust turns hard and nutty, and a handvo that is soft all the way through has been hurried. It is eaten warm with green chutney, often at teatime, and it is the one dish in this reading that makes a whole meal on its own.

Undhiyu, and the pot buried upside down.

Undhiyu is a winter dish and a Surat one, and the name comes from the Gujarati word undhu, meaning upside down. In the old method an unglazed clay pot was packed with vegetables and green masala, sealed, turned over and buried mouth-down in a pit, and a fire of dried leaves was lit on top of it. The food cooked for hours in its own steam with no stirring and no added water. Villages around Surat still cook it that way in January, and what comes out of the pit is called umbhadiyu.

A home version is a heavy covered pot on a stove, and the pit is gone. What survives is everything that matters: the winter vegetables, the green paste and the refusal to stir. Purple yam, sweet potato, small potatoes, baby aubergines and green banana go in, and with them the vegetable that makes the dish, surti papdi, a flat green bean grown around Surat that is only good for a few weeks a year.

Two other things go into the pot. The green masala is coriander, coconut, green chilli, ginger, sesame and a little sugar, ground coarse and stuffed into slits cut in the aubergines and the bananas. And muthiya, small dumplings of gram flour and fenugreek leaf squeezed into shape in the fist, are fried and dropped in near the end, where they soak up the liquid and hold their shape.

Farsan: the fried counter after all.

Not everything is steamed. Farsan is the whole category of savoury snacks that sit alongside a meal, and a good part of it comes out of oil. The best-known is fafda, strips of gram-flour dough pressed out by hand and deep-fried until they snap, eaten with fried green chillies and a shredded raw papaya relish called sambharo.

Fafda is eaten with jalebi, which is a coil of fermented batter deep-fried and soaked in sugar syrup, and the combination is a Sunday breakfast across the state and a compulsory one at Dussehra. Salt and sugar in the same mouthful ought to be a mistake, and it is the same instinct that put jaggery in the dal in the first reading. Gujarat has been doing this for a long time and it knows what it is doing.
