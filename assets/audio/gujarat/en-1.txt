Sweet, sour and hot: the dhana-jeera pantry.

A cook in Ahmedabad tastes a pot of dal and reaches for three things one after another: a lump of jaggery, a piece of dried kokum rind and a slit green chilli. To a European that looks like three separate mistakes in the same pot. It is one decision, taken on purpose, and you have to accept it before any of this food makes sense.

Three tastes, one mouthful.

Gujarati food is sweet, sour and hot at once, and the three are held in balance rather than blended into a single flavour. The jaggery is not there to make the dal taste of sugar. It rounds off the acid of the kokum and it takes the sharp edge off the chilli, and in return the sour and the heat stop the sweetness from turning cloying. Remove any one of the three and the other two come at you unguarded.

You notice this most in a plain pot of toor dal. It is thin, yellow and mild for the first second, sour a moment later, and warm at the back of the throat after that. A Punjabi dal arrives as one flavour, and a Gujarati dal arrives as three in sequence.

The balance also moves across the state. Surat and the central districts cook sweeter, and a Surti cook will put jaggery into almost everything. Kathiawad in the west, where the land is drier and poorer, cooks hotter, drier and far less sweet, and a Kathiawadi will tell you so without being asked.

Dhana-jeera, the blend that does the work.

Every Gujarati kitchen keeps a jar of dhana-jeera, and the name is simply the two things in it: dhana is coriander seed and jeera is cumin. They are dry-roasted separately, cooled and ground together, roughly two parts coriander to one part cumin. Some households go as far as three to one, and nobody measures.

The blend does two jobs at once. Coriander is mild and slightly sweet, and ground coriander also thickens, so it gives a thin vegetable dish some body. Cumin is the warm, earthy half, and a little of it goes a long way. Used together they sit underneath a dish rather than on top of it, which is exactly what is wanted when the loud flavours are already sugar, sour fruit and green chilli.

Dhana-jeera goes in where a north Indian cook would reach for garam masala, and the difference matters. Garam masala is built on cinnamon, clove and cardamom, and it perfumes a dish. Dhana-jeera has none of those, so it seasons without perfuming. It also goes in early, with the vegetables, rather than being scattered over at the end.

Hing, and the kitchen with no onions.

Asafoetida is the dried resin of a giant fennel relative, tapped from the taproot of Ferula species growing in Iran and Afghanistan. Raw it smells sulphurous and slightly rotten. Dropped into hot fat for a few seconds it changes completely, and what comes off the pan is savoury and warm and close to frying onion.

That resemblance to onion is the whole reason it matters here. Many Jain households eat no onion and no garlic at all, and so do a great many Vaishnav ones, which means a large part of Gujarat cooks without the savoury base that most of the world starts a pot with. Hing fills the gap. A pinch of it in the hot oil gives a dal the depth that a fried onion would have given, and a Gujarati cook who does use onion will still add the hing.

It is also credited with settling a heavy pot of beans, which is one reason it goes into every pulse dish in the state. Reading four explains where the rule against onions comes from, and the answer is older than most European countries.

Jaggery, kokum and the sour side.

Jaggery, gud in Hindi and gol in Gujarati, is cane juice boiled down and set in blocks without being refined. It keeps the molasses and the minerals that white sugar has had taken out, so it tastes of caramel and iron rather than of sweetness alone. A piece the size of a walnut goes into a pot of dal for four, and smaller amounts go into kadhi, into vegetables, and sometimes into the dough for rotli.

Kokum is the other half of the argument. It is the rind of Garcinia indica, dried until it is purple-black and leathery, and its acidity is clean and fruity with no sweetness behind it. Gujarat uses it in two ways. Two or three pieces go into the dal and stay there while it simmers, and in summer the same rind is steeped in water with sugar, salt and roasted cumin and drunk cold. Goa, further down the same coast, takes the identical fruit and mixes it with coconut milk instead, and that drink belongs to the Goan module rather than this one.

Mustard, sesame and the moment the pan goes on.

A Gujarati dish usually starts in the same way, and the Gujarati word for it is vaghar. Oil goes into a small pan, then black mustard seed, and the cook waits until the seeds jump and crack. Curry leaves go in next and spit, then a pinch of hing, and only then the ground spices, with the pan pulled off the flame so the dhana-jeera does not burn. The whole business takes about forty seconds and it decides how the dish will smell.

White sesame comes in later and elsewhere. It crusts the top of a handvo, it is ground into the green masala that holds undhiyu together, and it turns up over khandvi with the mustard seed. In January it takes over the state completely: on the fourteenth, at Uttarayan, Ahmedabad flies kites all day and eats sesame and jaggery pressed into brittle, because both are considered winter food.

In the pantry: dhana-jeera.

 Aroma: Warm citrus peel and wood from the coriander, with the earthier cumin behind it.

 Flavour: Mild and slightly sweet, never sharp; it is a base note rather than a top one.

 What it does: Seasons and thickens at the same time, and gives a watery vegetable dish some body.

 When to add: Early, with the vegetables, and with the pan off the flame for a few seconds so it does not scorch.

 Instead of it: Ground coriander and cumin from two jars, two to one. Garam masala is not a substitute.
