Sweet, sour and hot: the dhana-jeera pantry.

A cook in Ahmedabad tastes a pot of dal and reaches for three things one after another: a lump of jaggery, a piece of dried kokum rind and a slit green chilli. To a European that looks like three separate mistakes in the same pot. It is one decision, taken on purpose, and you have to accept it before any of this food makes sense.

Many Gujarati dishes balance sweetness, acidity and heat. The jaggery is not there simply to make dal taste sugary. It rounds the acid of kokum or another souring ingredient and softens the chilli; the acid and heat keep the sweetness from becoming cloying.

You notice the sequence in some versions of toor dal: mild and earthy at first, sour a moment later, then warm from chilli. The balance is adjusted by household and region rather than fixed across the state.

Surat and parts of central Gujarat are known for sweeter seasoning. Food from Kathiawad in the west is often hotter, drier and less sweet, though neither description covers every cook or dish.

A common Gujarati pantry blend is dhana-jeera. The name identifies its two ingredients: dhana is coriander seed and jeera is cumin. They may be roasted separately, cooled and ground together. Two parts coriander to one part cumin is a useful starting point, but household mixtures vary.

The blend does two jobs at once. Coriander is mild and slightly sweet, and ground coriander also thickens, so it gives a thin vegetable dish some body. Cumin is the warm, earthy half, and a little of it goes a long way. Used together they sit underneath a dish rather than on top of it, which is exactly what is wanted when the loud flavours are already sugar, sour fruit and green chilli.

In some vegetable dishes, dhana-jeera fills the role that a cook elsewhere might give garam masala. The blends are not interchangeable. Dhana-jeera has no fixed set of aromatic spices such as cinnamon, clove or cardamom, and it is often added during cooking rather than at the end.

Asafoetida is the dried resin of a giant fennel relative, tapped from the taproot of Ferula species growing in Iran and Afghanistan. Raw it smells sulphurous and slightly rotten. Dropped into hot fat for a few seconds it changes completely, and what comes off the pan is savoury and warm and close to frying onion.

That resemblance to onion helps explain its place here. Observant Jain cooks and many Vaishnav cooks avoid onion and garlic, though practice varies. Hing gives dal and vegetables savoury depth without either allium, and cooks who use onion may add it too.

Hing is also traditionally valued in pulse dishes, where a very small amount seasons the whole pot.

Jaggery, gud in Hindi and gol in Gujarati, is concentrated cane juice set in blocks without being refined into white crystals. Its molasses-like compounds give it caramel and earthy notes as well as sweetness. The amount used in dal, kadhi or vegetables depends on the cook and the souring ingredient.

Kokum supplies one sour note used in parts of Gujarat. It is the rind of Garcinia indica, dried until purple-black and leathery. Pieces can simmer in dal, while a sweetened kokum drink may be served cold in summer. Farther down the coast, Goan cooks also combine kokum with coconut milk.

The Gujarati word for a hot-fat tempering is vaghar. One common sequence starts with black mustard seed, followed by curry leaves and a pinch of hing. Ground spices need gentler heat so they do not scorch. Ingredients and order change with the dish, but the seasoned fat carries their aroma through it.

White sesame crusts handvo, enriches some undhiyu masalas and is scattered over khandvi. During the January kite festival Uttarayan, sweets made with sesame and jaggery are part of the seasonal food.
